## Introduction

The Eisenhower Briefing Document is the central text in the original MJ-12 document controversy. Dated 18 November 1952, it presents itself as a highly classified briefing prepared for President-elect Dwight D. The result is an unusually instructive authentication case: parts of the text fit real 1952 circumstances, but the most important tests — provenance, independent archival corroboration and a verifiable physical original — remain unsatisfied.

## What the briefing claims

The document describes itself as an introductory briefing prepared on 18 November 1952 by Rear Admiral Roscoe H. [Hillenkoetter]({{ 'hillenkoetter/' | relative_url }}), the former Director of Central Intelligence. It says “Operation Majestic-12” was a top-secret research and intelligence operation answerable directly to the President and controlled by a twelve-member group created by Truman on 24 September 1947. The named members include senior figures from the military, intelligence establishment and scientific community, among them Hillenkoetter, Vannevar Bush and General Nathan [Twining]({{ 'twining/' | relative_url }}).<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/MJ-12_Document" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: en.wikisource.org">[Wikisource]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">en.wikisource.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">MJ-12 Document</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">MJ-12 Document - Wikisource, the free online libraryNovember 18, 1952...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 18, 1952</span></span></span>

Its narrative links the purported organisation to several events that later became staples of MJ-12 lore. It describes the recovery of an unusual craft near Roswell, New Mexico, in July 1947 and says four small humanoid bodies were recovered nearby. It also refers to another alleged recovery in December 1950 and portrays the government’s public UFO investigations as partly useful for collecting information while concealing the deeper operation. The document ends by recommending that the programme continue into the Eisenhower administration under strict secrecy.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/MJ-12_Document" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: en.wikisource.org">[Wikisource]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">en.wikisource.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">MJ-12 Document</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">MJ-12 Document - Wikisource, the free online libraryNovember 18, 1952...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 18, 1952</span></span></span>

The briefing packet is commonly treated as seven pages of main text accompanied by a one-page Truman-to-[Forrestal]({{ 'forrestal/' | relative_url }}) memorandum dated 24 September 1947. That attachment matters enormously to the authenticity question because the briefing explicitly relies on it as documentary authority for MJ-12’s creation. ## How the document surfaced

Nothing resembling a normal declassification trail accompanied the Eisenhower briefing. According to the account given by the researchers who received it, television producer Jaime Shandera was sent an anonymous package in December 1984 containing undeveloped 35 mm black-and-white film. The envelope bore an Albuquerque, New Mexico postmark but no return address. When developed, the film contained images of the Eisenhower briefing and its Truman attachment.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.theufochronicles.com/2014/06/the-myth-of-mj-12-appendix-pt-1.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theufochronicles.com">[theufochronicles.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theufochronicles.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">the myth of mj 12 appendix pt 1</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Myth of MJ-12: Appendix A –Pt 1June 29, 2014...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 29, 2014</span></span></span>

That method of disclosure creates the document’s biggest evidential problem. Researchers possessed the film sent to Shandera, but not the government papers supposedly photographed onto it. Consequently, the alleged 1952 original could not be tested directly for paper composition, ink, typewriter impressions, ageing, original classification stamps or other physical features that can sometimes establish when and how a document was produced.

The missing provenance also prevents investigators from reconstructing a custody history. There is no verified answer to basic archival questions: who obtained the supposed original, from which government file it came, when it left official custody, who photographed it, or why the sender chose Shandera. Even an immaculate-looking photograph can be created from a recently manufactured paper. Without the source document or an independently documented archival copy, visual plausibility cannot fill that gap.

This sharply distinguishes the briefing from an ordinary leaked classified record. Genuine leaked documents may initially arrive anonymously, but authenticity can later be strengthened when matching copies, file numbers, references, drafts, distribution records or related correspondence are found in government collections. Extensive official searches have not produced such a documentary trail for the Eisenhower briefing. The National Archives says searches of Air Force, Joint Chiefs of Staff, National Security Council, Truman Library and Eisenhower Library holdings found no copies of the briefing and no corresponding MJ-12 documentation apart from the separately disputed Cutler-Twining memorandum.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/research/military/air-force/ufos" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives Project BLUE BOOK</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesProject BLUE BOOK - Unidentified Flying Objects &#124; National ArchivesJune 25, 2024...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 25, 2024</span></span></span>

## The strongest case for authenticity

Supporters of the Eisenhower document have not relied solely on its appearance. [Stanton Friedman]({{ 'friedman-s-case/' | relative_url }}), its most prominent defender, argued that critics sometimes treated ordinary variations in 1940s and 1950s government paperwork as decisive evidence of forgery. He noted, for example, that real classified documents used multiple date formats and argued that expressions criticised as linguistic anachronisms, including “media” and “impacted”, existed before 1952.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.theblackvault.com/casefiles/update-on-operation-majestic-12-documents/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theblackvault.com">[The Black Vault]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theblackvault.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Black Vault Update on Operation Majestic 12 Documents</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Black VaultUpdate on Operation Majestic 12 Documents - The Black Vault Case Files...</span></span></span>

That is an important caution. Document authentication is strongest when anomalies can be shown to be impossible or highly improbable for the claimed period, not merely unusual. Government bureaucracy did not always apply formats consistently, especially across different agencies and offices. A peculiar date style or unconventional marking therefore deserves weight only in combination with other evidence.

Supporters also point to the document’s choice of 18 November 1952. Contemporary records confirm that this was a genuine and unusually important transition day. Truman met Eisenhower at the [White House]({{ 'white-house/' | relative_url }}) to discuss foreign-policy problems, and a State Department memorandum records Eisenhower saying that immediately afterwards he was going with Secretary of Defense Robert Lovett to meet the Joint Chiefs of Staff for a military briefing.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1952-54v01p1/d8" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: history.state.gov">[Office of the Historian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">history.state.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Office of the Historian Historical Documents</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Office of the HistorianHistorical Documents - Office of the Historian...</span></span></span>

That historical match is real. A hypothetical MJ-12 briefing on that date would therefore not conflict with Eisenhower’s known whereabouts in the simple sense that he was somewhere else.

But its evidential value is limited. The meeting and military briefing were part of the public and documentary record of the presidential transition. Truman publicly announced his meeting with Eisenhower on 18 November 1952, and government records preserve the surrounding arrangements. A later document-maker with historical knowledge could therefore have selected a date on which a secret briefing was plausible.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.trumanlibrary.gov/library/public-papers/331/joint-statement-following-meeting-president-elect" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: trumanlibrary.gov">[Truman Library]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">trumanlibrary.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on trumanlibrary.gov.</span></span></span>

The useful distinction is between **consistency** and **corroboration**. Eisenhower’s real briefing schedule makes the MJ-12 document consistent with one known event. It does not independently show that an MJ-12 presentation occurred during that schedule.

## Why provenance weighs so heavily against it

The most damaging evidence is not a disputed typeface, vocabulary choice or punctuation habit. It is the absence of the document from the archival systems in which corroborating traces would normally be sought.

The National Archives reports that it searched records of the US Air Force and Joint Chiefs of Staff for the “Briefing Document: Operation Majestic 12”. The Truman and Eisenhower presidential libraries also searched their holdings. National Security Council indexes covering both administrations were checked under terms including “MJ-12”, “Majestic”, “UFO”, “flying saucers” and “extraterrestrial biological entities”. Those searches produced no Eisenhower briefing and no broader MJ-12 file trail.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/research/military/air-force/ufos" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives Project BLUE BOOK</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesProject BLUE BOOK - Unidentified Flying Objects &#124; National ArchivesJune 25, 2024...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 25, 2024</span></span></span>

A 1995 General Accounting Office review reached the same problem from another direction. GAO asked the Information Security Oversight Office, Air Force and National Archives about the circulated MJ-12 material. Those organisations reported that their knowledge of it came from documents supplied by private individuals, not from records in their own custody. GAO concluded that there was no evidence the MJ-12 material constituted documents originally created within the executive branch and found nothing contradicting the agencies’ conclusions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gao.gov/products/154832" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gao.gov">[Government Accountability Office]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gao.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Government Accountability Office[Comments on Majestic 12 Material] &#124; U.S. GAO</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Government Accountability Office[Comments on Majestic 12 Material] &#124; U.S. GAO</span></span></span>

The Truman and Eisenhower libraries likewise told GAO that searches including classified intelligence and National Security Council material had found nothing fitting the MJ-12 description or even references to that designation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gao.gov/products/154832" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gao.gov">[Government Accountability Office]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gao.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Government Accountability Office[Comments on Majestic 12 Material] &#124; U.S. GAO</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Government Accountability Office[Comments on Majestic 12 Material] &#124; U.S. GAO</span></span></span>

None of this proves, as a matter of logic, that a sufficiently compartmented programme could not have existed. Highly classified records can be withheld, destroyed, misfiled or retained outside collections available to researchers. But that possibility cannot itself authenticate a particular document. In documentary analysis, missing corroboration becomes especially important when the only known source is an anonymous photographic copy.

## The FBI and Air Force verdict

The federal response became more direct after copies of the MJ-12 material reached government investigators. The FBI records that in 1988 two of its offices received similar versions of an “Operation Majestic-12” document describing a secret committee and a recovered extraterrestrial craft. According to the FBI’s current Vault description, an Air Force investigation determined the document to be fake.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://vault.fbi.gov/search?SearchableText=Majestic" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: vault.fbi.gov">[FBI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">vault.fbi.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">— Federal Bureau of Investigation</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">FBI — Federal Bureau of Investigation...</span></span></span>

The FBI file has therefore become one of the most frequently cited pieces of evidence against the briefing. It demonstrates that the document was not simply ignored by the government after publication: officials made inquiries about whether it corresponded to an authentic classified programme and received a negative answer.

There is nevertheless a limit to what this establishes. The surviving public record does not amount to a full modern forensic examination of the [anonymous film]({{ 'anonymous-film/' | relative_url }}), paper originals and production history, because the supposed originals were unavailable. The official conclusion is consequently much stronger evidence about the absence of recognised government provenance than it is a laboratory demonstration identifying who manufactured the pages.

The GAO investigation later reinforced that institutional assessment. Its significance lies less in a dramatic declaration of forgery than in the convergence of several agencies: none could locate an executive-branch origin for the MJ-12 material.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gao.gov/products/154832" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gao.gov">[Government Accountability Office]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gao.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Government Accountability Office[Comments on Majestic 12 Material] &#124; U.S. GAO</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Government Accountability Office[Comments on Majestic 12 Material] &#124; U.S. GAO</span></span></span>

## The Truman attachment creates a second authenticity problem

The briefing’s evidential position is further complicated by its attached Truman-to-Forrestal memorandum. The memorandum purports to show Truman authorising the MJ-12 undertaking on 24 September 1947. Because the briefing cites that action as the origin of Majestic-12, the attachment functions as a foundational record rather than an incidental extra page.

Sceptical investigator Philip J. Klass argued that Truman’s signature on the purported MJ-12 memorandum was reproduced from an authentic Truman letter to Vannevar Bush dated 1 October 1947. His comparison focused not merely on the general shape of Truman’s signature but on matching incidental marks, which he regarded as evidence that a photocopied signature had been transferred onto the disputed memorandum.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://inquiry201.rssing.com/chan-16157765/all_p1.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: inquiry201.rssing.com">[Inquiry 201]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">inquiry201.rssing.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Inquiry 201Skeptical Inquirer</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Inquiry 201Skeptical Inquirer</span></span></span>

That claim has been contested by MJ-12 defenders, and because researchers lack the alleged original memorandum, questions about paste-up, copying technique and physical alteration cannot now be settled by examining the source sheet itself. Friedman, for example, argued that other characteristics of the memorandum and its historical setting favoured authenticity and rejected many of the objections raised against the original documents.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.theblackvault.com/casefiles/update-on-operation-majestic-12-documents/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theblackvault.com">[The Black Vault]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theblackvault.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Black Vault Update on Operation Majestic 12 Documents</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Black VaultUpdate on Operation Majestic 12 Documents - The Black Vault Case Files...</span></span></span>

Even so, the dependency between the two documents is important. If the Truman memorandum was assembled from an existing signature, the Eisenhower briefing cannot remain untouched by that result: it expressly treats the 1947 memorandum as part of its own historical record. A forged attachment embedded in the packet would be much harder to explain as an innocent later addition.

## Formatting anomalies are suggestive, not decisive

Critics have identified numerous textual and typographical peculiarities in the Eisenhower briefing. One frequently discussed example is its date style, such as “18 November, 1952” — a hybrid construction combining day-month-year order with a comma. Philip Klass argued that the same unusual pattern appeared in correspondence written by William Moore, one of the researchers involved in releasing the documents.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cdn.centerforinquiry.org/wp-content/uploads/sites/29/docs/SUN/SUN49.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cdn.centerforinquiry.org">[cdn.centerforinquiry.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cdn.centerforinquiry.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">SU N #49</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">S KEPTICS UFO NEWSLETTER By Philip J. KlNovember 14, 2024...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 14, 2024</span></span></span>

Other sceptical arguments have concerned classification markings, page-control conventions, the wording of security warnings and Hillenkoetter’s name format. These details can be relevant because authentic classified documents usually belong to administrative systems whose conventions can be compared with surviving contemporaneous records.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://lurch2.blogspot.com/2013/09/majestic-12.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: lurch2.blogspot.com">[Lurch 2]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">lurch2.blogspot.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">majestic 12</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">majestic 12</span></span></span>

But this category of argument requires caution. Friedman produced examples showing that authentic government records did not always use a single date convention and disputed claims that particular words or formats were chronologically impossible.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.theblackvault.com/casefiles/update-on-operation-majestic-12-documents/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theblackvault.com">[The Black Vault]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theblackvault.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Black Vault Update on Operation Majestic 12 Documents</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Black VaultUpdate on Operation Majestic 12 Documents - The Black Vault Case Files...</span></span></span>

For that reason, formatting questions are best treated cumulatively rather than as single-item “proofs”. An unusual comma does not authenticate or destroy the document. The stronger case emerges when stylistic anomalies are considered alongside the far more consequential absence of originals, custody records and corroborating files.

## The 18 November briefing is a real clue — but not the missing link

Perhaps the most interesting feature of the Eisenhower document is that its author, whoever that was, chose an excellent date.

Government records show Eisenhower was receiving precisely the kind of national-security transition briefing that day that could, in principle, have concealed an additional compartmented presentation. After discussions with Truman and senior cabinet officials, he was due to meet the Joint Chiefs.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1952-54v01p1/d8" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: history.state.gov">[Office of the Historian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">history.state.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Office of the Historian Historical Documents</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Office of the HistorianHistorical Documents - Office of the Historian...</span></span></span>

This is stronger than a blatant historical error would have been. Had Eisenhower been demonstrably elsewhere on 18 November, the briefing would face a severe contradiction. Instead, its chronology passes a basic plausibility test.

Yet authentication requires more than avoiding contradiction. A genuine supporting trail might include a Hillenkoetter diary entry, an MJ-12 distribution record, an executive order establishing the committee, a copy preserved among transition papers, an identifiable Top Secret control record, contemporaneous correspondence mentioning the briefing, or a later document independently referring back to it. Decades of archival searching have not yielded such corroboration for the Eisenhower paper.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/research/military/air-force/ufos" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives Project BLUE BOOK</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesProject BLUE BOOK - Unidentified Flying Objects &#124; National ArchivesJune 25, 2024...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 25, 2024</span></span></span>

The date is therefore best understood as an intriguing piece of internal plausibility, not independent confirmation.

## Where the authenticity case stands

The Eisenhower Briefing Document cannot be authenticated by the normal evidential route because the supposed government original has never been produced. What survives publicly derives from anonymously mailed film, so investigators cannot establish the original paper’s provenance or perform decisive physical examination.

Arguments in its favour are not entirely trivial. Its authorship claims use real officials, its broad bureaucratic setting is historically plausible, and 18 November 1952 genuinely was a major Eisenhower transition-briefing day. Defenders such as Stanton Friedman also showed that some supposedly fatal stylistic objections were less clear-cut than critics claimed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1952-54v01p1/d8" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: history.state.gov">[Office of the Historian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">history.state.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Office of the Historian Historical Documents</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Office of the HistorianHistorical Documents - Office of the Historian...</span></span></span>

Those points, however, are outweighed by the evidential deficits. No authenticated executive-branch copy has surfaced; official searches found no MJ-12 administrative trail corresponding to the briefing; the attached Truman memorandum has itself faced a serious copied-signature challenge; and both the Air Force assessment relayed by the FBI and the later GAO review rejected the idea that the circulated material had a demonstrated government origin.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://vault.fbi.gov/search?SearchableText=Majestic" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: vault.fbi.gov">[FBI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">vault.fbi.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">— Federal Bureau of Investigation</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">FBI — Federal Bureau of Investigation...</span></span></span>

The most defensible conclusion is therefore not that every disputed typographical objection proves forgery, nor that absolute disproof is possible from an anonymous photographic copy. It is that the Eisenhower briefing lacks the provenance and independent corroboration required to treat it as an authentic 1952 US government record. The historical coincidences that keep the controversy alive establish plausibility at points; they do not bridge that fundamental evidential gap.

      .filter(function (marketId) { return !!markets[marketId]; });
  }
  function applyMarket(section, marketId, persist) {
    var available = availableMarkets(section);
    if (available.indexOf(marketId)< 0) marketId = available[0] || defaultMarket;
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-localized-link]')).forEach(function (link) {
      var query = link.getAttribute('data-ebay-query') || '';
      var reference = link.getAttribute('data-ebay-reference') || '';
      link.setAttribute('href', buildUrl(query, reference, marketId));
    });
    var select = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-select]');
    if (select && available.indexOf(marketId) >= 0) select.value = marketId;
    var selectedText = marketId;
    if (select && select.selectedIndex >= 0) selectedText = select.options[select.selectedIndex].text;
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-selected-market-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = selectedText;
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-selected-market-flag], [data-ebay-trigger-market-flag]')).forEach(function (flag) {
      flag.className = flagClass(marketId);
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-trigger-market-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = selectedText;
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-domain-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = domainLabel(marketId);
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-option]')).forEach(function (button) {
      button.setAttribute('aria-selected', button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-option') === marketId ? 'true' : 'false');
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      var active = button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button') === marketId;
      button.setAttribute('aria-pressed', active ? 'true' : 'false');
    });
    section.setAttribute('data-ebay-selected-market', marketId);
    if (persist) {
      try { if (window.localStorage) localStorage.setItem('phoenix-ebay-market', marketId); } catch (err) {}
    }
    closeMenu(section);
  }
  sections.forEach(function (section) {
    if (section.getAttribute('data-ebay-localized-init') === '1') return;
    section.setAttribute('data-ebay-localized-init', '1');
    var available = availableMarkets(section);
    var marketId = defaultMarket;
    if (window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation && window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation.inferMarket) {
      marketId = window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation.inferMarket(available, {
        storageKey: 'phoenix-ebay-market',
        defaultMarket: defaultMarket
      });
    } else if (available.indexOf(defaultMarket)< 0) {
      marketId = available[0] || defaultMarket;
    }
    var select = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-select]');
    if (select) {
      select.addEventListener('change', function () {
        applyMarket(section, select.value, true);
      });
    }
    var trigger = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-trigger]');
    var menu = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-menu]');
    if (trigger && menu) {
      trigger.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
        event.stopPropagation();
        var expanded = trigger.getAttribute('aria-expanded') === 'true';
        menu.hidden = expanded;
        trigger.setAttribute('aria-expanded', expanded ? 'false' : 'true');
        var picker = trigger.closest('.fr-ebay-market-picker');
        if (picker) picker.classList.toggle('fr-ebay-market-picker--open', !expanded);
      });
      Array.prototype.slice.call(menu.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-option]')).forEach(function (button) {
        button.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
          event.stopPropagation();
          applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-option'), true);
        });
      });
      document.addEventListener('click', function () { closeMenu(section); });
    }
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      button.addEventListener('click', function () {
        applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button'), true);
      });
    });
    applyMarket(section, marketId, false);
  });
