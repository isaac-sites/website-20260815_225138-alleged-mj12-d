## Introduction

Stanton Friedman’s position on [the alleged MJ-12 documents]({{ 'the-alleged-mj-12-documents/' | relative_url }}) was narrower than the blanket endorsement sometimes attributed to him. He argued most strongly for three papers: the **18 November 1952 Eisenhower Briefing Document (EBD)**, the **24 September 1947 Truman-[Forrestal]({{ 'forrestal/' | relative_url }}) memorandum (TFM)** embedded in that briefing, and the **14 July 1954 Cutler-Twining memorandum (CTM)** found in National Archives files. At the same time, he concluded that many later purported MJ-12 papers were fabricated, altered or otherwise unreliable.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.theblackvault.com/casefiles/update-on-operation-majestic-12-documents/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theblackvault.com">[The Black Vault]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theblackvault.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Black Vault Update on Operation Majestic 12 Documents</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Black VaultUpdate on Operation Majestic 12 Documents - The Black Vault Case FilesJanuary 17, 2024...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 17, 2024</span></span></span>

## Which documents Friedman defended

By 2004 Friedman described the EBD, TFM and CTM as the “[original documents]({{ 'original-document/' | relative_url }})” and said that, “on balance”, he regarded all three as almost certainly genuine. He treated them as a linked evidential set rather than three interchangeable items. The EBD purported to be a briefing prepared for President-elect Dwight Eisenhower; the TFM purported to be Truman’s authorisation to James Forrestal to proceed with “Operation Majestic Twelve”; and the CTM referred to an “NSC/MJ-12 Special Studies Project” briefing in July 1954.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.theblackvault.com/casefiles/update-on-operation-majestic-12-documents/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theblackvault.com">[The Black Vault]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theblackvault.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Black Vault Update on Operation Majestic 12 Documents</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Black VaultUpdate on Operation Majestic 12 Documents - The Black Vault Case FilesJanuary 17, 2024...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 17, 2024</span></span></span>

That distinction became more important as additional MJ-12 material appeared. Friedman eventually argued that the large collection supplied to researcher Timothy Cooper contained demonstrable “emulations”: genuine historical documents had apparently been copied or retyped, with names, destinations or MJ-12 references substituted. This willingness to reject material was central to Friedman’s self-presentation as an authenticator rather than an indiscriminate believer. It also has an important consequence for assessing his argument: evidence that a later Cooper document was fraudulent does not, by itself, establish that the earlier EBD, TFM or CTM was fraudulent. Conversely, proving that one criticism of the core papers was mistaken would not establish that the papers were genuine. Each disputed feature has to be weighed separately.

## His responses to forgery claims

Friedman’s defence was strongest when he could show that a criticism presented as a decisive anachronism was actually less decisive than advertised. His case was weaker when he had to explain away anomalies rather than independently trace a document into official custody.

One recurrent controversy concerned **typefaces and office practice**. Philip Klass had argued that the Cutler-Twining memorandum’s Pica type did not fit the National Security Council material he had examined, which predominantly used Elite type. Friedman searched Eisenhower-era files and said he produced more than fourteen documents from James Lay’s NSC office using the same Pica typeface. According to Friedman’s account, Klass consequently paid him $1,000 under a wager concerning whether such examples could be found. Whatever one makes of the wager, it undercut the simple proposition that Pica type alone made the CTM impossible as a 1954 NSC document.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.theblackvault.com/casefiles/update-on-operation-majestic-12-documents/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theblackvault.com">[The Black Vault]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theblackvault.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Black Vault Update on Operation Majestic 12 Documents</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Black VaultUpdate on Operation Majestic 12 Documents - The Black Vault Case FilesJanuary 17, 2024...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 17, 2024</span></span></span>

Friedman made a similar argument about the **date and formatting conventions** of the EBD. Critics noted irregular styles, including the form “18 November, 1952”, and argued that it departed from standard government practice. Friedman answered that archival records did not follow one immutable style: in his research he found White House and intelligence documents using several date formats, sometimes within the same office over short periods. This response matters because a style manual can establish what was prescribed without proving what every official actually typed. It reduces the force of a formatting anomaly, but it still does not establish who created the EBD.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://medium.com/stanton-friedmans-articles/update-on-operation-majestic-12-documents-ed2df58fd8a1" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: medium.com">[Medium]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">medium.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UPDATE ON OPERATION MAJESTIC 12 DOCUMENTS &#124; by Richard Geldreich, Jr. &#124; Physicist Stanton Friedman’s Articles &#124; Medium...</span></span></span>

The **military ranks** in the briefing attracted a related objection. The document referred generically to men as “Admiral” or “General” rather than always giving their precise grade. Friedman searched Eisenhower White House material and reported finding memoranda by Andrew Goodpaster that similarly used generic ranks for meeting participants, even where Goodpaster himself signed with his exact grade. His conclusion was again limited but relevant: an imprecise rank was not automatically an anachronism or proof of amateur authorship.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.theblackvault.com/casefiles/update-on-operation-majestic-12-documents/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theblackvault.com">[The Black Vault]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theblackvault.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Black Vault Update on Operation Majestic 12 Documents</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Black VaultUpdate on Operation Majestic 12 Documents - The Black Vault Case FilesJanuary 17, 2024...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 17, 2024</span></span></span>

### The Hillenkoetter language argument

Friedman also sought evidence that the EBD’s prose was compatible with its purported author, former CIA director Rear Admiral Roscoe Hillenkoetter. He obtained 27 examples of Hillenkoetter’s writing from the Truman Library and supplied them, along with the EBD, to linguist Robert Wescott. Friedman later quoted Wescott as concluding that there was no compelling linguistic reason to regard the material as fraudulent or to attribute it to someone other than Hillenkoetter.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.theblackvault.com/casefiles/update-on-operation-majestic-12-documents/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theblackvault.com">[The Black Vault]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theblackvault.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Black Vault Update on Operation Majestic 12 Documents</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Black VaultUpdate on Operation Majestic 12 Documents - The Black Vault Case FilesJanuary 17, 2024...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 17, 2024</span></span></span>

That finding was useful to Friedman because it answered a particular question: did the briefing’s linguistic style obviously contradict Hillenkoetter’s known writing? Wescott’s answer, as Friedman reported it, was no. Yet stylistic compatibility has an obvious limit. A capable imitator using authentic correspondence as a model can produce compatible language, and stylistics cannot provide a chain of custody. Wescott’s conclusion therefore functions more naturally as evidence **against an obvious stylistic forgery** than as positive proof that Hillenkoetter created the document.

## The Truman signature was Friedman's hardest problem

The Truman-Forrestal memorandum presented perhaps the most damaging technical objection to the original set. Its Truman signature closely resembles a signature on an authentic 1 October 1947 Truman letter to Vannevar Bush. Critics, notably Klass, argued that the genuine signature had been copied and transplanted onto the alleged MJ-12 memorandum. The similarity was especially suspicious because apparent incidental pen marks were also said to correspond.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.theufochronicles.com/2014/06/the-myth-of-mj-12-appendix-pt-2.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theufochronicles.com">[The UFO Chronicles]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theufochronicles.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">the myth of mj 12 appendix pt 2</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">the myth of mj 12 appendix pt 2</span></span></span>

Friedman did not simply deny that the signatures resembled one another; indeed, he took credit for finding the Bush document used in the comparison. He argued that measurements showed that portions of the two signatures did not scale identically and therefore resisted describing them as exact copies. More importantly, he offered an alternative explanation for why a reproduced signature might appear on a genuine briefing attachment. He suggested that the original Forrestal memorandum could have been unavailable by 1952 and that intelligence personnel preparing material for Eisenhower might have reproduced Truman’s signature from another document for inclusion in the briefing copy.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.theblackvault.com/casefiles/update-on-operation-majestic-12-documents/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theblackvault.com">[The Black Vault]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theblackvault.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Black Vault Update on Operation Majestic 12 Documents</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Black VaultUpdate on Operation Majestic 12 Documents - The Black Vault Case FilesJanuary 17, 2024...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 17, 2024</span></span></span>

A document examiner cited by Friedman, James A. Black, also complicated rather than resolved the dispute. Black reportedly concluded that the memorandum’s typeface was compatible with an Underwood Standard exemplar from 1940, weakening claims that the typing technology necessarily dated from the 1960s. But Black also judged the Truman signature itself most likely to be a reproduction because of characteristics of its ink line. Friedman regarded that as compatible with his “briefing copy” explanation rather than proof of forgery.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.theblackvault.com/casefiles/update-on-operation-majestic-12-documents/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theblackvault.com">[The Black Vault]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theblackvault.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Black Vault Update on Operation Majestic 12 Documents</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Black VaultUpdate on Operation Majestic 12 Documents - The Black Vault Case FilesJanuary 17, 2024...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 17, 2024</span></span></span>

This illustrates the central weakness in Friedman’s method. He could devise a historically possible scenario in which a genuine government file contained a reproduced signature. But possibility is not evidence that this actually happened. Once a signature that should be powerful authentication evidence instead requires a special explanation, the burden on independent provenance becomes greater, not smaller.

## Why he thought the Cutler-Twining memo was unusually strong

For Friedman, the Cutler-Twining memorandum was especially significant because, unlike the anonymously mailed EBD photographs, a physical copy was found in a federal archive. The memorandum refers explicitly to an “NSC/MJ-12 Special Studies Project”, apparently connecting the term MJ-12 to Eisenhower-era national-security administration.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/research/military/air-force/ufos" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives Project BLUE BOOK</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesProject BLUE BOOK - Unidentified Flying Objects &#124; National Archives...</span></span></span>

Critics quickly identified an awkward fact: Robert Cutler was travelling abroad on 14 July 1954, the date on the memorandum. The National Archives itself records that Cutler was visiting military installations in Europe and North Africa from 3 to 15 July. Its reference report adds that Cutler had left instructions to subordinates James Lay and J. Patrick Coyne concerning NSC administrative matters during his absence. The Archives regarded Cutler’s absence as one of several reasons the memorandum posed authentication problems.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/research/military/air-force/ufos" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives Project BLUE BOOK</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesProject BLUE BOOK - Unidentified Flying Objects &#124; National Archives...</span></span></span>

Friedman turned precisely that objection into part of his defence. The CTM is unsigned and contains no “/s/” notation indicating that Cutler had personally signed an original. Friedman argued that Lay, the NSC executive secretary, could have issued a short scheduling memorandum in Cutler’s name while handling his office during the trip. He further reported finding correspondence showing that Cutler had told Lay to keep matters moving during his absence and that Lay was communicating with both Cutler and Eisenhower. In Friedman’s view, a naïve forger might actually have made the more obvious mistake of putting Cutler’s signature on the document.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.theblackvault.com/casefiles/update-on-operation-majestic-12-documents/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theblackvault.com">[The Black Vault]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theblackvault.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Black Vault Update on Operation Majestic 12 Documents</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Black VaultUpdate on Operation Majestic 12 Documents - The Black Vault Case FilesJanuary 17, 2024...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 17, 2024</span></span></span>

He also emphasised physical and clerical details: the CTM was on onionskin paper, bore a slanting red pencil mark through its security marking, and used a typeface he found elsewhere in NSC material. These points made the document harder to dismiss as a crude modern fabrication.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://medium.com/stanton-friedmans-articles/book-review-kevin-d-randles-case-mj-12-the-true-story-behind-the-government-s-ufo-conspiracies-9e06f4e82250" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: medium.com">[Medium]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">medium.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Book Review: Kevin D. Yet the National Archives found broader problems that Friedman's Lay hypothesis did not remove. The document lacked the [Top Secret register]({{ 'register-numbers/' | relative_url }}) number used to organise the surrounding record series; no other MJ-12 document appeared in its folder; searches of Air Force, Joint Chiefs of Staff, National Security Council and presidential-library holdings found no supporting documentation; and Eisenhower’s appointment books contained no entry for the purported 16 July 1954 MJ-12 briefing. ## The “how would a hoaxer know?” argument

Friedman’s most distinctive positive argument concerned details that he said were confirmed only after the core papers surfaced. He claimed to have assembled dozens of such points and repeatedly asked how a forger would have known them.

One example involved **Donald Menzel**, the Harvard astronomer and prominent public UFO sceptic listed among the alleged MJ-12 members. Friedman initially regarded Menzel’s inclusion as suspicious. His subsequent research in Menzel’s papers, however, convinced him that Menzel had undertaken extensive classified work connected with US intelligence and cryptographic organisations. Friedman argued that this made Menzel a much more plausible candidate for a highly classified committee than his public career alone suggested.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.theblackvault.com/casefiles/update-on-operation-majestic-12-documents/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theblackvault.com">[The Black Vault]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theblackvault.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Black Vault Update on Operation Majestic 12 Documents</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Black VaultUpdate on Operation Majestic 12 Documents - The Black Vault Case FilesJanuary 17, 2024...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 17, 2024</span></span></span>

This is an interesting discovery, but it requires careful separation of two propositions. Menzel having classified connections undermines the objection that he was obviously unsuitable for secret government work. It does **not** show that he served on MJ-12. Friedman acknowledged that he found no direct reference to MJ-12 in Menzel’s records; he argued that one should not expect records of a deeply compartmented programme to survive in an open personal archive.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.theblackvault.com/casefiles/update-on-operation-majestic-12-documents/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theblackvault.com">[The Black Vault]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theblackvault.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Black Vault Update on Operation Majestic 12 Documents</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Black VaultUpdate on Operation Majestic 12 Documents - The Black Vault Case FilesJanuary 17, 2024...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 17, 2024</span></span></span>

Friedman used the same reasoning with **Nathan Twining’s movements**. The EBD places the beginning of an investigation in New Mexico on 7 July 1947. Friedman later found flight records showing Twining travelling from Dayton to New Mexico on that date, which he regarded as a striking retrospective confirmation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.theblackvault.com/casefiles/update-on-operation-majestic-12-documents/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theblackvault.com">[The Black Vault]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theblackvault.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Black Vault Update on Operation Majestic 12 Documents</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Black VaultUpdate on Operation Majestic 12 Documents - The Black Vault Case FilesJanuary 17, 2024...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 17, 2024</span></span></span>

Again, the evidential value is real but bounded. A correct obscure detail increases the sophistication required of a forger if there is good reason to believe that the information was genuinely inaccessible beforehand. It does not identify the author, and it becomes less probative if an unknown document maker could have had archival access, military contacts or unpublished records. Friedman’s argument is therefore strongest as an argument against the idea of an uninformed, careless hoaxer.

## Where his case remained incomplete

Friedman succeeded in showing that several popular “one-line” debunking arguments were too strong. The wrong typeface was not demonstrably wrong; governmental date formats were less uniform than critics sometimes implied; generic military ranks existed in contemporary White House records; Menzel’s secret-government background was more substantial than outsiders had known; and Cutler’s absence did not make it administratively impossible for his office to send an unsigned memorandum in his name. These findings are a legitimate part of the historical debate.

But they do not amount to authentication. The most important missing evidence remained unchanged: no original EBD or Truman-Forrestal memorandum with traceable government custody has surfaced. The EBD arrived as images on anonymously mailed film. The CTM’s physical presence in Record Group 341 gives it a different evidential status, but the National Archives documented serious filing, scheduling and records-context anomalies around it.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/research/military/air-force/ufos" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives Project BLUE BOOK</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesProject BLUE BOOK - Unidentified Flying Objects &#124; National Archives...</span></span></span>

Official searches also failed to produce the supporting paper trail that would normally transform circumstantial compatibility into historical confirmation. In 1995 the US Government Accountability Office reported that the Information Security Oversight Office, [Air Force]({{ 'air-force/' | relative_url }}) and National Archives knew the Majestic-12 material only through submissions from private individuals and that there was no evidence the material constituted actual documents originating in the executive branch.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gao.gov/products/154832" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gao.gov">[Government Accountability Office]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gao.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Government Accountability Office[Comments on Majestic 12 Material] &#124; U.S. GAOJuly 28, 1995...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 28, 1995</span></span></span>

The FBI’s position was more categorical. After copies of the alleged briefing reached its offices in 1988, the Bureau consulted the Air Force and subsequently characterised the document as fake; the FBI continues to describe the Majestic-12 document in its public archive as fraudulent.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://vault.fbi.gov/search?SearchableText=Majestic" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: vault.fbi.gov">[FBI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">vault.fbi.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">— Federal Bureau of Investigation</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">— Federal Bureau of Investigation</span></span></span>

Those official findings do not, by themselves, explain who created the papers or precisely how the CTM entered archival files. Nor do they negate every archival observation Friedman made. They do establish that his cumulative defence never crossed the key threshold from **plausibility arguments and rebutted anomalies** to **verified governmental provenance**.

## What Friedman's defence ultimately establishes

The fairest reading of Stanton Friedman’s MJ-12 work lies between two caricatures. He was not simply accepting every document carrying an MJ-12 label: he rejected numerous later papers after comparing them with authentic sources and identifying copied wording, transplanted handwriting and historical errors.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.theblackvault.com/casefiles/update-on-operation-majestic-12-documents/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theblackvault.com">[The Black Vault]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theblackvault.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Black Vault Update on Operation Majestic 12 Documents</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Black VaultUpdate on Operation Majestic 12 Documents - The Black Vault Case FilesJanuary 17, 2024...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 17, 2024</span></span></span>

Nor, however, did his extensive archival research authenticate the three papers he most strongly defended. Much of his best work demonstrated that particular alleged “proofs of forgery” were not proofs at all. That is historically worthwhile, because questioned documents should not be condemned on erroneous assumptions about typewriters, formatting or bureaucratic procedure.

The unresolved step was positive identification. Friedman's evidence could often support the statement, **“this feature is compatible with the period”**, or **“a knowledgeable government office could have done this”**. It could not securely establish, **“this document was created by the US government in 1947, 1952 or 1954 as part of a real MJ-12 programme.”** The absence of authenticated originals, the uncertain chain of custody, the anomalous archival context of the CTM and the negative results of government searches remained obstacles that his rebuttals never eliminated.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/research/military/air-force/ufos" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives Project BLUE BOOK</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesProject BLUE BOOK - Unidentified Flying Objects &#124; National Archives...</span></span></span>

That distinction is the lasting significance of Friedman’s case within the alleged MJ-12 documents controversy. He made the argument for the core papers more sophisticated than a simple appeal to their appearance, and he demonstrated why later hoaxes should not automatically be used to dispose of earlier material. But his defence ultimately remained a case for contested plausibility rather than documentary authentication.
