## Introduction

The General Accounting Office (GAO) reached a narrow but important conclusion about [the alleged MJ-12 documents]({{ 'the-alleged-mj-12-documents/' | relative_url }}) in 1995: after consulting the federal bodies most likely to possess or recognise such records, it found no evidence that the publicly circulated material was genuinely created within the executive branch of the United States government. The Information Security Oversight Office, the [Air Force]({{ 'air-force/' | relative_url }}) and the National Archives all told GAO that their knowledge of the material came from documents supplied by people outside government, not from official files. GAO said its own work uncovered nothing that contradicted that assessment.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gao.gov/products/154832" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gao.gov">[GAO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gao.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GAO[Comments on Majestic 12 Material] &#124; U.S. GAOJuly 28, 1995...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 28, 1995</span></span></span>

## Why government records were searched

GAO's examination of MJ-12 grew out of a broader inquiry requested by Representative Steven H. Schiff into surviving federal records concerning the 1947 Roswell incident. The main GAO investigation examined classified and unclassified material from the late 1940s and 1950s across the Department of Defense, Federal Bureau of Investigation, Central Intelligence Agency, National Security Council and other organisations. A separate two-page letter, also dated 28 July 1995, addressed the authenticity of the publicly circulated Majestic 12 material directly.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gao.gov/products/nsiad" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gao.gov">[gao.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gao.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">nsiad</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Government Records: Results of a Search for Records Concerning the 1947 Crash Near Roswell, New Mexico &#124; U.S. GAOJuly 28, 1995...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 28, 1995</span></span></span>

That distinction matters. The main Roswell audit asked what government records survived concerning the 1947 crash. The MJ-12 letter asked a more specific provenance question: did federal agencies recognise the alleged Majestic 12 papers as authentic records produced inside government?

GAO approached agencies with institutional responsibility or relevant archival holdings. It consulted the Information Security Oversight Office (ISOO), which oversees executive-branch programmes for classified national-security information; the Office of the Secretary of the Air Force's security and investigative staff; and the National Archives. According to GAO, all said that what they knew of MJ-12 came from written material submitted by nongovernmental individuals. Their own files supplied no independent documentary source for it.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gao.gov/products/154832" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gao.gov">[GAO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gao.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GAO[Comments on Majestic 12 Material] &#124; U.S. GAOJuly 28, 1995...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 28, 1995</span></span></span>

This is an important authentication test because a purported presidentially authorised, highly classified programme would normally be expected to leave some corroborating administrative footprint beyond a single disputed set of papers: associated correspondence, classification records, index entries, briefing references, routing copies, meeting documentation or later records referring back to the programme. Secrecy could sharply limit such traces, but the existence of multiple independent records would make an executive-branch origin considerably easier to establish.

## The executive-branch evidence gap

GAO reported a consistent answer from the agencies it contacted: they had found no records in their files relating to Majestic 12, and there was no evidence that the circulating MJ-12 material consisted of documents originally created within the executive branch. ISOO and the Air Force went further, advising that the papers should not be treated as material that had actually been classified by an executive-branch agency or official. GAO stated that its own investigation found nothing contradicting those conclusions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gao.gov/products/154832" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gao.gov">[GAO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gao.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GAO[Comments on Majestic 12 Material] &#124; U.S. GAOJuly 28, 1995...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 28, 1995</span></span></span>

The [presidential-library]({{ 'library-searches/' | relative_url }}) searches reinforced that gap. Archivists at both the Harry S. Truman and Dwight D. Eisenhower libraries told GAO that they had received public questions about MJ-12 for years. Their searches, including classified intelligence and National Security Council material, had produced nothing fitting the description of the alleged Majestic 12 papers and no references to that designation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gao.gov/products/154832" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gao.gov">[GAO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gao.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GAO[Comments on Majestic 12 Material] &#124; U.S. GAOJuly 28, 1995...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 28, 1995</span></span></span>

The National Archives describes a similarly broad search history. Its staff searched Air Force and Joint Chiefs of Staff holdings, while the Truman and Eisenhower libraries examined their own records. National Security Council indexes were checked under terms including “MJ-12”, “majestic”, “unidentified flying objects”, “UFO”, “flying saucers”, “extraterrestrial biological entities” and “Aquarius”. Those searches were negative apart from the disputed Cutler-to-[Twining]({{ 'twining/' | relative_url }}) memorandum already known to researchers.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/research/military/air-force/ufos" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives Project BLUE BOOK</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesProject BLUE BOOK - Unidentified Flying Objects &#124; National ArchivesJune 25, 2024...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 25, 2024</span></span></span>

That exception does not supply the missing corroborating chain. The Cutler-Twining memorandum was physically located in National Archives holdings, but archival staff identified multiple anomalies. It lacked the [Top Secret register]({{ 'register-numbers/' | relative_url }}) number expected for the series in which it appeared; no related MJ-12 material was found in its folder; searches of Secretary of Defense, Joint Chiefs, Air Force and National Security Council records produced no supporting documentation; and the Eisenhower Library found no corresponding presidential appointment-book entry for the purported 16 July 1954 briefing. National Archives research also established that Robert Cutler was away inspecting military facilities in Europe and North Africa when the memorandum was supposedly issued in his name on 14 July.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/research/military/air-force/ufos" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives Project BLUE BOOK</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesProject BLUE BOOK - Unidentified Flying Objects &#124; National ArchivesJune 25, 2024...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 25, 2024</span></span></span>

## The “MJ Twelve” message failed a second authenticity test

GAO also encountered a document that might, at first sight, have offered independent support for the name. Among material sent to ISOO by members of the public in connection with earlier Freedom of Information Act requests was a message dated 17 November 1980 that appeared to have originated within the Air Force Office of Special Investigations (AFOSI). It contained the words “[MJ Twelve]({{ 'mj-twelve/' | relative_url }})”.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://groups.google.com/g/alt.ufo.reports/c/1g8hHH9pHEo" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: groups.google.com">[Google Groups]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">groups.google.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Groups CONSPIr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Google GroupsCONSPIrMarch 12, 2000...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 12, 2000</span></span></span>

Rather than treating the apparent government formatting as proof, GAO asked AFOSI to check the message. The commander of AFOSI's Investigative Operations Center reported in February 1995 that a search of AFOSI files had found no official record copy. He also told GAO that AFOSI had examined the same message during an earlier Freedom of Information Act inquiry and had concluded that it was a forgery.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://groups.google.com/g/alt.ufo.reports/c/1g8hHH9pHEo" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: groups.google.com">[Google Groups]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">groups.google.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Groups CONSPIr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Google GroupsCONSPIrMarch 12, 2000...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 12, 2000</span></span></span>

This episode illustrates the core problem GAO was testing. A document can contain official terminology, security markings and the name of a real government organisation while still lacking an authenticating record inside that organisation. For provenance purposes, an internally retained copy, routing record or related file can matter more than how convincing the typography looks. The supposed 1980 AFOSI message supplied no such confirmation and therefore did not close the executive-branch evidence gap.

## What a negative search can and cannot establish

GAO's conclusion should be stated precisely. It did **not** say that its research constituted mathematical proof that an MJ-12 organisation could never have existed. It said there was no evidence that the publicly circulated Majestic 12 material represented actual documents originally created in the executive branch, and that GAO found nothing contradicting the agencies that had reached that conclusion.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gao.gov/products/154832" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gao.gov">[GAO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gao.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GAO[Comments on Majestic 12 Material] &#124; U.S. GAOJuly 28, 1995...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 28, 1995</span></span></span>

There are practical reasons not to turn any archival search into an absolute claim about everything that might once have existed. GAO's broader Roswell investigation found that some Roswell Army Air Field records had been destroyed. Administrative records covering March 1945 through December 1949 and outgoing messages covering October 1946 through December 1949 were gone, and the surviving disposition documentation did not identify who destroyed them, when the destruction occurred or under what authority. GAO also noted that its search was complicated by missing records and by record-management rules that were sometimes unclear or changing.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://irp.fas.org/gao/nsi95187.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: irp.fas.org">[Intelligence Resource Program]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">irp.fas.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Intelligence Resource Program GAO</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Intelligence Resource ProgramGAO - Government Records: Results of a Search for Records Concerning the 1947 Crash Near Roswell, New Mexico...</span></span></span>

That missing-record problem is genuinely relevant to how strongly a negative search should be expressed, but it does not positively authenticate MJ-12. A destroyed Roswell message file does not establish that the missing messages mentioned Majestic 12, and GAO did not claim that they did. Likewise, the possibility that exceptionally sensitive records could have been compartmented, misfiled or destroyed is a reason to avoid claiming perfect archival completeness; it is not affirmative evidence that the disputed documents are genuine.

The evidential weight instead comes from convergence. The problem was not merely that a single “MJ-12” folder was missing. Agencies responsible for classified-information oversight did not recognise the circulating material as their records; relevant Air Force files did not corroborate it; presidential libraries found no supporting references; National Security Council searches were negative; the principal archival MJ-12 memorandum presented anomalies rather than a normal documentary trail; and another purported official document using “MJ Twelve” was judged forged by the agency it supposedly came from.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/research/military/air-force/ufos" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[archives.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives Project BLUE BOOK</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesProject BLUE BOOK - Unidentified Flying Objects &#124; National ArchivesJune 25, 2024...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 25, 2024</span></span></span>

For the alleged MJ-12 documents, that is the central significance of the GAO search. It did not settle every conceivable historical hypothesis about secret government activity. It did establish that, when investigators looked for the institutional traces that should support the papers' claimed provenance, they did not find an executive-branch record trail capable of authenticating them.
