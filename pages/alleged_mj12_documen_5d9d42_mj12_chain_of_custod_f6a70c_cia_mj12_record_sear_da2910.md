## Introduction

The CIA’s own released information-review and Freedom of Information Act records supply an unusually clear answer to one narrow question in the MJ-12 controversy: repeated CIA searches did not locate records establishing Majestic 12 as an agency programme or tying the circulating MJ-12 papers to CIA [custody]({{ 'custody/' | relative_url }}). One CIA response says an earlier search was “thorough and diligent”; after the requester appealed, the agency searched again and still located nothing. A separate CIA case log records a Majestic-12 request simply as “No Records Located”.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cia.gov/readingroom/docs/F-2003-00606%20FINAL%20RESPON%5B16139957%5D.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cia.gov">[CIA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cia.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Approved for Release: 2022/10/13 C00934938October 19, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 19, 2025</span></span></span>

Those results do not logically prove that no Majestic 12 record could ever have existed anywhere in government. Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) searches have defined scopes and legal limitations. But for the narrower chain-of-custody question, the distinction is crucial: the searches failed to produce the archival bridge that the alleged documents need. Nothing located by the CIA connects the publicly circulating papers backwards into an identifiable CIA file, records system or documented agency custody.

## What the CIA searches were trying to locate

The most useful CIA evidence is not a general statement about unidentified flying objects but the agency’s description of its own Majestic 12 searches. In a released response to FOIA case F-2003-00606, the CIA told a requester that its records showed it had previously searched for “records pertaining to Majestic 12”. The agency characterised that search as thorough and diligent and said no records had been located. More importantly, the earlier requester had appealed the result, prompting a second search. That search also found no records.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cia.gov/readingroom/docs/F-2003-00606%20FINAL%20RESPON%5B16139957%5D.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cia.gov">[CIA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cia.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Approved for Release: 2022/10/13 C00934938October 19, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 19, 2025</span></span></span>

That second search matters because it makes the evidential position stronger than a single unsuccessful database query. An administrative appeal gave the CIA another opportunity to reconsider whether responsive material had been missed. Yet the outcome remained negative. The surviving CIA correspondence does not identify a recovered Majestic file, routing slip, memorandum, register entry or related body of records after either search.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cia.gov/readingroom/docs/F-2003-00606%20FINAL%20RESPON%5B16139957%5D.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cia.gov">[CIA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cia.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Approved for Release: 2022/10/13 C00934938October 19, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 19, 2025</span></span></span>

The result also appears in the agency’s administrative record. A CIA FOIA case log lists case F-2001-01943, subject “MAJESTIC-12”, with the disposition “No Records Located”. The case-log entry is significant because it is an agency-generated record documenting the processing of an MJ-12 request, rather than one of the alleged historical MJ-12 papers submitted from outside government. It therefore demonstrates that the CIA dealt with the subject as a records-search question while simultaneously showing that the search did not recover the historical records being sought.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cia.gov/readingroom/docs/FOIA%20CASE%20LOG%20CALENDER%20YE%5B16111550%5D.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cia.gov">[CIA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cia.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">FOIA CASE LOG CALENDER YE[16111550</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Approved for Release: 2022/O8/23 CO6935701...</span></span></span>

An internal CIA information-review document provides still broader confirmation. Discussing subjects frequently raised by UFO researchers, it states that Majestic 12 was a recurring request topic and that the CIA had been unable to locate records on it in previous FOIA searches. The same document separately says that the CIA held no information on the alleged Roswell event. The wording is useful because it describes an established pattern of unsuccessful MJ-12 searches rather than treating the absence as the result of one isolated request.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cia.gov/readingroom/docs/INFORMATION%20REVIEW%20%26%20RELE%5B15598960%5D.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cia.gov">[CIA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cia.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">INFORMATION REVIEW &amp; RELE[15598960</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Approved for Release: 2019/03/27 C01247755March 26, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 26, 2026</span></span></span>

## What a “no records” result actually means

A negative FOIA result is narrower than the statement “this thing never existed”. Under the Freedom of Information Act, a search means reviewing [agency records]({{ 'agency-records/' | relative_url }}), manually or electronically, to locate records responsive to a request. The statute requires reasonable search efforts; it does not convert an unsuccessful search into proof about every document that might once have existed in every government repository.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.justice.gov/oip/freedom-information-act-5-usc-552" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: justice.gov">[Department of Justice]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">justice.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">freedom information act 5 usc 552</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Department of JusticeOffice of Information Policy &#124; The Freedom of Information Act, 5 U.S.C. § 552January 21, 2022...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 21, 2022</span></span></span>

US Department of Justice guidance makes the same distinction explicit. Courts judge the adequacy of a FOIA search principally by whether the methods were reasonably calculated to find responsive records, not simply by what the search produced. In one case summarised by the Department, the court stressed that the issue was not whether further documents might conceivably exist, but whether the government's search was adequate.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.justice.gov/oip/page/file/1199421/download" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: justice.gov">[Department of Justice]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">justice.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Department of JusticeDepartment of Justice Guide to the Freedom of Information ActApril 13, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 13, 2026</span></span></span>

That distinction prevents two opposite overstatements about MJ-12. The CIA’s negative searches should not be presented as mathematical proof that no person anywhere in the US government ever used the words “Majestic 12”. Conversely, it is equally unsound to dismiss the searches as evidentially meaningless merely because a hypothetical hidden or lost record can be imagined. The actual historical question is comparative: what documentary traces should be available if the circulating papers really descended from an official classified records system, and what traces have searches actually produced?

There is a further technical qualification. CIA records are subject to specialised statutory rules, including provisions concerning operational files. In other FOIA litigation, courts have recognised that CIA operational files can be categorically exempt from ordinary FOIA search and review requirements under the CIA Information Act, subject to statutory exceptions. That limitation means a CIA “no records” response should not automatically be read as a certified inventory of every conceivable CIA-held record.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.justice.gov/oip/talbot-v-dept-state-no-17-cv-0588-2018-wl-2739921-ddc-june-7-2018-cooper-j" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: justice.gov">[Department of Justice]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">justice.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">talbot v dept state no 17 cv 0588 2018 wl 2739921 ddc june 7 2018 cooper j</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Department of JusticeOffice of Information Policy &#124; Talbot v. The CIA’s handling of F-2003-00606 itself illustrates why wording matters. In the same response in which the agency straightforwardly said that repeated Majestic 12 searches had located no records, it gave a different answer concerning Area 51: for that portion of the request it said it could neither confirm nor deny whether responsive records existed, invoking national-security and intelligence-sources-and-methods grounds. Whatever conclusions are drawn from that contrast, the documented point is that the CIA did **not** use the same neither-confirm-nor-deny formulation for the Majestic 12 search. It reported unsuccessful searches instead.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cia.gov/readingroom/docs/F-2003-00606%20FINAL%20RESPON%5B16139957%5D.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cia.gov">[CIA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cia.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Approved for Release: 2022/10/13 C00934938October 19, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 19, 2025</span></span></span>

## Why the searches never supplied the missing archival bridge

The central problem is provenance. The National Archives defines provenance both as the principle of keeping records associated with the records-creating unit and as information about a particular record’s chain of ownership and custody. Its federal holdings are organised on the same principle: records are attributed to the agency that created or maintained them and, where possible, kept in the arrangement in which they were used.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/research/alic/reference/archives-resources/terminology.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesArchives and Records Management Resources &#124; National ArchivesAugust 15, 2016...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 15, 2016</span></span></span>

For an alleged CIA-linked MJ-12 document, therefore, a powerful authentication trail would not necessarily require the CIA to endorse its extraordinary contents. Something more mundane could establish the missing link: an agency file containing the document; a registry or control entry; an authenticated CIA copy received from another office; correspondence discussing its handling; or related records whose filing context independently identifies the document and explains why the CIA possessed it. Such material would connect the object now circulating publicly to an institutional records system.

The released CIA search results provide none of those things. They document **searches about MJ-12**, but those search records are not themselves evidence of **historical CIA custody of the alleged MJ-12 papers**. That distinction is easy to miss. A modern CIA FOIA file can prove that somebody asked the CIA about Majestic 12 and that the agency processed the request. It cannot, without responsive historical records, move the disputed papers backwards through time into CIA custody.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cia.gov/readingroom/docs/F-2003-00606%20FINAL%20RESPON%5B16139957%5D.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cia.gov">[CIA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cia.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Approved for Release: 2022/10/13 C00934938October 19, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 19, 2025</span></span></span>

This is where the CIA evidence fits into the wider federal archival picture. The National Archives says it made extensive searches among [Air Force]({{ 'air-force/' | relative_url }}) and Joint Chiefs of Staff records for MJ-12 material; the Truman and Eisenhower presidential libraries also searched their holdings. National Archives staff additionally searched related defence, Joint Chiefs and Air Force files and reported finding no further information supporting the subject.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/research/military/air-force/ufos" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives Project BLUE BOOK</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Archives Project BLUE BOOK</span></span></span>

The Government Accountability Office reached the same provenance problem from another direction in 1995. After seeking the views of the Information Security Oversight Office, Air Force and National Archives, GAO reported that those organisations' knowledge of Majestic 12 was limited to written material submitted by nongovernmental people and that they found no records in their files relating to Majestic 12. GAO found nothing contradicting their conclusion that there was no evidence the circulating Majestic 12 material consisted of genuine documents originating in the executive branch.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gao.gov/products/154832" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gao.gov">[Government Accountability Office]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gao.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on gao.gov.</span></span></span>

The CIA records therefore add an important agency-specific layer to that broader finding. They do not merely leave the origin of the papers unidentified; they show that attempts to locate the expected CIA side of the documentary trail repeatedly came back empty. In chain-of-custody terms, the arrow runs from outside requesters **towards** the CIA — people asking the agency about Majestic 12 — but no located archival evidence completes the arrow in the opposite direction, from a historical CIA records system **towards** the disputed documents.

## The difference between absence and missing provenance

The most defensible conclusion is consequently narrower, but stronger, than saying “the CIA proved MJ-12 never existed”. The available CIA evidence establishes repeated unsuccessful record searches. It does not establish universal non-existence, and FOIA law itself cautions against treating search results that way.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cia.gov/readingroom/docs/F-2003-00606%20FINAL%20RESPON%5B16139957%5D.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cia.gov">[CIA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cia.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Approved for Release: 2022/10/13 C00934938October 19, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 19, 2025</span></span></span>

For document authentication, however, universal non-existence is not the standard that matters. The burden is to establish the provenance of the particular papers being offered as historical government records. National Archives practice underscores why: archival descriptions link records to their creators and to the series and record groups in which they belong, preserving the organisational context that gives a government document much of its evidential meaning.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/research/catalog/lcdrg/archival-materials" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives Archival Materials and Related Elements &#124; National Archives</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Archives Archival Materials and Related Elements &#124; National Archives</span></span></span>

The CIA searches never supplied that context for MJ-12. No located CIA record identified a circulating core document as an agency-held classified record; no located file history carried one of those documents through CIA custody; and the CIA's released search documentation instead records unsuccessful efforts to find Majestic 12 records, including a repeat search following an appeal.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cia.gov/readingroom/docs/F-2003-00606%20FINAL%20RESPON%5B16139957%5D.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cia.gov">[CIA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cia.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Approved for Release: 2022/10/13 C00934938October 19, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 19, 2025</span></span></span>

That is the missing archival bridge in its most precise form. The negative searches are not a stand-alone disproof of every Majestic 12 allegation. They are evidence that, when the CIA looked for the records needed to connect the allegation to its own documentary system, it did not find them. Combined with the parallel National Archives and GAO findings, the result leaves the circulating MJ-12 documents on the public side of the provenance gap rather than securely anchored to documented classified government custody.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/research/military/air-force/ufos" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives Project BLUE BOOK</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Archives Project BLUE BOOK</span></span></span>
