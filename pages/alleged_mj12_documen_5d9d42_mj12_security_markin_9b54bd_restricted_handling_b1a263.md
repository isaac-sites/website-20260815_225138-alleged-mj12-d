## Introduction

Yes — in principle, “[Restricted]({{ 'restricted/' | relative_url }})” could have been intended as a handling caveat rather than as a fourth classification level. That is the strongest version of the defence offered for the disputed MJ-12 wording. A document could be formally classified **Top Secret** while carrying an additional instruction limiting who could see it. Genuine Eisenhower-era records demonstrate exactly that general mechanism: Top Secret material could also be marked “Eyes Only”, “Special Handling” or “Limited Distribution”.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1952-54v02p1/d143" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: history.state.gov">[Office of the Historian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">history.state.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Office of the Historian Historical Documents</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Office of the HistorianHistorical Documents - Office of the HistorianDecember 21, 1954...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 21, 1954</span></span></span>



<figure class="article-media-figure article-media-figure-illustration"><img src="{{ "/assets/images/alleged_mj12_documen_5d9d42_mj12_security_markin_9b54bd_restricted_handling_b1a263-Illustration-1-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" alt="Handling Caveat illustration 1" data-theme-src-dark="{{ "/assets/images/alleged_mj12_documen_5d9d42_mj12_security_markin_9b54bd_restricted_handling_b1a263-Illustration-1-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" data-theme-src-light="{{ "/assets/images/alleged_mj12_documen_5d9d42_mj12_security_markin_9b54bd_restricted_handling_b1a263-Illustration-1-light.svg" | relative_url }}" width="1920" height="1080" loading="lazy" decoding="async" fetchpriority="low"><figcaption class="article-media-caption"><span class="article-media-caption-label">Explanatory illustration 1</span></figcaption></figure>

The difficulty is narrower but substantial. Archival evidence shows that the US Government had just abolished **Restricted** as an ordinary defence classification, while authentic highly sensitive records used recognisable supplementary language to impose tighter distribution. More importantly, the National Archives says the National Security Council itself reported that the disputed “Top Secret Restricted Information” marking did not enter NSC use until the Nixon administration, and the Eisenhower [Presidential Library]({{ 'library-searches/' | relative_url }}) found it absent from Eisenhower-era practice.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/research/military/air-force/ufos" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives Project BLUE BOOK</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesProject BLUE BOOK - Unidentified Flying Objects &#124; National ArchivesJune 25, 2024...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 25, 2024</span></span></span> Thus the caveat hypothesis is mechanically possible, but it does not by itself explain the specific wording attributed to Robert Cutler’s office in July 1954.

## Classification and handling were different things

The distinction on which the defence depends is real. A **classification level** states how damaging unauthorised disclosure would be and therefore what level of protection the information requires. A **handling or dissemination restriction** can impose additional controls within the population already authorised to receive information at that classification.

Executive Order 10501, which governed national-defence classification when the Cutler–[Twining]({{ 'twining/' | relative_url }}) memorandum was supposedly written, limited the ordinary classification system to **Top Secret, Secret and Confidential**. It expressly said that no other designation was to be used to *classify* defence information unless authorised by statute.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.presidency.ucsb.edu/documents/executive-order-10501-safeguarding-official-information-the-interests-the-defense-the" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: presidency.ucsb.edu">[The American Presidency Project]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">presidency.ucsb.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The American Presidency ProjectExecutive Order 10501—Safeguarding Official Information in the Interests of the Defense of the United Stat...</span></span></span> A CIA implementation notice issued in November 1953 consequently stated that the classification “RESTRICTED” had been abolished.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cia.gov/readingroom/document/cia-rdp78-04718a000800290033-8" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cia.gov">[CIA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cia.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">CLASSIFICATION OF DOCUMENTS SAFEGUARDING OFFICIAL INFORMATION IN THE INTERESTS OF THE DEFENSE OF THE UNITED STATES &#124; CIA FOIA (foia.ci...</span></span></span> A 1954 Federal Register notice likewise described the Defence Department's implementation of Executive Order 10501 as having eliminated Restricted from the classification system.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/d/d2/Federal_Register_1954-05-19-_Vol_19_Iss_97_%28IA_sim_federal-register-find_1954-05-19_19_97%29.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: upload.wikimedia.org">[Wikimedia Commons]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">upload.wikimedia.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wikimedia CommonsFederal Register 1954-05-19: Vol 19 Iss 97August 22, 2020...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 22, 2020</span></span></span>

None of that meant that every person holding a Top Secret clearance automatically received every Top Secret document. Contemporary practice imposed further access controls. An official 1954 study of atomic-weapons information, for example, described some Top Secret material as being released only to specified allied officers on a “strictly need-to-know basis”; it separately discussed highly sensitive military information being handled on a restrictive, compartmentalised basis.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1952-54v02p2/d125" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: history.state.gov">[Office of the Historian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">history.state.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Office of the Historian Historical Documents</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Office of the HistorianHistorical Documents - Office of the HistorianDecember 4, 1953...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 4, 1953</span></span></span>

Period documents make the distinction particularly clear. A March 1954 State Department memorandum was recorded as **“top secret / special handling”**.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://history.state.gov//historicaldocuments/frus1952-54v15p2/d874fn4" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: history.state.gov">[Office of the Historian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">history.state.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Office of the Historian Historical Documents</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Office of the HistorianHistorical Documents - Office of the Historian...</span></span></span> A March 1954 telegram was Top Secret but ordered “Limited distribution”.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://history.state.gov//historicaldocuments/frus1952-54v11p2/d802" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: history.state.gov">[Office of the Historian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">history.state.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Office of the Historian Historical Documents</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Office of the HistorianHistorical Documents - Office of the Historian...</span></span></span> And genuine National Security Council discussion memoranda from 1954 carried **“top secret / eyes only”** markings.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1952-54v02p2/d209" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: history.state.gov">[Office of the Historian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">history.state.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Office of the Historian Historical Documents</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Office of the Historian Historical Documents</span></span></span>

Those examples matter because they establish the underlying mechanism without relying on MJ-12 material at all. A second marking beneath “TOP SECRET” need not represent a second classification level. It can tell custodians how a Top Secret document is to be distributed.

## Why the caveat defence has some plausibility

Defenders of the alleged MJ-12 material have made essentially this argument. The word “restricted”, they contend, need not be read as the obsolete **Restricted** classification. In ordinary English it can mean simply that distribution is restricted — effectively an emphatic need-to-know warning attached to an already Top Secret document. One MJ-12 advocacy site explicitly makes this distinction, acknowledging that Restricted had ceased to be a classification while arguing that the word could still carry a generic access-control meaning.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.specialoperationsmanual.com/2014/12/30/som1-01-overview-authentication/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: specialoperationsmanual.com">[specialoperationsmanual.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">specialoperationsmanual.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">SO M1-01 Overview &amp; Authentication</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">SO M1-01 Overview &amp; Authentication</span></span></span>

That interpretation cannot be dismissed merely by saying “Top Secret and Restricted are contradictory”. Authentic documents demonstrate that **Top Secret plus an additional restriction was entirely possible**. The State Department could use “Top Secret; Special Handling”; [NSC records]({{ 'nsc-records/' | relative_url }}) could use “Top Secret / Eyes Only”; telegrams could combine Top Secret classification with limited-distribution instructions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://history.state.gov//historicaldocuments/frus1952-54v15p2/d874fn4" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: history.state.gov">[Office of the Historian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">history.state.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Office of the Historian Historical Documents</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Office of the HistorianHistorical Documents - Office of the Historian...</span></span></span>

There is another historical wrinkle. “Restricted Security Information” had genuinely existed shortly before the alleged 1954 memorandum. A State Department telegram of April 1953 distinguished Top Secret and Secret security information from “Confidential and restricted security information”.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1952-54v01p2/d209" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: history.state.gov">[Office of the Historian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">history.state.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Office of the Historian Historical Documents</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Office of the Historian Historical Documents</span></span></span> An [Air Force]({{ 'air-force/' | relative_url }})-related 1953 record likewise bears the marking “RESTRICTED / SECURITY INFORMATION”.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nicap.org/4602smpl2.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nicap.org">[Nicap]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nicap.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nicap.org.</span></span></span> The terminology was therefore not something invented decades later; people working in the early 1950s had encountered it.

But this point cuts both ways. Executive Order 10501 deliberately changed that system. The CIA's November 1953 implementation instructions specifically said that **“the security classification of RESTRICTED is abolished”**, while explaining how surviving material bearing the old Restricted Security Information designation should be treated.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cia.gov/readingroom/document/cia-rdp78-04718a000800290033-8" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cia.gov">[CIA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cia.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">CLASSIFICATION OF DOCUMENTS SAFEGUARDING OFFICIAL INFORMATION IN THE INTERESTS OF THE DEFENSE OF THE UNITED STATES &#124; CIA FOIA (foia.ci...</span></span></span> A later Defence Department directive similarly described “Restricted-Security Information” as the category used *before* Executive Order 10501 and said that the order had eliminated it.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nsa.gov/Portals/75/documents/news-features/declassified-documents/friedman-documents/reports-research/FOLDER_051/41708959074831.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nsa.gov">[NSA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nsa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nsa.gov.</span></span></span>

So an isolated appearance of the word “restricted” after 1953 is not automatically incriminating. The more discriminating authentication question is whether it appears in a form that makes sense as a handling instruction for that particular office, document type and date.

## The NSC evidence is the harder obstacle

This is where the generic-use defence becomes much weaker. The Cutler–Twining memorandum is not presented as an anonymous military note whose originating office is uncertain. It purports to be a memorandum from Robert Cutler, President Eisenhower's Special Assistant for National Security Affairs, concerning an “NSC/MJ-12 Special Studies Project”. That makes documented National Security Council practice especially relevant.

The National Archives investigated precisely this issue after receiving enquiries about the memorandum. According to its MJ-12 reference report, the NSC Freedom of Information Office told archivists that **“Top Secret Restricted Information”** did not come into use at the National Security Council until the Nixon administration. The Eisenhower Presidential Library separately confirmed that the particular marking was not used during Eisenhower's presidency.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/research/military/air-force/ufos" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives Project BLUE BOOK</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesProject BLUE BOOK - Unidentified Flying Objects &#124; National ArchivesJune 25, 2024...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 25, 2024</span></span></span>

That finding is stronger than the general observation that Restricted ceased to be a classification. If the only objection were Executive Order 10501's three-level system, the handling-caveat defence would have considerable force: the order prohibited an extra *classification*, not necessarily every supplementary access warning. But the National Archives report addresses the **office-specific marking itself**. To preserve the caveat explanation, one must therefore propose that the wording was an unusual or exceptional restriction that escaped the NSC and Eisenhower Library's examination of contemporary practice.

Authentic NSC material also shows that the organisation had other ways of signalling exceptional sensitivity. The State Department's published *Foreign Relations of the United States* series preserves a May 1954 NSC discussion memorandum — only about two months before the alleged Cutler–Twining memorandum — marked **“top secret / eyes only”**.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1952-54v02p2/d209" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: history.state.gov">[Office of the Historian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">history.state.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Office of the Historian Historical Documents</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Office of the Historian Historical Documents</span></span></span> Another NSC discussion memorandum from December 1954 has the same combination.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1952-54v02p1/d143" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: history.state.gov">[Office of the Historian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">history.state.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Office of the Historian Historical Documents</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Office of the HistorianHistorical Documents - Office of the HistorianDecember 21, 1954...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 21, 1954</span></span></span> Those records are particularly useful comparators because they come from the same administration and the same institutional environment.

The comparison does not prove that every NSC document had to say “Eyes Only”. Government markings were not perfectly uniform, and different document types could follow different procedures. It does show, however, that the NSC had a demonstrable period method for adding a distribution restriction to Top Secret information. Against that evidence, treating the disputed “Restricted” wording as merely another way of saying the same thing requires positive evidence that Cutler's office actually used that formulation in 1954.

## “Restricted Data” does not solve the problem

There is one important exception to the disappearance of the word “Restricted” from the post-1953 security landscape: **[Restricted Data]({{ 'restricted-data/' | relative_url }})**, the statutory category associated with atomic-energy information. Executive Order 10501 itself allowed classifications expressly provided by statute, and nuclear information operated under the Atomic Energy legislation rather than simply becoming a fourth ordinary defence classification.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.presidency.ucsb.edu/documents/executive-order-10501-safeguarding-official-information-the-interests-the-defense-the" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: presidency.ucsb.edu">[The American Presidency Project]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">presidency.ucsb.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The American Presidency ProjectExecutive Order 10501—Safeguarding Official Information in the Interests of the Defense of the United Stat...</span></span></span> Contemporary State Department records describe Restricted Data as a legally defined category covering specified atomic-weapons and nuclear information.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1952-54v02p2/d125" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: history.state.gov">[Office of the Historian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">history.state.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Office of the Historian Historical Documents</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Office of the HistorianHistorical Documents - Office of the HistorianDecember 4, 1953...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 4, 1953</span></span></span>

That exception demonstrates why terminology must be read precisely. Restricted Data could coexist with an overall security classification; modern regulations, reflecting the continuing statutory system, give examples such as “SECRET RD” and require the nuclear-information category to be distinguished from the overall classification level.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/10/1045.140" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: law.cornell.edu">[Legal Information Institute]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">law.cornell.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cornell.edu.</span></span></span>

But the Cutler–Twining issue concerns **“Restricted” or “Restricted Information/Security Information”**, not an identified Restricted Data marking under atomic-energy law. Nothing in the National Archives' assessment treats the disputed wording as an Atomic Energy Act designation. Indeed, the archive's objection is explicitly that the particular NSC marking belongs to a later period.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/research/military/air-force/ufos" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives Project BLUE BOOK</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesProject BLUE BOOK - Unidentified Flying Objects &#124; National ArchivesJune 25, 2024...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 25, 2024</span></span></span> The existence of Restricted Data therefore establishes that “Restricted” did not vanish from every government security context after 1953; it does not authenticate this particular use.

## The wording itself needs to be handled carefully

A further complication is that MJ-12 discussions do not always reproduce the disputed marking identically. The National Archives summarises it as **“Top Secret Restricted Information”**.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/research/military/air-force/ufos" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives Project BLUE BOOK</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesProject BLUE BOOK - Unidentified Flying Objects &#124; National ArchivesJune 25, 2024...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 25, 2024</span></span></span> MJ-12 advocates have argued that the physical memorandum instead separates “TOP SECRET”, “RESTRICTED” and “SECURITY INFORMATION”, and that the Archives' compressed description therefore makes the marking sound more like a single classification title than the document itself does. Contemporary pro-MJ-12 discussion was already making this objection in 1987.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://electronicsandbooks.com/edt/manual/Magazine/M/MUFON%20UFO%20Journal/MUFON%20UFO%20Journal%20-%20November%201987.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: electronicsandbooks.com">[Electronics and Books]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">electronicsandbooks.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">MUFON UFO Journal November 1987</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">MUFON UFO Journal November 1987</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 1987</span></span></span>

Visually separating the terms strengthens the *possibility* of a caveat interpretation: “TOP SECRET” could theoretically be the classification, with “RESTRICTED” supplying an additional instruction. Yet typography cannot establish what the instruction officially meant. “Security Information” was itself terminology inherited from the preceding classification regime; CIA's November 1953 implementation guidance noted that forms containing those words were being changed after Executive Order 10501.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cia.gov/readingroom/document/cia-rdp78-04718a000800290033-8" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cia.gov">[CIA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cia.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">CLASSIFICATION OF DOCUMENTS SAFEGUARDING OFFICIAL INFORMATION IN THE INTERESTS OF THE DEFENSE OF THE UNITED STATES &#124; CIA FOIA (foia.ci...</span></span></span>

The distinction therefore prevents an over-simple criticism — one should not automatically parse every word on the page as a formal classification level — but it does not overturn the archival finding about NSC usage. The question remains institutional rather than grammatical: **did the originating office use that combination in July 1954?**

## What the caveat argument can and cannot establish

The fairest assessment separates three propositions that are sometimes collapsed into one.

**First, could a Top Secret document have additional handling restrictions in 1954?** Definitely. “Eyes Only”, “Special Handling”, “Limited Distribution” and explicit need-to-know controls are all documented in contemporary government records.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1952-54v02p1/d143" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: history.state.gov">[Office of the Historian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">history.state.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Office of the Historian Historical Documents</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Office of the HistorianHistorical Documents - Office of the HistorianDecember 21, 1954...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 21, 1954</span></span></span>

**Second, could the English word “restricted” conceivably have been used descriptively rather than as the abolished Restricted classification?** Yes. Executive Order 10501's abolition of Restricted as a classification does not logically forbid every ordinary-language use of the word. That makes the generic-use defence a genuine interpretive possibility rather than a semantic impossibility.

**Third, does that possibility explain the Cutler–Twining marking well enough to remove the authentication problem?** The available archival evidence says no. The National Archives obtained an NSC finding that the particular marking was not used there until the Nixon administration, while the Eisenhower Presidential Library confirmed its absence during the administration to which the memorandum is attributed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/research/military/air-force/ufos" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives Project BLUE BOOK</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesProject BLUE BOOK - Unidentified Flying Objects &#124; National ArchivesJune 25, 2024...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 25, 2024</span></span></span> Genuine 1954 NSC records meanwhile demonstrate period-appropriate supplementary markings such as “Eyes Only”.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1952-54v02p2/d209" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: history.state.gov">[Office of the Historian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">history.state.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Office of the Historian Historical Documents</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Office of the Historian Historical Documents</span></span></span>

That does not turn one security marking into conclusive proof of fabrication. Historical bureaucracies produced exceptions, inconsistent markings and locally improvised instructions. But authentication works by asking which explanation requires fewer unsupported exceptions. Here, the handling-caveat defence successfully answers the broad claim that **Top Secret could never coexist with another access restriction**. It does not answer the more damaging, office-specific question of why an alleged 1954 NSC memorandum carries terminology that NSC and Eisenhower Library archivists themselves reported was not then in use.

The distinction is therefore important but limited. “Restricted” **could** describe handling rather than classification in the abstract. For the alleged MJ-12 memorandum, however, plausibility at the level of security mechanics is not the same as evidence of period practice. The surviving archival comparators favour familiar restrictions such as “Eyes Only”, “Special Handling” and “Limited Distribution”, while the strongest directly relevant institutional finding places the disputed NSC wording years later.
