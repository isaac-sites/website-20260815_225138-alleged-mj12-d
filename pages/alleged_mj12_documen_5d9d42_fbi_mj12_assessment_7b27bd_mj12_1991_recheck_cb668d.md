## Introduction

The FBI revisited Majestic-12 in 1991 not because it had reversed its earlier judgement that the famous MJ-12 papers were bogus, but because another official-looking copy unexpectedly entered Bureau hands and raised a fresh classified-information question. The Salt Lake City field office initially regarded the material and its security markings as a hoax. Yet contacts with the Department of Defense produced comments that, in the FBI's own striking wording, appeared to “buttress a portion” of the document. Headquarters therefore asked the [Air Force]({{ 'air-force/' | relative_url }}) Office of Special Investigations (OSI) to check its origin and classification status.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://vault.fbi.gov/Majestic%2012/Majestic%2012%20Part%2001%20%28Final%29/at_download/file" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: vault.fbi.gov">[FBI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">vault.fbi.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Majestic 12 Part 1 of 1</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Majestic 12 Part 1 of 1...</span></span></span>

That distinction explains the apparent contradiction. The 1991 recheck was not evidence that the FBI had authenticated MJ-12 or reopened the extraterrestrial claims on their merits. It was a precautionary inquiry into whether a document suspected of being fraudulent nevertheless contained, copied or overlapped with genuinely sensitive material. The FBI's 15 November letter states that responsibility explicitly: possible mishandling of classified data still required investigative attention even when the document itself might be a hoax.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://vault.fbi.gov/Majestic%2012/Majestic%2012%20Part%2001%20%28Final%29/at_download/file" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: vault.fbi.gov">[FBI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">vault.fbi.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Majestic 12 Part 1 of 1</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Majestic 12 Part 1 of 1...</span></span></span>

## The November 1991 FBI letter to Air Force investigators

The immediate episode began in Salt Lake City in October 1991. According to the released FBI file, an individual encountered the document on the floor of the lobby at his place of employment, read it and decided that its contents warranted contacting the Bureau. The copy subsequently reached the Salt Lake City field office. The FBI's surviving record identifies it as a nine-page document dated 18 November 1952 — the date associated with the purported MJ-12 briefing prepared for President-elect Dwight D. Eisenhower.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://vault.fbi.gov/Majestic%2012/Majestic%2012%20Part%2001%20%28Final%29/at_download/file" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: vault.fbi.gov">[FBI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">vault.fbi.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Majestic 12 Part 1 of 1</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Majestic 12 Part 1 of 1...</span></span></span>

The field office was sceptical from the outset. Its internal account says that, considering both the contents and the unusual circumstances in which the papers had been recovered, Salt Lake City initially dismissed the document and its classification markings as a hoax. That is an important detail because it rules out a simple interpretation sometimes encouraged by the existence of the later correspondence: the FBI did **not** receive the document in 1991 and suddenly decide that MJ-12 might be genuine.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://vault.fbi.gov/Majestic%2012/Majestic%2012%20Part%2001%20%28Final%29/at_download/file" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: vault.fbi.gov">[FBI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">vault.fbi.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Majestic 12 Part 1 of 1</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Majestic 12 Part 1 of 1...</span></span></span>

Nevertheless, the office sought to determine whether the Department of Defense had any interest in the material. A synopsis was passed through official channels, and the resulting Department of Defense representations, the FBI airtel recorded, “tended to buttress a portion of the document”. The surviving file does not clearly identify what portion was meant. Nor does that sentence establish that the Department confirmed MJ-12, recovered extraterrestrial spacecraft or the purported 1952 briefing. What it establishes is narrower but historically significant: the response was sufficiently unexpected to move the document beyond the field office's initial dismissal and towards another formal check.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://vault.fbi.gov/Majestic%2012/Majestic%2012%20Part%2001%20%28Final%29/at_download/file" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: vault.fbi.gov">[FBI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">vault.fbi.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Majestic 12 Part 1 of 1</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Majestic 12 Part 1 of 1...</span></span></span>

After discussion with Harry B. Brandon III, then acting within the FBI's Intelligence Division leadership, Salt Lake City forwarded the material to FBI headquarters on 31 October 1991 so that headquarters could determine whether the Department of Defense had an interest in it. The tone remained decidedly sceptical. The author even added an editorial joke that the Halloween date was coincidental and that an April Fool's Day date would have been worse.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://vault.fbi.gov/Majestic%2012/Majestic%2012%20Part%2001%20%28Final%29/at_download/file" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: vault.fbi.gov">[FBI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">vault.fbi.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Majestic 12 Part 1 of 1</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Majestic 12 Part 1 of 1...</span></span></span>

Two weeks later, on **15 November 1991**, Brandon formally wrote to the Commander of the Air Force Office of Special Investigations at Bolling Air Force Base. The subject was not the truth of extraterrestrial visitation but the “possible unauthorized disclosure of classified information” relating to Operation Majestic-12. Brandon explained that the Bureau had recovered what might be a classified 1952 document and asked OSI to determine its originator and/or its current classification.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://vault.fbi.gov/Majestic%2012/Majestic%2012%20Part%2001%20%28Final%29/at_download/file" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: vault.fbi.gov">[FBI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">vault.fbi.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Majestic 12 Part 1 of 1</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Majestic 12 Part 1 of 1...</span></span></span>

The wording of that request is probably the clearest surviving answer to why MJ-12 returned to FBI attention. Brandon acknowledged that the paper might be a hoax while simultaneously stating that incidents involving possible mishandling of classified data had to receive adequate investigative attention. In other words, **suspected forgery did not by itself settle the security question**.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://vault.fbi.gov/Majestic%2012/Majestic%2012%20Part%2001%20%28Final%29/at_download/file" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: vault.fbi.gov">[FBI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">vault.fbi.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Majestic 12 Part 1 of 1</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Majestic 12 Part 1 of 1...</span></span></span>

## Why a suspected hoax still required checking

The FBI faced two logically separate questions in 1991: *Was this document authentic?* and *Did anything in it nevertheless involve real classified information?* A negative answer to the first did not automatically answer the second.

A fabricated document can contain genuine names, terminology or information copied from authentic sources. It can also reproduce classification markings or refer to actual programmes while surrounding them with invented material. That possibility helps explain why the Bureau's field-office scepticism coexisted with a formal referral to OSI. The FBI's letter did not ask the Air Force to investigate extraterrestrials. It specifically requested help identifying the document's originator and determining its current classification.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://vault.fbi.gov/Majestic%2012/Majestic%2012%20Part%2001%20%28Final%29/at_download/file" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: vault.fbi.gov">[FBI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">vault.fbi.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Majestic 12 Part 1 of 1</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Majestic 12 Part 1 of 1...</span></span></span>

The mysterious reference to Department of Defense comments that “buttress[ed]” part of the document needs particular care. The released record does not justify treating the phrase as confirmation of MJ-12. The purported briefing mentioned real historical programmes and subjects alongside its extraordinary claims. The FBI's own 1988 file, for example, noted that Project Blue Book was a real Air Force programme. Thus an official could confirm some peripheral feature of an alleged document without validating the document as a whole.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://vault.fbi.gov/Majestic%2012/Majestic%2012%20Part%2001%20%28Final%29/at_download/file" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: vault.fbi.gov">[FBI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">vault.fbi.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Majestic 12 Part 1 of 1</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Majestic 12 Part 1 of 1...</span></span></span>

There was also an existing archival reason to distinguish isolated factual correspondences from authentication of the MJ-12 story. The US National Archives had undertaken extensive searches of Air Force, Joint Chiefs of Staff, National Security Council, Truman and Eisenhower-era records. Those searches failed to locate the documentary trail that would be expected for the alleged project, apart from the controversial Cutler-[Twining]({{ 'twining/' | relative_url }}) memorandum. The Archives lists substantial anomalies surrounding that memorandum, including its lack of the [register number]({{ 'register-number/' | relative_url }}) expected in its filing series, the absence of supporting MJ-12 documents in its folder and the failure of related record searches to produce corroboration.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/research/military/air-force/ufos" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives Project BLUE BOOK</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesProject BLUE BOOK - Unidentified Flying Objects &#124; National ArchivesJune 25, 2024...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 25, 2024</span></span></span>

That broader documentary picture makes the restrained reading of the 1991 FBI language the strongest one. Something reported through Department of Defense contacts apparently corresponded with part of what the paper said; that was enough to justify checking the document again. It was not enough to establish that its central claims were true.

## How the 1991 episode differed from the 1988 closure

The 1991 inquiry can look like a reversal only if it is detached from what happened three years earlier. In 1988, FBI offices had received versions of the purported Operation Majestic-12 document bearing impressive classification markings. Dallas asked headquarters to determine whether the document was still classified and held further investigation in abeyance while that question was resolved. The surviving file subsequently records that Dallas was advised the document was bogus and that the case should be closed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://vault.fbi.gov/Majestic%2012/Majestic%2012%20Part%2001%20%28Final%29/at_download/file" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: vault.fbi.gov">[FBI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">vault.fbi.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Majestic 12 Part 1 of 1</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Majestic 12 Part 1 of 1...</span></span></span>

The FBI's present-day Vault summary retains that basic institutional judgement: an Air Force investigation determined the MJ-12 document to be fake.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://vault.fbi.gov/search?SearchableText=Majestic" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: vault.fbi.gov">[FBI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">vault.fbi.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">— Federal Bureau of Investigation</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">FBI — Federal Bureau of Investigation...</span></span></span> The 1991 correspondence therefore did not begin on a blank slate. Bureau personnel already had a precedent for treating MJ-12 papers as fraudulent.

What changed was the **event**, rather than the underlying assessment. A new physical copy had turned up through a different route in Salt Lake City. Its discovery created a new potential disclosure incident, and an initial contact with the Department of Defense generated an unexpectedly non-dismissive response concerning at least one part of the material. The procedural consequence was a fresh referral to headquarters and then to Air Force OSI.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://vault.fbi.gov/Majestic%2012/Majestic%2012%20Part%2001%20%28Final%29/at_download/file" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: vault.fbi.gov">[FBI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">vault.fbi.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Majestic 12 Part 1 of 1</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Majestic 12 Part 1 of 1...</span></span></span>

There is another revealing difference in the language. In the 1988 material, the operational endpoint was categorical: the document was bogus and the investigation should close. In the 1991 letter, the FBI instead described the newly recovered document conditionally — it “may” be classified and “may” represent a hoax — while asking the Air Force to establish its origin and classification. Those qualifications reflect uncertainty about the status of the particular copy and its contents, not an FBI endorsement of the MJ-12 narrative.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://vault.fbi.gov/Majestic%2012/Majestic%2012%20Part%2001%20%28Final%29/at_download/file" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: vault.fbi.gov">[FBI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">vault.fbi.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Majestic 12 Part 1 of 1</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Majestic 12 Part 1 of 1...</span></span></span>

## What the recheck actually tells us

The 1991 episode is valuable chiefly because it shows how the FBI handled an apparent classified-document problem after it already had strong reasons to distrust MJ-12. The sequence in the released file is unusually clear: an odd discovery in Salt Lake City; immediate suspicion of a hoax; a Department of Defense contact that unexpectedly appeared to support some unspecified portion; escalation to headquarters; and a 15 November request for Air Force OSI to determine the document's origin and classification.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://vault.fbi.gov/Majestic%2012/Majestic%2012%20Part%2001%20%28Final%29/at_download/file" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: vault.fbi.gov">[FBI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">vault.fbi.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Majestic 12 Part 1 of 1</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Majestic 12 Part 1 of 1...</span></span></span>

It does **not** follow from that sequence that the FBI secretly considered the alleged MJ-12 committee genuine. Indeed, the field office's own Halloween joke is difficult to reconcile with the idea that agents had suddenly become convinced they were handling proof of an extraterrestrial recovery programme. The surviving correspondence instead portrays investigators doing something more mundane: taking security markings seriously enough to verify them despite doubting the document displaying them.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://vault.fbi.gov/Majestic%2012/Majestic%2012%20Part%2001%20%28Final%29/at_download/file" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: vault.fbi.gov">[FBI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">vault.fbi.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Majestic 12 Part 1 of 1</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Majestic 12 Part 1 of 1...</span></span></span>

Nor does the unexplained “buttress” sentence outweigh the larger official record. The National Archives reports extensive unsuccessful searches for corroborating MJ-12 records and identifies multiple archival problems with the related Cutler-Twining memorandum. The FBI's own public description continues to say that the Air Force determined the MJ-12 document to be fake.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/research/military/air-force/ufos" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives Project BLUE BOOK</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesProject BLUE BOOK - Unidentified Flying Objects &#124; National ArchivesJune 25, 2024...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 25, 2024</span></span></span>

The most defensible historical conclusion is therefore narrower — and more interesting — than either extreme. **The FBI did revisit MJ-12 in 1991, but it revisited the status of a newly encountered document, not its earlier conclusion wholesale.** The Bureau's records show that suspicion of a hoax and responsibility for protecting classified information were not mutually exclusive. When another convincing-looking MJ-12 paper surfaced, and a Department of Defense contact gave agents an unexpected reason to check part of it more carefully, the FBI followed the classification question through official channels once again.
