## Introduction

CIA correspondence rules from the early 1950s provide an unusually strict benchmark for judging what a **CIA-originated Top Secret document** was expected to look like. The Agency's surviving correspondence handbook did not merely require a classification stamp. It directed that **each copy of every page** of Top Secret correspondence carry a CIA [Top Secret control]({{ 'control-sheets/' | relative_url }}) number, its stage of preparation and its copy number. It separately required page numbering and prescribed controls for Top Secret enclosures and distribution.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cia.gov/readingroom/document/cia-rdp74-00005r000200060045-8" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cia.gov">[CIA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cia.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">CORRESPONDENCE STYLE &amp; PROCEDURES &#124; CIA FOIA (foia.cia.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">CORRESPONDENCE STYLE &amp; PROCEDURES &#124; CIA FOIA (foia.cia.gov)December 22, 2016...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 22, 2016</span></span></span>



<figure class="article-media-figure article-media-figure-illustration"><img src="{{ "/assets/images/alleged_mj12_documen_5d9d42_control_number_objec_f6faad_cia_control_marking_cc85bb-Illustration-1-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" alt="CIA Rules illustration 1" data-theme-src-dark="{{ "/assets/images/alleged_mj12_documen_5d9d42_control_number_objec_f6faad_cia_control_marking_cc85bb-Illustration-1-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" data-theme-src-light="{{ "/assets/images/alleged_mj12_documen_5d9d42_control_number_objec_f6faad_cia_control_marking_cc85bb-Illustration-1-light.svg" | relative_url }}" width="1920" height="1080" loading="lazy" decoding="async" fetchpriority="low"><figcaption class="article-media-caption"><span class="article-media-caption-label">Explanatory illustration 1</span></figcaption></figure>

That is valuable evidence when examining alleged MJ-12 documents, but only if it is used at the right scale. The CIA handbook establishes a strong benchmark for material actually produced and controlled as CIA Top Secret correspondence. It does **not** establish that every Top Secret paper produced anywhere in the US government had to follow CIA typography and control-number placement. Government-wide rules deliberately left agencies responsible for detailed accountability procedures.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.presidency.ucsb.edu/documents/executive-order-10501-safeguarding-official-information-the-interests-the-defense-the" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: presidency.ucsb.edu">[The American Presidency Project]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">presidency.ucsb.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The American Presidency ProjectExecutive Order 10501—Safeguarding Official Information in the Interests of the Defense of the United Stat...</span></span></span>

The resulting authenticity test is therefore powerful but conditional: first establish which organisation supposedly created and controlled a document; then compare it with that organisation's contemporary procedures.

## CIA requirements for control numbers and copies

The most useful primary source is the CIA's *Correspondence – General* handbook. Its chapter on Top Secret correspondence begins with an unusually emphatic statement: Top Secret information was to be protected from the moment it was prepared, including while still in draft. Clerical and stenographic personnel were instructed to ensure that Top Secret documents entering or leaving their [custody]({{ 'custody/' | relative_url }}) were logged by the relevant Area Top Secret Control Officer.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cia.gov/readingroom/document/cia-rdp74-00005r000200060045-8" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cia.gov">[CIA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cia.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">CORRESPONDENCE STYLE &amp; PROCEDURES &#124; CIA FOIA (foia.cia.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">CORRESPONDENCE STYLE &amp; PROCEDURES &#124; CIA FOIA (foia.cia.gov)December 22, 2016...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 22, 2016</span></span></span>

The handbook then becomes much more specific. Paragraph 106 required each copy of every page of Top Secret correspondence to carry three linked identification elements in the lower-right area:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* a **CIA Top Secret control number**, obtained from the Area Top Secret Control Officer;
* the **stage of preparation**, such as a numbered draft, “Final-A” or a revision;
* a **copy number**, showing the particular copy and the total produced.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cia.gov/readingroom/document/cia-rdp74-00005r000200060045-8" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cia.gov">[CIA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cia.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">CORRESPONDENCE STYLE &amp; PROCEDURES &#124; CIA FOIA (foia.cia.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">CORRESPONDENCE STYLE &amp; PROCEDURES &#124; CIA FOIA (foia.cia.gov)December 22, 2016...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 22, 2016</span></span></span>

</div>

The handbook's model was essentially “CIA TS Control No. 023456 / Final-A / Copy No. 5 of 6”. Crucially, this group was supposed to appear on **every page of each copy**, not merely on a cover sheet. The rules also called for page numbering in a “Page No. … of …” format and prescribed how Top Secret enclosures should be tied to the parent document and identified page by page.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cia.gov/readingroom/docs/CIA-RDP74-00005R000100020013-8.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cia.gov">[CIA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cia.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Approved For Release 2001/07/16: CIA-RDP74-00005R000100020013-8...</span></span></span>

This was therefore an accountability system rather than decorative stationery. A control number linked the physical document to the control machinery; a copy number distinguished one controlled copy from another; preparation markings distinguished drafts, final runs and revisions; and page totals made it easier to detect a missing or substituted sheet. Distribution information on retained CIA copies recorded which numbered copies went to which recipients.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cia.gov/readingroom/document/cia-rdp74-00005r000200060045-8" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cia.gov">[CIA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cia.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">CORRESPONDENCE STYLE &amp; PROCEDURES &#124; CIA FOIA (foia.cia.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">CORRESPONDENCE STYLE &amp; PROCEDURES &#124; CIA FOIA (foia.cia.gov)December 22, 2016...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 22, 2016</span></span></span>

Surviving CIA records show that the underlying control machinery was real rather than an aspirational rule existing only in a manual. A 1953 Top Secret document in the CIA archive carries a signature-record-and-cover sheet whose registry fields include the CIA control number, copy number and number of pages. The form says it was to remain attached to each Top Secret document received by the Agency or classified Top Secret within it until the material was downgraded, destroyed or sent outside the CIA. It also recorded custody and access.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cia.gov/readingroom/document/cia-rdp79b00970a000100010077-8" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cia.gov">[CIA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cia.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">SIGNATURE RECORD AND COVER SHEET &#124; CIA FOIA (foia.cia.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">SIGNATURE RECORD AND COVER SHEET &#124; CIA FOIA (foia.cia.gov)June 30, 1953...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 30, 1953</span></span></span>

Other surviving examples make the pattern especially concrete. A March 1953 record has CIA Control No. 76993, a numbered copy, a page count and the Agency's signature-record cover sheet. An April 1952 Intelligence Advisory Committee paper survives with CIA Control No. 36234-1 and the same type of control sheet. A 1955 example carries CIA Control No. 94984 and identifies itself as Copy No. 3.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cia.gov/readingroom/document/cia-rdp80-01065a000600040115-1" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cia.gov">[CIA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cia.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">MEMORANDUM OF INFORMAL PSB MEETING, 19 MARCH 1953 &#124; CIA FOIA (foia.cia.gov)March 19, 1953...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 19, 1953</span></span></span>

The system was already operating before the dates of the best-known alleged MJ-12 papers. A February 1951 CIA Top Secret record identifies itself as Copy 2 of 3 and carries a CIA control number; other 1951 examples likewise preserve control-sheet fields for source, control number, copy number and pages.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cia.gov/readingroom/document/cia-rdp57-00384r000700010058-2" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cia.gov">[CIA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cia.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">SIGNATURE RECORD AND COVER SHEET &#124; CIA FOIA (foia.cia.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">SIGNATURE RECORD AND COVER SHEET &#124; CIA FOIA (foia.cia.gov</span></span></span>

The surviving record also demonstrates an important distinction between the **document itself** and the Agency's **control apparatus around it**. CIA Form 38-13, the signature record and cover sheet, could be attached to Top Secret material that originated elsewhere after it entered CIA custody. A 1952 Psychological Strategy Board paper, for example, is followed in the CIA archive by the Agency's Top Secret signature-record-and-cover sheet.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cia.gov/readingroom/document/cia-rdp80-01065a000300010037-4" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cia.gov">[CIA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cia.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">TERMS OF REFERENCE FOR SOUTHEAST ASIA PLANNING PANEL &#124; CIA FOIA (foia.cia.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">TERMS OF REFERENCE FOR SOUTHEAST ASIA PLANNING PANEL &#124; CIA FOIA (foia.cia.gov</span></span></span> This matters because a CIA control number on an archival copy does not automatically prove that CIA authored the underlying document.

## Why CIA rules cannot be applied universally

The strictness of the CIA rules makes them tempting as a universal authenticity checklist: no CIA-style control number on every page, therefore not authentic Top Secret. That conclusion goes further than the evidence permits.

The government-wide framework was deliberately broader. Under President Truman's Executive Order 10290, agency heads could issue additional safeguarding instructions required by their own organisations. The order required Top Secret Control Officers to receive, register and dispatch Top Secret information, but it did not prescribe the CIA handbook's exact lower-right-corner combination of control number, preparation stage and copy number on every page throughout the executive branch.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.trumanlibrary.gov/library/executive-orders/10290/executive-order-10290" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: trumanlibrary.gov">[Harry S. Truman Presidential Library]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">trumanlibrary.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">executive order 10290</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Harry S. Truman Presidential LibraryEXECUTIVE ORDER 10290 &#124; Harry S. Truman...</span></span></span>

The distinction continued under President Eisenhower. Executive Order 10501, signed in November 1953 and effective from 15 December, demanded particularly severe accountability for Top Secret material. It required agency heads to prescribe procedures capable of controlling dissemination and called for Top Secret Control Officers to maintain accountability registers and dispatch Top Secret material. But the order again placed responsibility for the detailed accountability procedures on the individual departments and agencies.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.presidency.ucsb.edu/documents/executive-order-10501-safeguarding-official-information-the-interests-the-defense-the" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: presidency.ucsb.edu">[The American Presidency Project]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">presidency.ucsb.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The American Presidency ProjectExecutive Order 10501—Safeguarding Official Information in the Interests of the Defense of the United Stat...</span></span></span>

That structure explains why contemporary records can share the principle of close Top Secret control without looking identical. CIA correspondence could be governed by the Agency's page-by-page rules, while military, [White House]({{ 'white-house/' | relative_url }}), National Security Council or interagency papers passed through different administrative systems. A September 1954 Operations Coordinating Board memorandum preserved in CIA holdings, for example, visibly carries Top Secret markings, a copy-number field, page count and an OCB file number. Its existence in the CIA archive does not turn its OCB formatting into CIA-originated correspondence formatting.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cia.gov/readingroom/docs/CIA-RDP80R01731R003000040001-8.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cia.gov">[CIA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cia.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">CIA RDP80R01731R003000040001 8</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">CIA RDP80R01731R003000040001 8</span></span></span>

Even within CIA holdings, document type matters. A March 1954 *Current Intelligence Bulletin* is visibly marked Top Secret and “Copy No. 84”, while June and July 1954 *Weekly Indochina Reports* similarly show numbered copies. These intelligence publications are useful evidence of copy accountability, but they are not simply ordinary office memoranda and should not be expected to reproduce every feature of a correspondence handbook in precisely the same visual form.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cia.gov/readingroom/document/cia-rdp79t00975a001500050001-9" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cia.gov">[CIA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cia.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">CENTRAL INTELLIGENCE BULLETIN &#124; CIA FOIA (foia.cia.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">CENTRAL INTELLIGENCE BULLETIN &#124; CIA FOIA (foia.cia.gov</span></span></span>

The safest historical rule is consequently **originator first, procedure second**. A document purporting to be an internal CIA Top Secret memorandum should face a much stronger CIA-format test than a document created by the White House and merely received by CIA. Likewise, a document found in CIA's later files should not be assumed to have been created under CIA correspondence rules merely because CIA eventually possessed it.

## What CIA practice can tell us about MJ-12

For [the alleged MJ-12 documents]({{ 'the-alleged-mj-12-documents/' | relative_url }}), CIA procedure is most useful as a **conditional authenticity benchmark**, not as a shortcut to a verdict on the entire collection. The famous alleged 1952 Eisenhower briefing document presents itself as a Top Secret “Eyes Only” briefing prepared for the President-elect and attributes the briefing role to Admiral Roscoe [Hillenkoetter]({{ 'hillenkoetter/' | relative_url }}), a former Director of Central Intelligence. That connection naturally makes genuine intelligence-era paperwork relevant for comparison, but it does not by itself prove that the document was CIA correspondence subject to the handbook's precise formatting rules.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/MJ-12_Document" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: en.wikisource.org">[Wikisource]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">en.wikisource.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">MJ-12 Document</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">MJ-12 Document</span></span></span>

Three different propositions therefore need to be kept separate.

**First, CIA genuinely operated an elaborate Top Secret accountability system.** The handbook, contemporary control sheets and surviving records establish that point strongly. A purported CIA Top Secret document that lacks features explicitly required for its document type has an authenticity problem that deserves explanation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cia.gov/readingroom/document/cia-rdp74-00005r000200060045-8" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cia.gov">[CIA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cia.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">CORRESPONDENCE STYLE &amp; PROCEDURES &#124; CIA FOIA (foia.cia.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">CORRESPONDENCE STYLE &amp; PROCEDURES &#124; CIA FOIA (foia.cia.gov)December 22, 2016...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 22, 2016</span></span></span>

**Second, missing CIA-style markings are strongest evidence when CIA provenance is independently established.** If a disputed paper claims to have been produced as ordinary CIA Top Secret correspondence during a period covered by these procedures, failure to display the expected control number, copy identification or page controls becomes a meaningful discrepancy. The more closely the claimed document type matches the handbook's scope, the more probative that discrepancy becomes.

**Third, CIA procedure cannot legitimately be converted into “all authentic Top Secret documents had CIA [control numbers]({{ 'control-numbers/' | relative_url }}) on every page”.** Executive Orders 10290 and 10501 established stringent government-wide protection and accountability but allowed departments and agencies to implement those obligations through their own procedures.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.trumanlibrary.gov/library/executive-orders/10290/executive-order-10290" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: trumanlibrary.gov">[Harry S. Truman Presidential Library]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">trumanlibrary.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">executive order 10290</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Harry S. Truman Presidential LibraryEXECUTIVE ORDER 10290 &#124; Harry S. Truman...</span></span></span>

That qualification is especially important in the wider MJ-12 control-number debate. The National Archives' objection to the alleged 14 July 1954 Cutler–Twining memorandum is stronger than a generic CIA-format comparison because it is tied to the document's actual archival setting. The Archives reports that the Air Force record series in which the memorandum was found was **filed by Top Secret register number**, yet the disputed memorandum bears no such number. The Archives also reports that searches of related Air Force, Joint Chiefs, [presidential-library]({{ 'library-searches/' | relative_url }}) and National Security Council holdings failed to uncover corroborating MJ-12 documentation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/research/military/air-force/ufos" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives Project BLUE BOOK</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Archives Project BLUE BOOK</span></span></span>

CIA practice helps explain why that kind of evidence matters. Control numbers were not merely visual conventions that a typist might choose to omit. In a tightly controlled system they connected a sensitive document to an administrative record of its existence, copies, movement and custody. CIA's contemporary Form 38-13 even instructed control officers and viewers to record the handling of individual Top Secret documents.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cia.gov/readingroom/document/cia-rdp79b00970a000100010077-8" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cia.gov">[CIA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cia.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">SIGNATURE RECORD AND COVER SHEET &#124; CIA FOIA (foia.cia.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">SIGNATURE RECORD AND COVER SHEET &#124; CIA FOIA (foia.cia.gov)June 30, 1953...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 30, 1953</span></span></span> A missing identifier is therefore most significant when there should also be a corresponding registry trail.

At the same time, the CIA evidence cautions against making the argument broader than the records support. A genuine Top Secret document could enter CIA from another organisation and acquire a CIA control cover sheet; an interagency publication could use its own identifying conventions; and a presidential or military document could be governed by procedures different from those prescribed for CIA correspondence. The mere absence of the exact CIA lower-right-corner format is consequently not proof of fabrication.

## The most useful authenticity test

CIA's page-by-page rules work best as a layered test rather than a binary one. For an alleged MJ-12 document, the first question is **who supposedly originated it?** The second is **what kind of document was it?** The third is **which security and correspondence rules applied to that organisation and document type on the claimed date?** Only then does the question “where is the control number?” acquire its proper evidential weight.

For a purported CIA Top Secret memorandum, contemporary CIA instructions create a demanding benchmark: control through a Top Secret Control Officer, a CIA control number, numbered copies, page accounting and, for correspondence within the handbook's scope, page-by-page identification of the controlled copy and preparation stage. Contemporary CIA records demonstrate that this was an operating administrative system, not merely theoretical guidance.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cia.gov/readingroom/document/cia-rdp74-00005r000200060045-8" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cia.gov">[CIA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cia.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">CORRESPONDENCE STYLE &amp; PROCEDURES &#124; CIA FOIA (foia.cia.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">CORRESPONDENCE STYLE &amp; PROCEDURES &#124; CIA FOIA (foia.cia.gov)December 22, 2016...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 22, 2016</span></span></span>

For a White House, Air Force or interagency paper, however, the relevant benchmark must come from that organisation's rules and records. That is why CIA practice strengthens the broader control-number objection without proving its most sweeping formulation. It shows just how meticulous one major intelligence agency could be about Top Secret paperwork, while the government-wide executive orders show why that agency's detailed implementation cannot simply be projected across the whole federal government.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.trumanlibrary.gov/library/executive-orders/10290/executive-order-10290" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: trumanlibrary.gov">[Harry S. Truman Presidential Library]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">trumanlibrary.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">executive order 10290</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Harry S. Truman Presidential LibraryEXECUTIVE ORDER 10290 &#124; Harry S. Truman...</span></span></span>

The distinction ultimately makes document authentication more rigorous, not less. **“CIA did it this way” is evidence about CIA practice. “This particular office and record system required it this way” is evidence about a particular disputed document.** For the alleged MJ-12 papers, the latter is the stronger test.
