## Introduction

Declassified National Security [Agency records]({{ 'agency-records/' | relative_url }}) show, in unusually concrete form, what intensive Top Secret document control could look like in the mid-1950s. Surviving NSA papers do not merely carry a classification stamp. They can identify a **[Top Secret control]({{ 'control-sheets/' | relative_url }}) number, the particular copy, the total number of copies, the current page and the total page count**. Behind those visible markings sat a formal system of control officers, receipts, custody records and disposition logs.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nsa.gov/portals/75/documents/news-features/declassified-documents/friedman-documents/patent-equipment/FOLDER_143/41726519076582.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nsa.gov">[NSA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nsa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">OFFICIAL REPORT</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">OFFICIAL REPORT - NATIONAL SECURITY AGENCY FIELD COMMANDERS CONFERENCE - 1955...</span></span></span>

That makes the NSA collection useful when assessing objections to [the alleged MJ-12 documents]({{ 'the-alleged-mj-12-documents/' | relative_url }}). It demonstrates that elaborate accountability markings were real period practice, rather than a standard reconstructed retrospectively. But it also supplies an important caution: these are **NSA rules and NSA records**, not proof that every Top Secret paper produced anywhere in the US government had to look the same. The strongest authentication comparisons remain those tied to the originating organisation and document series.

## Visible control numbers in surviving NSA records

One of the clearest examples is the NSA's *Official Report — National Security Agency Field Commanders Conference — 1955*. On an ordinary interior page, beneath the substantive text, the surviving document displays a compact accountability block: Top Secret control number **55-1035**, a copy field showing that the report was produced in a run of **210 copies**, and the notation **“Page 3 of 140 Pages”**. The Top Secret classification is also repeated.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nsa.gov/portals/75/documents/news-features/declassified-documents/friedman-documents/patent-equipment/FOLDER_143/41726519076582.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nsa.gov">[NSA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nsa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">OFFICIAL REPORT</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">OFFICIAL REPORT - NATIONAL SECURITY AGENCY FIELD COMMANDERS CONFERENCE - 1955...</span></span></span>

The significance is not simply that a control number appears. Several different pieces of accountability information occupy the same small area of the page. The document could therefore be identified as a controlled object, an individual copy could be distinguished within the distribution, and a loose page could be recognised as belonging to a particular 140-page document. The surviving paper itself carried information useful for reconstructing what had been issued and what might be missing.

A 1954 NSA-related memorandum provides an even more compact example. A memorandum concerning communications-intelligence relations, dated 5 November 1954, bears **Top Secret Control Number 54-3083**, identifies a particular copy out of **48 copies**, and marks the sheet as **Page 1 of 1 Pages**. An attached draft retains corresponding control, copy and pagination information.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nsa.gov/portals/75/documents/news-features/declassified-documents/friedman-documents/panel-committee-board/FOLDER_333/41746089078529.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nsa.gov">[NSA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nsa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">COMINT RELATIONS, 5 NOVEMBER 1954; MEMORANDUM FOR THE MEMBERS OF USCIB...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: NOVEMBER 1954</span></span></span>

Another surviving 1954 document, a British Joint Services Mission memorandum to the Director of the NSA, shows the same basic pattern: **Top Secret Control Number 54-591(C)**, a copy designation within a run of **44 copies**, and **Page 1 of 1 Pages**.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nsa.gov/portals/75/documents/news-features/declassified-documents/friedman-documents/panel-committee-board/FOLDER_503/41785929082494.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nsa.gov">[NSA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nsa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">PROGRAM FOR THE IMPROVEMENT OF ALLIED (NATO) COMMUNICATIONS SECURITYMarch 28, 2018...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 28, 2018</span></span></span> The examples differ in origin, subject and length, but the repeated control-number/copy/page pattern makes clear that these markings were functioning administrative devices rather than decorative additions to especially important-looking documents.

There are earlier examples as well. A surviving NSA directive from 1953 carries **Top Secret Control Number 53-2**, identifies its copy within a run of **133 copies**, and numbers the sheet within a **32-page** document.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nsa.gov/portals/75/documents/news-features/declassified-documents/nsa-60th-timeline/1950s/19530101_1950_Doc_3978795_NSADirective1.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nsa.gov">[NSA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nsa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">s__.............. -...April 13, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 13, 2026</span></span></span> That helps establish continuity: the visible accountability pattern was not peculiar to a single 1955 conference report.

## The markings reflected an actual control system

The strongest evidence comes from comparing those documents with NSA Regulation No. 121-7, *Security of Classified Information and Material*, dated 8 April 1955. The regulation is especially valuable because it explains what the markings were supposed to accomplish.

For written classified material generally, NSA required the appropriate classification to appear conspicuously on **all pages of the original and all copies**, normally at the top and bottom. Reproductions were to receive the same appropriate markings as the original.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nsa.gov/portals/75/documents/news-features/declassified-documents/friedman-documents/reports-research/FOLDER_059/41711419075073.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nsa.gov">[NSA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nsa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NSA REGULATION NUMBER 121-7 AND ANNEX. SECURITY OF CLASSIFIED INFORMATION AND MATERIAL...</span></span></span>

For Top Secret material, the rules became much more specific. NSA Regulation 121-7 stated that Top Secret material received or produced by an NSA element, subject to stated exceptions, was to be controlled by a number. That number was to be displayed on bound-document covers and title pages or conspicuously on other material. The number normally incorporated two digits indicating the year in which the material was received or originated — exactly the sort of **53-, 54- and 55-** prefixes visible on surviving documents.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nsa.gov/portals/75/documents/news-features/declassified-documents/friedman-documents/reports-research/FOLDER_059/41711419075073.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nsa.gov">[NSA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nsa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NSA REGULATION NUMBER 121-7 AND ANNEX. SECURITY OF CLASSIFIED INFORMATION AND MATERIAL...</span></span></span>

The same regulation prescribed copy and page accounting. For letters, memoranda, bulletins, orders, contracts and written material generally, **every page** was to carry a block containing the NSA Top Secret control number, copy number and a “Page ___ of ___ Pages” entry. Bound documents had corresponding control, copy and total-page information on their cover and title page.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nsa.gov/portals/75/documents/news-features/declassified-documents/friedman-documents/reports-research/FOLDER_059/41711419075073.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nsa.gov">[NSA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nsa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NSA REGULATION NUMBER 121-7 AND ANNEX. SECURITY OF CLASSIFIED INFORMATION AND MATERIAL...</span></span></span>

This is unusually strong historical evidence because rule and artefact agree. The 1955 conference report's **55-1035 / copy of 210 / page of 140** block looks like the surviving implementation of the procedure described in the April 1955 regulation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nsa.gov/portals/75/documents/news-features/declassified-documents/friedman-documents/patent-equipment/FOLDER_143/41726519076582.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nsa.gov">[NSA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nsa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">OFFICIAL REPORT</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">OFFICIAL REPORT - NATIONAL SECURITY AGENCY FIELD COMMANDERS CONFERENCE - 1955...</span></span></span>

There were exceptions even inside this tightly controlled system. Regulation 121-7 explicitly said that messages and documents already assigned certain other [control numbers]({{ 'control-numbers/' | relative_url }}) did not also require an NSA Top Secret control number.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nsa.gov/portals/75/documents/news-features/declassified-documents/friedman-documents/reports-research/FOLDER_059/41711419075073.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nsa.gov">[NSA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nsa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NSA REGULATION NUMBER 121-7 AND ANNEX. SECURITY OF CLASSIFIED INFORMATION AND MATERIAL...</span></span></span> That qualification matters when using the NSA material as authentication evidence: even an organisation with unusually explicit rules did not reduce every possible document type to one universal marking formula.

## Copy and page accounting went beyond the printed footer

The visible numbers were only the outward face of a larger accountability system. NSA Regulation 121-7 assigned responsibility for the agency's Top Secret Control Program to the Adjutant General and required an Agency Top Secret Control Officer. Chiefs of staff divisions and offices were likewise to appoint Top Secret control officers and alternates for their activities.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nsa.gov/portals/75/documents/news-features/declassified-documents/friedman-documents/reports-research/FOLDER_059/41711419075073.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nsa.gov">[NSA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nsa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NSA REGULATION NUMBER 121-7 AND ANNEX. SECURITY OF CLASSIFIED INFORMATION AND MATERIAL...</span></span></span>

Those officers were responsible for receiving, holding, accounting for and distributing Top Secret material. The regulation instructed them to maintain records of Top Secret material received, dispatched or destroyed and, importantly, records sufficient to indicate **who was responsible for its [custody]({{ 'custody/' | relative_url }}) at a given time**. It also called, where possible, for continuous receipts and periodic inventories.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nsa.gov/portals/75/documents/news-features/declassified-documents/friedman-documents/reports-research/FOLDER_059/41711419075073.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nsa.gov">[NSA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nsa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NSA REGULATION NUMBER 121-7 AND ANNEX. SECURITY OF CLASSIFIED INFORMATION AND MATERIAL...</span></span></span>

The NSA's central Top Secret Control Office was instructed to receive and check incoming material, issue receipts, record it, assign Top Secret control numbers, maintain records of disposition, transmit it to authorised recipients and handle material returned for filing or destruction. Separate control points within staff divisions and offices performed comparable functions locally.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nsa.gov/portals/75/documents/news-features/declassified-documents/friedman-documents/reports-research/FOLDER_059/41711419075073.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nsa.gov">[NSA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nsa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NSA REGULATION NUMBER 121-7 AND ANNEX. SECURITY OF CLASSIFIED INFORMATION AND MATERIAL...</span></span></span>

Copy numbers therefore had an operational purpose. The agency's records were to include the control number, **number of copies, enough information to identify the material, the disposition of each copy and destruction certificates for destroyed material**. Top Secret documents were to remain under a receipt system, and even material addressed for opening only by a particular person was brought into the accountability machinery rather than simply bypassing it.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nsa.gov/portals/75/documents/news-features/declassified-documents/friedman-documents/reports-research/FOLDER_059/41711419075073.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nsa.gov">[NSA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nsa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NSA REGULATION NUMBER 121-7 AND ANNEX. SECURITY OF CLASSIFIED INFORMATION AND MATERIAL...</span></span></span>

That is the deeper lesson of the surviving NSA pages. “Control number” can sound like a minor formatting convention when an alleged document is examined decades later. In the NSA system it represented part of a chain of accountability linking a physical copy to administrative records, custody and eventual disposition.

The procedure extended to movement outside an office. Top Secret material transmitted within the agency through its messenger system had to be wrapped, sealed, appropriately marked, assigned a control number and receipted by the messenger. Material taken to a conference was to be **logged out** through the appropriate control point; if custody was transferred permanently, a receipt was required.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nsa.gov/portals/75/documents/news-features/declassified-documents/friedman-documents/reports-research/FOLDER_059/41711419075073.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nsa.gov">[NSA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nsa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NSA REGULATION NUMBER 121-7 AND ANNEX. SECURITY OF CLASSIFIED INFORMATION AND MATERIAL...</span></span></span>

This helps explain why copy and page markings were valuable. A document might travel through offices, messenger systems or conferences, and copies might ultimately be filed or destroyed. Numbering the document, the copy and its constituent pages provided several overlapping ways to detect a break in that chain.

## NSA practice was stricter than a generic Top Secret stamp

The NSA records also put the broader federal rules into perspective. President Truman's Executive Order 10290 required agency heads to designate Top Secret Control Officers who would **receive, maintain registers of and dispatch** Top Secret security information. It also prescribed unusually protected methods for transmitting Top Secret information.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.trumanlibrary.gov/library/executive-orders/10290/executive-order-10290" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: trumanlibrary.gov">[Truman Library]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">trumanlibrary.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">executive order 10290</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Truman LibraryEXECUTIVE ORDER 10290 &#124; Harry S. TrumanSeptember 24, 1951...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: September 24, 1951</span></span></span>

NSA turned that government-wide principle into detailed agency procedure. Its 1955 regulation specified control points, numbering conventions, page and copy markings, receipts, custody records, inventories and disposition information. It even prescribed the physical format of Top Secret control markings.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nsa.gov/portals/75/documents/news-features/declassified-documents/friedman-documents/reports-research/FOLDER_059/41711419075073.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nsa.gov">[NSA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nsa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NSA REGULATION NUMBER 121-7 AND ANNEX. SECURITY OF CLASSIFIED INFORMATION AND MATERIAL...</span></span></span>

NSA was not alone in imposing detailed internal requirements. Contemporary CIA correspondence guidance likewise instructed that each copy of every page of Top Secret correspondence carry a CIA Top Secret control number, preparation-stage information and its copy number, with separate pagination requirements.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cia.gov/readingroom/document/cia-rdp74-00005r000200060045-8" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cia.gov">[CIA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cia.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">CORRESPONDENCE STYLE &amp; PROCEDURES &#124; CIA FOIA (foia.cia.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">CORRESPONDENCE STYLE &amp; PROCEDURES &#124; CIA FOIA (foia.cia.gov)December 22, 2016...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 22, 2016</span></span></span> A surviving CIA Top Secret cover sheet from 1951 similarly records a CIA control number, **“2 of 3”** copy information, page count and fields intended to record custody and access.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cia.gov/readingroom/document/cia-rdp57-00384r000700010058-2" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cia.gov">[CIA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cia.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">SIGNATURE RECORD AND COVER SHEET &#124; CIA FOIA (foia.cia.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">SIGNATURE RECORD AND COVER SHEET &#124; CIA FOIA (foia.cia.gov)February 21, 1951...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: February 21, 1951</span></span></span>

These parallels strengthen the modest historical proposition: **intensive individual accountability for Top Secret paperwork was unquestionably a real feature of early Cold War security administration**. NSA's records are particularly useful because the agency has preserved both the procedural instructions and documents visibly implementing them.

## Why the NSA examples are useful but not universal

For the alleged MJ-12 documents, these NSA records defeat one weak response to control-number objections: the suggestion that detailed numbering and copy accounting were somehow not characteristic of genuine 1950s Top Secret paperwork. They plainly were characteristic of at least some important organisations and document classes. A genuine NSA memorandum subject to the April 1955 rules could be expected to carry a remarkably specific administrative footprint.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nsa.gov/portals/75/documents/news-features/declassified-documents/friedman-documents/reports-research/FOLDER_059/41711419075073.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nsa.gov">[NSA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nsa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NSA REGULATION NUMBER 121-7 AND ANNEX. SECURITY OF CLASSIFIED INFORMATION AND MATERIAL...</span></span></span>

But the opposite overstatement is equally problematic. The NSA evidence does **not** establish a government-wide rule that every authentic Top Secret sheet had to possess an NSA-style control block. Executive Order 10290 mandated Top Secret control officers and registers, but NSA's detailed formatting requirements came from its own implementing regulations. NSA Regulation 121-7 itself contained exceptions, including provision for documents carrying other control numbers.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.trumanlibrary.gov/library/executive-orders/10290/executive-order-10290" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: trumanlibrary.gov">[Truman Library]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">trumanlibrary.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">executive order 10290</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Truman LibraryEXECUTIVE ORDER 10290 &#124; Harry S. TrumanSeptember 24, 1951...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: September 24, 1951</span></span></span>

The appropriate use of these records is therefore comparative rather than absolute. If an alleged document claims to be an NSA product from a period covered by a known NSA regulation, the regulation and surviving NSA documents provide a strong baseline against which its markings can be checked. If the alleged paper instead purports to come from the [White House]({{ 'white-house/' | relative_url }}), an [Air Force]({{ 'air-force/' | relative_url }}) office or another agency, NSA practice becomes contextual evidence rather than a dispositive rule.

That distinction is particularly important in the MJ-12 control-number dispute. The most persuasive objection is not simply that “real Top Secret documents had numbers”. The NSA collection shows why such a statement is too broad. The stronger method is to establish **which organisation controlled the document, which procedure governed that type of document at that date, and whether surviving records from the same administrative setting display the expected accountability features**.

NSA's declassified papers make that approach tangible. On genuine surviving documents, Top Secret control could leave a conspicuous trail: **year-coded control number, individually accounted-for copy, complete page count, repeated classification markings, control officers, receipts, custody records and disposition records**.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nsa.gov/portals/75/documents/news-features/declassified-documents/friedman-documents/patent-equipment/FOLDER_143/41726519076582.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nsa.gov">[NSA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nsa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">OFFICIAL REPORT</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">OFFICIAL REPORT - NATIONAL SECURITY AGENCY FIELD COMMANDERS CONFERENCE - 1955...</span></span></span> For alleged MJ-12 material, that is a valuable benchmark — not a universal authenticity formula, but a documented example of just how systematic 1950s Top Secret accountability could be.
