## Introduction

The Cantwheel source story cannot presently be verified in the way historians would normally verify the provenance of sensitive government records. According to Timothy Cooper and the researchers who later promoted his papers, an elderly man using the name Thomas Cryll “Cy” Cantwheel supplied a substantial run of alleged intelligence documents between roughly 1992 and 1996. Cooper said that Cantwheel was a retired Army counter-intelligence officer, met him once in person in July 1995, and came to believe that he was responsible for documents appearing in Cooper's post-office box.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://majesticdocuments.com/documents/document-sources/timothy-s-cooper/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: majesticdocuments.com">[Majestic Documents]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">majesticdocuments.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Majestic Documents Timothy S. Cooper &#124; Majestic Documents</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Majestic DocumentsTimothy S. Cooper &#124; Majestic DocumentsNovember 14, 2018...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 14, 2018</span></span></span>

The central problem is not simply that “Cantwheel” may have been an alias. It is that the evidential chain stops with Cooper. No independently established service identity, personnel file, archival accession trail or government-held counterpart has been produced that connects the alleged source to the documents attributed to him. Even Robert and Ryan Wood's Majestic Documents project, which argues strongly for the papers' authenticity, calls provenance its “weakest link” and acknowledges that it cannot prove that Cooper received the material from his mailbox as described.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://majesticdocuments.com/documents/document-authentication/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: majesticdocuments.com">[Majestic Documents]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">majesticdocuments.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Majestic Documents Document Authentication &#124; Majestic Documents</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Majestic Documents Document Authentication &#124; Majestic Documents</span></span></span>

## Who Cantwheel was said to be

The fullest account of Cantwheel's identity comes from Cooper himself. In a report dated 26 February 1996, Cooper described “Thomas Cantwheel” as a cover name and portrayed its user as an elderly former intelligence officer. Cooper's report said Cantwheel had entered the US Army before the Second World War, joined the Counter Intelligence Corps (CIC) around 1940, and served with an alleged Interplanetary Phenomenon Unit, or IPU. It further attributed to him CIA covert work during the 1960s, contacts with the FBI and Soviet intelligence services, retirement as a lieutenant colonel and knowledge of an alleged “Majestic Community”.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://majesticdocuments.com/documents/document-sources/thomas-cryll-cy-cantwheel/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: majesticdocuments.com">[Majestic Documents]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">majesticdocuments.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Majestic Documents Thomas Cryll “Cy” Cantwheel &#124; Majestic Documents</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Majestic DocumentsThomas Cryll “Cy” Cantwheel &#124; Majestic DocumentsFebruary 26, 1996...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: February 26, 1996</span></span></span>

These are strikingly specific biographical claims, but their source is circular for authentication purposes: they are principally things Cantwheel allegedly told Cooper, subsequently recorded by Cooper. Cooper's report itself describes the man's whereabouts as unknown and gives his age only as an estimate. It therefore does not function like an independently compiled personnel investigation in which military records, dates of assignment and identity documents establish that a particular officer existed and occupied the claimed posts.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://majesticdocuments.com/pdf/cantwheel_report.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: majesticdocuments.com">[Majestic Documents]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">majesticdocuments.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Majestic Documents</span></span></span>

The story became more concrete through Cooper's account of a single meeting. He said he met Cantwheel on 16 July 1995 at Pine Knot Landing in Big Bear Lake, California. A contemporary UFO newsletter summary of Cooper's account placed the meeting at about 1 am and said that Cantwheel acknowledged being the source of the documents. Cooper later made a notarised statement denying that he had invented either Cantwheel or this meeting.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.geocities.ws/server2/homestead/capecanaveral/4737/oksi/free_nl/article4_5.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: geocities.ws">[Geocities]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">geocities.ws</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Skywatch International, Inc</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Skywatch International, Inc - Skywatcher NewsletterMarch 31, 1999...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 31, 1999</span></span></span>

That affidavit is relevant evidence about what Cooper was willing formally to assert, but notarisation should not be confused with independent corroboration. A notary establishes matters such as the identity of the signer and execution of a statement; it does not establish that the events described in the statement occurred. Significantly, Cooper's 1999 declaration said that efforts were still being made to identify Cantwheel and establish his true identity and credentials.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://inquiry201.rssing.com/chan-16157765/all_p1.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: inquiry201.rssing.com">[Skeptical Inquirer]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">inquiry201.rssing.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Skeptical InquirerSkeptical Inquirer - Committee for Skeptical Inquiry...</span></span></span>

This leaves an unusual asymmetry. The alleged source was given an elaborate intelligence biography, yet the publicly available case for that biography remained dependent overwhelmingly on the recipient of the documents. No comparable evidence has surfaced showing an independently identified former CIC officer saying, in effect, “I am the man Cooper called Cantwheel, these are my service records, and I supplied these particular papers.”

## What documents were attributed to him

Cantwheel matters because this was not an attribution attached to one stray memorandum. The Majestic Documents catalogue assigns **22 items** to “Cantwheel — Retired Army Counterintelligence (1993–1996)”. They include purported memoranda associated with George C. Marshall, Roscoe [Hillenkoetter]({{ 'hillenkoetter/' | relative_url }}) and Dwight Eisenhower; an alleged flying-saucer analytical report; several retyped papers; a purported MJ-12 annual report; an alleged CIC/IPU intelligence assessment; an IPU field order; two Kennedy-era CIA papers; and a document headed as a personal communication from Cantwheel to Cooper.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://majesticdocuments.com/documents/document-sources/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: majesticdocuments.com">[Majestic Documents]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">majesticdocuments.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Majestic Documents Document Sources &#124; Majestic Documents</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Majestic Documents Document Sources &#124; Majestic Documents</span></span></span>

The form in which these papers supposedly travelled is important. A 1999 paper by Robert and Ryan Wood described thirteen document sets totalling 59 pages as copies left in Cooper's post-office box on five occasions between 1992 and 1996. Their later accounting was more specific about some items: it described the Hillenkoetter memorandum, Kennedy documents, IPU field order and nine shorter memoranda as having come from “Cantwell” through the postal system, while listing a seven-page IPU report and a 17-page “First Annual Report” as received personally from him in July 1995.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://majesticdocuments.com/pdf/anotherlookatmajestic.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: majesticdocuments.com">[Majestic Documents]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">majesticdocuments.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Majestic DocumentsMUFON UFO JournalMarch 1999Page 11Another look at MajesticBy Dr. Robert M. Wood and Ryan S. WoodThe hunt for a genuine...</span></span></span>

That distinction actually sharpens the provenance question. The claimed source history is not simply “anonymous papers arrived in the mail”. Cooper said that he eventually met the person responsible and personally obtained significant material from him. If Cantwheel's identity and intelligence career could have been independently established, that meeting could potentially have supplied a much stronger provenance bridge than an anonymous mailing. Instead, the identity behind the cover name remained unresolved.

A further complication arrived in June 1996, when another group of documents was attributed to “Salina”, described as Cantwheel's daughter. The Majestic Documents catalogue treats this as a separate source stream and lists fourteen items under her name. Its own description of a handwritten note says the Woods doubted that either “Salina” or “Cantwell” was a real name.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://majesticdocuments.com/documents/document-sources/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: majesticdocuments.com">[Majestic Documents]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">majesticdocuments.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Majestic Documents Document Sources &#124; Majestic Documents</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Majestic Documents Document Sources &#124; Majestic Documents</span></span></span>

This is why references to the Cantwheel material need careful wording. The catalogue establishes that researchers **attributed** more than twenty papers to Cantwheel. It does not establish that an independently identified former Army intelligence officer created, possessed or transmitted those records.

## The IPU trail provides context, not identification

One feature of Cantwheel's story has more external support than the man's identity: the name “Interplanetary Phenomenon Unit” appeared in Freedom of Information Act correspondence. This point has sometimes been used to lend plausibility to Cantwheel's claimed Army background, but it needs to be kept in proportion.

A long-running FOIA investigation reproduced Army Intelligence and Security Command correspondence stating that an IPU had been disestablished in the late 1950s and that its records had supposedly passed to the US [Air Force]({{ 'air-force/' | relative_url }}) Office of Special Investigations in connection with Project Blue Book. Yet the same investigation reported that repeated requests produced **no actual IPU records**. It says more than 20 requests and follow-up letters generated over 40 pieces of correspondence without recovering the unit's files. The National Archives reportedly found no IPU references in the Project Blue Book records it searched, while AFOSI did not produce the supposed transferred collection.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cufon.org/cufon/foiasop.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cufon.org">[Cufon]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cufon.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">US Army INSCOM FOIA &quot;Standard Operating procedures&quot; Document</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">US Army INSCOM FOIA &quot;Standard Operating procedures&quot; Document</span></span></span>

There is therefore a meaningful but limited distinction. The FOIA correspondence provides evidence that the Army used the IPU name in responding to researchers. It does not establish Cantwheel's claimed membership in such a unit, much less authenticate the extraordinary history attributed to that unit in the [Cooper papers]({{ 'cooper-papers/' | relative_url }}). Nor does the mere existence of an obscure intelligence-unit name prove that a document containing that name originated within the unit.

That limitation is especially important because Cooper himself had pursued FOIA material concerning the IPU. In a 1999 communication, he said he had filed an Army request years earlier and had received hundreds of pages of CIC material. Thus knowledge of obscure military terminology in papers reaching Cooper cannot automatically be treated as information that only Cantwheel could have possessed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://rense.com/ufo2/hovnew.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: rense.com">[Rense]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">rense.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on rense.com.</span></span></span>

## Where the chain of custody remains unproven

A strong provenance chain would normally allow an investigator to work backwards from the surviving document: from the present holder, to the person who transferred it, to the government office or personal papers in which it had previously been held, and ideally to contemporaneous filing or distribution records. The Cantwheel papers do not offer that sequence.

Instead, the publicly documented chain is essentially:

**alleged government document → unidentified holder using “Cantwheel” → Cooper → later researchers.**

The missing portion is the most important one: the connection between the historical government offices named in the documents and the unidentified man who allegedly supplied them.

The Majestic Documents project explicitly recognises this weakness. Its authentication discussion lists provenance among its criteria but states that provenance is its weakest area because it cannot prove that Cooper received the documents from his mailbox in the manner he described. The same page also acknowledges, in discussing the material then under consideration, that it did not have the government originals needed to close the authentication problem.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://majesticdocuments.com/documents/document-authentication/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: majesticdocuments.com">[Majestic Documents]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">majesticdocuments.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Majestic Documents Document Authentication &#124; Majestic Documents</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Majestic Documents Document Authentication &#124; Majestic Documents</span></span></span>

Some physical testing cited by the Woods can answer narrower questions. Their 2001 paper reported that the seven-page IPU document had been produced on a type-bar machine and that the reproduction was too poor for identification of the particular typewriter; the examiner reported finding nothing in that test that disproved the stated July 1947 date.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.majesticdocuments.com/pdf/rmwood_mufon2001.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: majesticdocuments.com">[Majestic Documents]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">majesticdocuments.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">rmwood mufon2001</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Majestic DocumentsMUFON 2001 paper.PDF...</span></span></span> That is not the same proposition as proving the document was typed in 1947. A technology being compatible with an alleged date establishes a possible production window, not authorship, official [custody]({{ 'custody/' | relative_url }}) or the date on which the surviving copy was actually produced.

This distinction is crucial to the whole Cantwheel question. A document can use period-compatible typography, names and terminology while still lacking provenance. Conversely, a demonstrably old sheet of paper does not reveal who typed on it. For a collection whose extraordinary significance rests on secret-government origins, source history is not a secondary technicality: it is part of the evidence required to establish those origins.

## The source story also accumulated internal warning signs

By the late 1990s, critics were raising objections that went beyond the absence of a personnel file for Cantwheel. Timothy Good, who had previously been sympathetic to MJ-12 claims, argued that features of Cooper's earlier correspondence resembled typing characteristics in some later documents. A contemporary report of Good's presentation also alleged similarities in formatting and raised questions about material apparently copied from records to which Cooper had access.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://rense.com/ufo4/TimGoodTimCooper_u.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: rense.com">[Rense]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">rense.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on rense.com.</span></span></span>

Ryan Wood disputed the stronger inference. He replied that a questioned-document examiner had not established that Cooper's own machine produced the disputed material, challenged the suggestion that all of the documents shared the same formatting, and said handwriting specialists regarded Cooper's and Salina's writing as different. Importantly, however, Wood conceded that the typewriter resemblance was suspicious and allowed that the possibility Cooper had fabricated an individual document remained open, while rejecting the idea that he had created the whole collection.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://rense.com/ufo4/TimGoodTimCooper_u.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: rense.com">[Rense]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">rense.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on rense.com.</span></span></span>

Other contemporary sceptical analysis identified apparent historical and textual problems within documents attributed to the Cooper stream. Philip Klass, for example, argued that the supposed “First Annual Report” contained a Flight 19 quotation that entered popular Bermuda Triangle lore only years after the report's supposed 1952 composition. He also reported similar typewriter defects across documents supposedly produced in different settings. These claims concern document authenticity rather than Cantwheel's biography directly, but they matter to provenance because the source story is supposed to explain how such papers entered Cooper's possession.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://inquiry201.rssing.com/chan-16157765/all_p1.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: inquiry201.rssing.com">[Skeptical Inquirer]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">inquiry201.rssing.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Skeptical InquirerSkeptical Inquirer - Committee for Skeptical Inquiry...</span></span></span>

None of these observations by itself establishes who produced the papers. That restraint is important. Evidence that a document contains anachronisms or resembles Cooper's typing does not logically prove that Cooper forged it, just as Cooper's sworn denial does not prove that Cantwheel existed as described. The defensible conclusion is narrower: the unresolved source identity prevents the Cantwheel story from resolving disputes raised by the documents themselves.

## Can the Cantwheel source story be verified?

On the currently available public evidence, **not independently**. There is evidence that Cooper told a consistent core story: documents appeared in his post-office box; an elderly man using the Cantwheel name claimed responsibility; Cooper said he met him on 16 July 1995; and Cooper later formally denied inventing either the man or the encounter. The Woods accepted Cooper as basically honest and considered the scale and variety of the material an argument against wholesale fabrication by him.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://majesticdocuments.com/documents/document-sources/timothy-s-cooper/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: majesticdocuments.com">[Majestic Documents]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">majesticdocuments.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Majestic Documents Timothy S. Cooper &#124; Majestic Documents</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Majestic DocumentsTimothy S. Cooper &#124; Majestic DocumentsNovember 14, 2018...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 14, 2018</span></span></span>

But credibility of a witness and provenance of an archive are different questions. Even if Cooper accurately reported meeting an elderly man calling himself Cantwheel, that would not by itself demonstrate that the man had been a CIC lieutenant colonel, belonged to the alleged IPU, possessed authorised access to the historical records in question, or obtained those records through an authentic government chain of custody.

The evidence that would materially change the assessment is straightforward: identification of Cantwheel's real name; independently obtainable military or intelligence personnel records matching his claimed career; corroboration from colleagues or family that does not derive from the Cooper source stream; surviving correspondence or postal evidence securely connecting him with the deliveries; or matching copies of the alleged documents found in independently accessioned government archives.

Until evidence of that kind appears, “Cantwheel” is best treated as an **asserted source attribution**, not an established archival provenance. That does not, by itself, prove every Cantwheel-attributed paper false. It does mean that the source story cannot carry the burden of authenticating them. Each document has to stand on independently testable evidence, while the crucial bridge between Cooper's post-office box and the government offices supposedly responsible for the papers remains missing.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://majesticdocuments.com/documents/document-authentication/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: majesticdocuments.com">[Majestic Documents]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">majesticdocuments.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Majestic Documents Document Authentication &#124; Majestic Documents</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Majestic Documents Document Authentication &#124; Majestic Documents</span></span></span>
