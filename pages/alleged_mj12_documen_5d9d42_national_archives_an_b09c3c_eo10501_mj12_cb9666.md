## Introduction

The classification language on the alleged 14 July 1954 Cutler–[Twining]({{ 'twining/' | relative_url }}) MJ-12 memorandum is difficult to reconcile with the rules President Dwight D. Eisenhower had put into force seven months earlier. Executive Order 10501, issued in November 1953 and effective from 15 December, reduced ordinary national-defence classification to three levels: **Top Secret, Secret and Confidential**. It expressly barred other designations for classifying defence information unless a statute provided otherwise.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.presidency.ucsb.edu/documents/executive-order-10501-safeguarding-official-information-the-interests-the-defense-the" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: presidency.ucsb.edu">[presidency.ucsb.edu]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">presidency.ucsb.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Executive Order 10501—Safeguarding Official Information in the Interests of the Defense of the United States &#124; The American Presidency Pr...</span></span></span>

## What Executive Order 10501 changed

Eisenhower signed Executive Order 10501 on 5 November 1953. Its opening section is unusually useful for assessing the MJ-12 memorandum because it states the permitted classification vocabulary directly. Information requiring protection in the interests of national defence was to be limited to three categories, in descending order: **Top Secret, Secret and Confidential**. The order added that no other designation was to be used to classify defence information, including military information, except where a statute expressly provided otherwise.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.presidency.ucsb.edu/documents/executive-order-10501-safeguarding-official-information-the-interests-the-defense-the" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: presidency.ucsb.edu">[presidency.ucsb.edu]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">presidency.ucsb.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Executive Order 10501—Safeguarding Official Information in the Interests of the Defense of the United States &#124; The American Presidency Pr...</span></span></span>

This was not an obscure rule that remained on paper while agencies continued unchanged. Contemporary implementation records show the change being transmitted into the defence establishment. A November 1953 disposition form preserved in declassified government records states that Executive Order 10501 had eliminated both the **“RESTRICTED” classification** and the “SECURITY INFORMATION” notation, effective 15 December 1953.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nsa.gov/portals/75/documents/news-features/declassified-documents/friedman-documents/panel-committee-board/FOLDER_380/41764429080355.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nsa.gov">[National Security Agency]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nsa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Security AgencyPUBLIC LAW 513 (13 MAY 1950)March 28, 2018 — By- Executive Order 10501~- dated 6 November 1953, the President has...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 28, 2018</span></span></span> A May 1954 Federal Register notice likewise described Department of Defense Directive 5200.1 of 19 November 1953 as having eliminated “Restricted” from the classification system.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-1954-05-19/pdf/FR-1954-05-19.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: govinfo.gov">[GovInfo]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">govinfo.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">AGRICULTURAL CREDIT CONTENTS... The timing is especially important. The Cutler–Twining memorandum is dated **14 July 1954**. It therefore purports to come from roughly seven months after the new system took effect, not from a transitional week immediately following the change. Someone preparing a genuine highly sensitive National Security Council communication at that point would be operating under an established Eisenhower classification order whose permitted ordinary classifications were explicitly defined.

The National Archives' own assessment fits this regulatory chronology. Its MJ-12 reference report records that the NSC Freedom of Information Office said **“Top Secret Restricted Information”** was a marking that did not come into use at the National Security Council until the Nixon administration.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/research/military/air-force/ufos" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives Project BLUE BOOK</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesProject BLUE BOOK - Unidentified Flying Objects &#124; National ArchivesJune 25, 2024...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 25, 2024</span></span></span> That finding is stronger when read alongside Executive Order 10501: the questionable wording does not simply differ from a sample of surviving NSC documents; it conflicts with the classification framework governing the period.

## Why ordinary “Restricted” had disappeared

There is an important historical distinction hidden inside the word “restricted”. Before Executive Order 10501, **Restricted** had existed as an ordinary security-classification level. Eisenhower's order eliminated that level. A historical study of US classification policy summarises the change in exactly those terms: EO 10501 retained Top Secret, Secret and Confidential while eliminating the Restricted classification level.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://sgp.fas.org/library/quist/chap_3.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sgp.fas.org">[FAS Project on Government Secrecy]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sgp.fas.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">chap 3</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">In November 1953 President Eisenhower replaced EO... That creates a straightforward historical test for the alleged MJ-12 memorandum. If “RESTRICTED INFORMATION” on the sheet was intended as an ordinary defence classification, it looks obsolete for July 1954. The document would effectively be combining the valid **Top Secret** level with terminology that the administration had just abolished from that classification system.

This does not mean that the word “restricted” vanished from every government document or every security context. Agencies could use handling and control terminology that was not itself a classification level, and Executive Order 10501 explicitly preserved classifications created by statute. The crucial question is therefore not whether the word *restricted* could appear anywhere in 1954 government paperwork. It plainly could. The question is whether **“Top Secret Restricted Information”** makes sense as the security marking of this purported NSC memorandum. The archival finding and the governing order both weigh against that interpretation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.presidency.ucsb.edu/documents/executive-order-10501-safeguarding-official-information-the-interests-the-defense-the" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: presidency.ucsb.edu">[presidency.ucsb.edu]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">presidency.ucsb.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Executive Order 10501—Safeguarding Official Information in the Interests of the Defense of the United States &#124; The American Presidency Pr...</span></span></span>

## Could “Restricted Data” explain the wording?

The strongest potential qualification is the special nuclear category **[Restricted Data]({{ 'restricted-data/' | relative_url }})**. Executive Order 10501 itself anticipated this issue. Section 13 declared that nothing in the order superseded requirements under the Atomic Energy Act of 1946 and specified that “Restricted Data”, as defined by that statute, would continue to be handled under Atomic Energy Act rules and Atomic Energy Commission regulations.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.presidency.ucsb.edu/documents/executive-order-10501-safeguarding-official-information-the-interests-the-defense-the" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: presidency.ucsb.edu">[presidency.ucsb.edu]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">presidency.ucsb.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Executive Order 10501—Safeguarding Official Information in the Interests of the Defense of the United States &#124; The American Presidency Pr...</span></span></span>

Restricted Data was therefore not the abolished fourth rung beneath Confidential. It was a separate, statutory category concerned with atomic information. The Atomic Energy Act of 1946 had created it for information concerning such matters as atomic weapons and fissionable material. Contemporary State Department records describe Restricted Data explicitly as a **special category of security information** created by the Atomic Energy Act.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1952-54v02p2/d125" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: history.state.gov">[Office of the Historian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">history.state.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Office of the Historian Historical Documents</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Office of the Historian Historical Documents</span></span></span>

That distinction is crucial because a reader might otherwise reason that “Top Secret Restricted” simply meant an exceptionally sensitive combination of classifications. In the post-10501 system, however, the concepts operated differently. **Top Secret** identified a classification level. **Restricted Data** identified a statutory category of atomic information to which special rules applied. Later government marking guidance illustrates the structure clearly: material can be Top Secret Restricted Data, Secret Restricted Data or Confidential Restricted Data, with the Restricted Data category accompanying rather than replacing the classification level.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gao.gov/assets/a250725.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gao.gov">[GAO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gao.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on gao.gov.</span></span></span>

So the Atomic Energy Act exception does not automatically rescue the MJ-12 wording. The alleged memorandum does not present itself simply as **Top Secret — Restricted Data** under an identifiable atomic-information regime. NARA identifies its marking as **“Top Secret Restricted Information”**, and reports that the NSC itself regarded that formulation as one that did not come into use there until the Nixon years.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/research/military/air-force/ufos" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives Project BLUE BOOK</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesProject BLUE BOOK - Unidentified Flying Objects &#124; National ArchivesJune 25, 2024...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 25, 2024</span></span></span>

There is also a chronological wrinkle worth keeping precise. The memorandum is dated 14 July 1954, while the new Atomic Energy Act of **1954** was enacted on 30 August. On the memorandum's purported date, the statutory Restricted Data regime nevertheless already existed under the **1946** Act, which Executive Order 10501 expressly preserved.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.presidency.ucsb.edu/documents/executive-order-10501-safeguarding-official-information-the-interests-the-defense-the" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: presidency.ucsb.edu">[presidency.ucsb.edu]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">presidency.ucsb.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Executive Order 10501—Safeguarding Official Information in the Interests of the Defense of the United States &#124; The American Presidency Pr...</span></span></span> Thus it would be wrong to argue that no Restricted Data category existed in July 1954. The problem is narrower: the existence of statutory Restricted Data does not establish that the MJ-12 sheet's particular “Restricted Information” marking was correct NSC usage.

## The memo fails a useful period-language test

Executive Order 10501 is valuable in the MJ-12 dispute because it supplies a benchmark independent of arguments about UFOs, witnesses or alleged secret programmes. Authentication can partly be tested through administrative language: does a document dated July 1954 use security terminology that officials were instructed to use in July 1954?

On that test, the Cutler–Twining sheet encounters several related difficulties. Ordinary Restricted classification had been eliminated effective December 1953; the surviving statutory exception was the specifically defined **Restricted Data** system; and NARA reports that the NSC considered the memorandum's actual formulation, “Top Secret Restricted Information”, an anachronism for the Eisenhower administration.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://media.defense.gov/2021/Jul/20/2002806676/-1/-1/0/19531120_1950_DOC_3993991_PRESIDENTIAL.PDF" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: media.defense.gov">[defense.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">media.defense.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">19531120 1950 DOC 3993991 PRESIDENTIAL</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">U.S. Department of WarDISPOSITION FORMCol.Geo.E.Campbell/60235/. - 1. By Executive Order 10501~ dated 6 Nov$1Dber 1953, the President has...</span></span></span>

The distinction also helps avoid overstating the evidence. Executive Order 10501 does **not**, standing alone, prove who created the Cutler–Twining memorandum or when the physical sheet entered [Air Force]({{ 'air-force/' | relative_url }}) files. Nor does an anomalous classification marking logically prove forgery by itself. Genuine documents can contain clerical errors, unconventional markings or irregular handling.

But the classification problem is harder to dismiss as merely unconventional because it aligns with the broader archival anomaly. The National Archives found the memorandum in an Air Force series filed by [Top Secret register]({{ 'register-numbers/' | relative_url }}) number, yet the sheet itself has no such number; searches of related Defence, Joint Chiefs, Air Force and [NSC records]({{ 'nsc-records/' | relative_url }}) produced no corroborating MJ-12 documentation; and the Archives separately obtained the adverse finding about its security marking.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/research/military/air-force/ufos" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives Project BLUE BOOK</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesProject BLUE BOOK - Unidentified Flying Objects &#124; National ArchivesJune 25, 2024...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 25, 2024</span></span></span>

Executive Order 10501 therefore adds a specific historical reason for caution. The issue is not simply that modern researchers think the wording “looks wrong”. Eisenhower's own classification policy had already established what ordinary defence classifications were supposed to be, and contemporary implementation documents show that **Restricted** had been deliberately removed. Against that documented 1954 framework, “TOP SECRET RESTRICTED INFORMATION” becomes another feature of the alleged MJ-12 memorandum that requires an exceptional explanation rather than supporting its authenticity.
