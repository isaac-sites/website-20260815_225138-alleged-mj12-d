## Introduction

The fact that Robert Cutler was abroad on 14 July 1954 is one of the clearest problems with the alleged Cutler–[Twining]({{ 'twining/' | relative_url }}) MJ-12 memorandum, but it is not quite the automatic disproof it can appear to be. Archival records cited by the US National Archives place Cutler on an official tour of military installations in Europe and North Africa from 3 to 15 July. The disputed memorandum is dated 14 July and carries his typed name and title. Cutler therefore was not in Washington when the paper was supposedly produced.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/research/military/air-force/ufos" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives Project BLUE BOOK</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesProject BLUE BOOK - Unidentified Flying Objects &#124; National ArchivesJune 25, 2024</span></span></span>



<figure class="article-media-figure article-media-figure-illustration"><img src="{{ "/assets/images/alleged_mj12_documen_5d9d42_anomaly_vs_falsifica_7cdc03_cutler_absence_autho_7cf721-Illustration-1-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" alt="Cutler Abroad illustration 1" data-theme-src-dark="{{ "/assets/images/alleged_mj12_documen_5d9d42_anomaly_vs_falsifica_7cdc03_cutler_absence_autho_7cf721-Illustration-1-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" data-theme-src-light="{{ "/assets/images/alleged_mj12_documen_5d9d42_anomaly_vs_falsifica_7cdc03_cutler_absence_autho_7cf721-Illustration-1-light.svg" | relative_url }}" width="1920" height="1080" loading="lazy" decoding="async" fetchpriority="low"><figcaption class="article-media-caption"><span class="article-media-caption-label">Explanatory illustration 1</span></figcaption></figure>

That matters because Cutler had explicitly delegated National Security Council (NSC) administrative business during his absence. Yet it also exposes the limit of the argument: the surviving MJ-12 paper is unsigned. Physical absence can rule out Cutler personally typing or signing a Washington document that day; it cannot, by itself, rule out a subordinate preparing an unsigned memorandum in his name. The stronger question is whether the document behaves as an authentic piece of delegated NSC correspondence should.

## Where Cutler was on 14 July 1954

There is little serious room for dispute about Cutler's absence. According to the National Archives' MJ-12 reference report, President Dwight D. Eisenhower had sent Cutler to inspect overseas military installations. A memorandum and report preserved in Eisenhower's presidential papers, dated 20 July 1954 after Cutler's return, records visits to installations in Europe and North Africa between 3 and 15 July. The alleged memorandum to General Nathan Twining falls almost at the end of that period, on 14 July.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/research/military/air-force/ufos" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives Project BLUE BOOK</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesProject BLUE BOOK - Unidentified Flying Objects &#124; National ArchivesJune 25, 2024</span></span></span>

The chronology is strengthened by another contemporary record. Before leaving, Cutler wrote a 3 July memorandum to two NSC subordinates, Executive Secretary James S. Lay Jr and J. Patrick Coyne, giving them instructions for handling administrative matters while he was away. The National Archives consequently regarded Cutler's whereabouts not as an inference reconstructed from a vague itinerary but as an absence documented in the records of the office itself.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/research/military/air-force/ufos" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives Project BLUE BOOK</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesProject BLUE BOOK - Unidentified Flying Objects &#124; National ArchivesJune 25, 2024</span></span></span>

This creates an immediate authenticity problem for the Cutler–Twining memorandum. A document dated in Washington during an official's recorded overseas absence deserves an explanation, especially when it appears under that official's name. But precisely what the absence proves depends on what is being claimed about authorship. A typed name at the foot of an unsigned office memorandum does not necessarily establish that the named official personally composed, dictated, approved or dispatched it.

That distinction is particularly important here because the alleged document does **not** contain Cutler's handwritten signature. MJ-12 defender [Stanton Friedman]({{ 'friedman-s-case/' | relative_url }}) made this a central part of his rebuttal: Cutler could not have signed a Washington memorandum while overseas, but staff remaining at the NSC could conceivably have produced routine correspondence for his office. Friedman pointed specifically to Cutler's instructions to Lay as evidence that business was supposed to continue during the trip.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://medium.com/stanton-friedmans-articles/update-on-operation-majestic-12-documents-ed2df58fd8a1" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: medium.com">[Medium]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">medium.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UPDATE ON OPERATION MAJESTIC 12 DOCUMENTS &#124; by Richard Geldreich, Jr. &#124; Physicist Stanton Friedman’s Articles &#124; MediumJune 28, 2024...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 28, 2024</span></span></span>

## Who was handling NSC business in his absence

The archival evidence actually makes the delegated-authorship hypothesis both more plausible in principle and more difficult in practice.

On the plausible side, James S. Lay was no incidental clerk. He was the NSC's Executive Secretary, a senior institutional figure whose tenure in that position ran from 1950 to 1961. Contemporary NSC material routinely shows Lay transmitting memoranda and Council business. Cutler's decision to leave administrative instructions with Lay and Coyne therefore establishes a genuine mechanism through which work could continue while Cutler was overseas.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1952-54v02p1/d117" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: history.state.gov">[Office of the Historian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">history.state.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">22, Executive Secretary of the National Security Council James S. Lay, Jr., transmitted a memorandum to the NSC which stated...Read more...</span></span></span>

That is enough to defeat the simplest version of the argument: **“Cutler was in Europe, therefore no genuine memorandum connected with Cutler's office could have been produced in Washington.”** Government offices do not stop operating whenever their principals travel. Staff prepare correspondence, circulate decisions and handle delegated business.

The National Archives nevertheless drew a more specific inference. Given Cutler's 3 July instructions, its reference report observed that if the Twining memorandum were genuine, one would expect Lay or Coyne to have signed it. That is an expectation about administrative practice rather than proof of physical impossibility. The wording matters: the archival finding supplies a reason for suspicion, not evidence that delegation could never occur in another form.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/research/military/air-force/ufos" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives Project BLUE BOOK</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesProject BLUE BOOK - Unidentified Flying Objects &#124; National ArchivesJune 25, 2024</span></span></span>

A particularly useful counterpoint comes from sceptic Philip J. Klass. Writing in *Skeptical Inquirer*, Klass reported that the Eisenhower Library held another Top Secret memorandum dated **14 July 1954** that Lay sent to the Secretary of Defense and the chairman of the Atomic Energy Commission under Lay's own name. He argued that this contemporary example weakened Friedman's proposal that Lay might simply have issued the MJ-12 memorandum under Cutler's name.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://skepticalinquirer.org/wp-content/uploads/sites/29/2019/03/Issue-03-9.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: skepticalinquirer.org">[Skeptical Inquirer]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">skepticalinquirer.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Skeptical InquirerSkeptical InquirerJune 26, 2006 —... President Eisenhower&#x27;s special assistant, Robert Cutler. Moore and... written by...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 26, 2006</span></span></span>

That comparison is more probative than Cutler's absence alone. It asks not merely whether Lay *could* transact business during Cutler's trip, but how Lay actually identified himself in classified correspondence on the very day in question. Still, one example cannot establish an exceptionless rule. To turn the point into a conclusive falsifier would require showing that NSC practice required delegated correspondence of this kind to bear Lay's or Coyne's name and that no authorised alternative existed.

## Why suspicious circumstances are not impossibility

The Cutler problem is best understood by separating three propositions that are sometimes treated as though they were identical.

**Cutler was outside the United States on 14 July.** This is strongly documented. His post-trip report covers Europe and North Africa from 3 to 15 July, and his pre-departure instructions confirm that he expected subordinates to handle NSC administration during his absence.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/research/military/air-force/ufos" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives Project BLUE BOOK</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesProject BLUE BOOK - Unidentified Flying Objects &#124; National ArchivesJune 25, 2024</span></span></span>

**Cutler therefore did not personally sign the alleged memorandum in Washington that day.** That conclusion is straightforward, but the surviving disputed paper is unsigned. There is no Cutler signature whose physical execution on 14 July needs explaining. Friedman correctly identified this narrow logical limitation in the absence argument, irrespective of whether his broader case for MJ-12 authenticity is persuasive.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://medium.com/stanton-friedmans-articles/update-on-operation-majestic-12-documents-ed2df58fd8a1" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: medium.com">[Medium]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">medium.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UPDATE ON OPERATION MAJESTIC 12 DOCUMENTS &#124; by Richard Geldreich, Jr. &#124; Physicist Stanton Friedman’s Articles &#124; MediumJune 28, 2024...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 28, 2024</span></span></span>

**The memorandum therefore cannot be genuine.** This does not follow from absence alone. A staff member could theoretically have prepared an unsigned paper carrying the principal's typed name, perhaps pursuant to earlier instructions or a decision communicated while the principal was away. Friedman's proposed candidate was Lay, and he further argued that Lay was in contact with Eisenhower during Cutler's absence. Those claims offer a possible administrative mechanism, but possibility is not evidence that this is what happened.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.theblackvault.com/casefiles/update-on-operation-majestic-12-documents/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theblackvault.com">[The Black Vault]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theblackvault.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Black Vault Update on Operation Majestic 12 Documents</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Black VaultUpdate on Operation Majestic 12 Documents - The Black Vault Case Files...</span></span></span>

The distinction cuts both ways. MJ-12 advocates cannot authenticate the document merely by showing that delegated authorship was conceivable. Once the normal-looking chain has been disrupted — the named official was abroad, the paper is unsigned, and contemporary archival investigators expected a subordinate to have signed in his place — the burden shifts towards finding positive evidence of who prepared or authorised the memorandum. No such provenance is supplied by Cutler's absence itself.

Nor does the fact that Lay was conducting genuine NSC business on 14 July solve the problem. Indeed, Klass's same-day comparison sharpens it: Lay demonstrably produced classified correspondence under his own name while Cutler was away. That does not make a Cutler-name memorandum impossible, but it means the proposed staff-authorship explanation requires an additional exception rather than following automatically from known office practice.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://skepticalinquirer.org/wp-content/uploads/sites/29/2019/03/Issue-03-9.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: skepticalinquirer.org">[Skeptical Inquirer]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">skepticalinquirer.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Skeptical InquirerSkeptical InquirerJune 26, 2006 —... ## What the absence actually proves

For evaluating [the alleged MJ-12 documents]({{ 'the-alleged-mj-12-documents/' | relative_url }}), Cutler's whereabouts should therefore be treated as **strong adverse evidence rather than a standalone falsification test**. The archival chronology securely establishes an awkward fact that any authenticity theory must explain. It also identifies the people — principally Lay and Coyne — through whom a genuine NSC administrative memorandum might have been generated in Cutler's absence.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/research/military/air-force/ufos" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives Project BLUE BOOK</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesProject BLUE BOOK - Unidentified Flying Objects &#124; National ArchivesJune 25, 2024</span></span></span>

The National Archives' interpretation is the more suspicious one: Cutler was overseas, had formally arranged for subordinates to handle business, and an authentic memorandum would therefore be expected to show their involvement. The same-day Lay memorandum reported by Klass adds concrete support for expecting Lay to use his own name.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://skepticalinquirer.org/wp-content/uploads/sites/29/2019/03/Issue-03-9.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: skepticalinquirer.org">[Skeptical Inquirer]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">skepticalinquirer.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Skeptical InquirerSkeptical InquirerJune 26, 2006 —... The opposing interpretation identifies a real but narrower point. An unsigned carbon or office copy cannot safely be treated as proof that the person whose name appears at the bottom personally authored or signed it. Government correspondence can be staff-produced, and Cutler had unquestionably delegated work before leaving. Thus, “Cutler could not have written this in Washington” is considerably stronger than “Cutler's office could not have issued anything like this while he was away”.

That distinction is important because document authentication is strongest when it separates **impossibility** from **improbability**. Had the disputed memorandum carried a demonstrably contemporaneous handwritten Cutler signature executed in Washington on 14 July, his documented presence overseas would pose a much harder contradiction. Instead, the actual anomaly is administrative: an unsigned memorandum bearing the absent official's name survives without the staff signature that archivists expected and without evidence identifying a substitute author.

Cutler's absence consequently deepens the authenticity problem without resolving it on its own. It narrows the range of plausible explanations and makes personal authorship highly implausible, but it leaves staff preparation logically open. The decisive issue is not simply where Robert Cutler was; it is whether the disputed paper has independent evidence connecting it to the people and procedures that were actually running NSC business while he was gone.
