## Introduction

The strongest version of the government-style objection to the alleged MJ-12 Eisenhower Briefing Document is narrower than is sometimes claimed. Federal printing guidance did recognise a standard military date form: **15 April 1951**, with the day before the month and no comma before the year. The MJ-12 paper's recurring form — for example, **“18 November, 1952”** — therefore departs from a documented convention.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/GOVPUB-GP-26106c7aa879602e0a802c3c3f4466cc/pdf/GOVPUB-GP-26106c7aa879602e0a802c3c3f4466cc.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: govinfo.gov">[GovInfo]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">govinfo.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">GOVPUB GP 26106c7aa879602e0a802c3c3f4466cc</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">United States Government Printing Office style manualApril 11, 2022...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 11, 2022</span></span></span>



<figure class="article-media-figure article-media-figure-illustration"><img src="{{ "/assets/images/alleged_mj12_documen_5d9d42_mj12_date_formats_57_934abc_gov_date_rules_pract_71faa6-Illustration-1-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" alt="Style Rules illustration 1" data-theme-src-dark="{{ "/assets/images/alleged_mj12_documen_5d9d42_mj12_date_formats_57_934abc_gov_date_rules_pract_71faa6-Illustration-1-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" data-theme-src-light="{{ "/assets/images/alleged_mj12_documen_5d9d42_mj12_date_formats_57_934abc_gov_date_rules_pract_71faa6-Illustration-1-light.svg" | relative_url }}" width="1920" height="1080" loading="lazy" decoding="async" fetchpriority="low"><figcaption class="article-media-caption"><span class="article-media-caption-label">Explanatory illustration 1</span></figcaption></figure>

What does not follow is that every genuine federal memorandum, intelligence paper or piece of correspondence had to obey that convention. The Government Printing Office (GPO) Style Manual was fundamentally a system for standardising **government printing**, not proof that every typewriter in every federal office produced identical dates. Period records preserve several date styles, and the GPO's own production system allowed originating agencies and copy instructions to affect what appeared in print. The MJ-12 date format is consequently useful as an anomaly, but much weaker as a stand-alone authenticity test.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/GPO-STYLEMANUAL-2008/html/GPO-STYLEMANUAL-2008-1.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: govinfo.gov">[GovInfo]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">govinfo.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">GPO STYLEMANUAL</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">U.S. Government Printing Office Style Manual - Title Page, Style Board, Extract from Title 44, U.S.C., About This Manual, GPO&#x27;s On...</span></span></span>

## What the Government Printing Office actually prescribed

There is no serious reason to deny that the federal government had formal style conventions. The GPO had issued style manuals for decades before the alleged MJ-12 briefing was supposedly prepared in November 1952; catalogues record editions in 1939, 1945 and 1953, among many others. The underlying statutory authority went back to an 1895 act empowering the Public Printer to determine the form and style in which departmental printing was executed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://catalog.hathitrust.org/Record/001161302" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: catalog.hathitrust.org">[HathiTrust]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">catalog.hathitrust.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Catalog Record: Style manual &#124; HathiTrust Digital LibraryGovernment Printing Office. Published Washington, 1953. Edition. Hathi...</span></span></span>

The relevant date rule is particularly clear. GPO editions identify **“15 April 1951”** as the military form. In the same guidance, ordinary dates appear in the familiar American order, such as **“June 29, 1935”**. The distinction matters: the military example is not “15 April, 1951”. There is no comma separating the month from the year.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/GOVPUB-GP-40fcd66dc28fe70156f8390dee4baa81/pdf/GOVPUB-GP-40fcd66dc28fe70156f8390dee4baa81.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: govinfo.gov">[GovInfo]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">govinfo.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">GOVPUB GP 40fcd66dc28fe70156f8390dee4baa81</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">United States Government Printing Office style manualApril 11, 2022...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 11, 2022</span></span></span>

That gives the MJ-12 criticism a genuine documentary foundation. The alleged briefing combines the military day-month-year sequence with a comma before the year, producing constructions such as “18 November, 1952”. Its text also contains dates such as “01 August, 1950”, adding a leading zero to a single-digit day. Whatever judgement is ultimately made about the documents, those constructions should not be described as straightforward examples of the GPO's published military-date convention.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/MJ-12_Document" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: en.wikisource.org">[Wikisource]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">en.wikisource.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">MJ-12 Document</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">MJ-12 Document - Wikisource, the free online library...</span></span></span>

The important governance question, however, is **what kind of rule this was**. The statutory authority concerned the form and style in which **printing or binding ordered by a department** was executed. The modern GPO explanation of the manual, which describes the same institutional tradition, calls it a standardisation device intended to produce uniform treatment and economical preparation of manuscripts. It explicitly describes the manual as primarily a printer's stylebook and warns against treating its rules as rigid in every circumstance.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/GPO-STYLEMANUAL-2008/html/GPO-STYLEMANUAL-2008-1.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: govinfo.gov">[GovInfo]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">govinfo.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">GPO STYLEMANUAL</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">U.S. Government Printing Office Style Manual - Title Page, Style Board, Extract from Title 44, U.S.C., About This Manual, GPO&#x27;s On...</span></span></span>

That distinction is easy to lose in an authenticity debate. A rule saying how government copy should be prepared or typeset by the federal printing establishment is evidence of the preferred standard. It is not automatically evidence that every memorandum typed within the [White House]({{ 'white-house/' | relative_url }}), Pentagon, State Department or intelligence community was invalid, corrected or impossible if a secretary used another form.

## Why printing rules differed from office practice

The surviving documentary record shows why a simple “government rule equals universal office practice” model is inadequate. Federal paperwork was generated through different administrative channels: formally printed publications, letters, memoranda, telegrams, minutes, drafts, transmittal papers and internal working documents did not all originate through one central typesetting process.

The GPO's own procedures reveal this flexibility. Its instructions distinguished between ordinary copy and material that was to be followed as supplied. In versions of the manual, copy marked to be followed literally could preserve the requisitioning agency's capitalisation, punctuation, abbreviations, figures and other features — even obvious errors. Other copy received different levels of editorial intervention.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/GOVPUB-GP-aecea18c00346f3195b54b86db9c4532/pdf/GOVPUB-GP-aecea18c00346f3195b54b86db9c4532.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: govinfo.gov">[GovInfo]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">govinfo.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">GOVPUB GP aecea18c00346f3195b54b86db9c4532</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">United States Government Printing Office style manual...</span></span></span>

This is an important institutional point. The existence of a house style tells an investigator what a document **ought normally to look like after a particular editorial process**. It does not establish that all documents passed through that process. Nor does it prove that originating offices always prepared their material in perfect conformity before submission. Indeed, GPO advice repeatedly told authors and departmental editors to prepare copy carefully precisely because manuscripts arrived from elsewhere and production changes cost time and money.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://device.report/m/8b6aa7cab86eb66e9a1f5b9fa792f6a3fc97f3d292ac4f8722a898bf0cb3bbcd" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: device.report">[Device Report]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">device.report</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Report Preliminary-cloth.indd PDF document</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Report Preliminary-cloth.indd PDF document</span></span></span>

The wider federal record also demonstrates multiple date conventions in actual use. A particularly useful comparison comes from records dated on or around the alleged MJ-12 briefing date itself. The State Department's official *Foreign Relations of the United States* collection reproduces a Top Secret 18 November 1952 letter from Secretary of Defense Robert Lovett to Secretary of State Dean Acheson with the dateline **“Washington, 18 November 1952.”** That closely matches the prescribed military sequence and omits the disputed comma.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://history.state.gov//historicaldocuments/frus1952-54v15p1/d330" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: history.state.gov">[Office of the Historian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">history.state.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Office of the Historian Historical Documents</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Office of the Historian Historical Documents</span></span></span>

Yet another Top Secret record from exactly the same date — a memorandum from Joint Chiefs of Staff chairman Omar Bradley to Lovett — is presented in the same official historical collection as **“Washington, November 18, 1952.”** CIA-derived records in that collection likewise use month-day-year dating.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://history.state.gov//historicaldocuments/frus1951-54Iran/d145fn4" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: history.state.gov">[Office of the Historian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">history.state.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Office of the Historian Historical Documents</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Office of the Historian Historical Documents</span></span></span>

This is not evidence that punctuation was arbitrary. It is evidence that **“the government date format” was not a single universal format governing every federal document category**. Civilian month-day-year and military day-month-year forms coexisted within high-level national-security paperwork. The GPO itself recognised both constructions: its rules supplied ordinary month-day-year examples alongside the specifically labelled military form.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/GOVPUB-GP-40fcd66dc28fe70156f8390dee4baa81/pdf/GOVPUB-GP-40fcd66dc28fe70156f8390dee4baa81.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: govinfo.gov">[GovInfo]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">govinfo.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">GOVPUB GP 40fcd66dc28fe70156f8390dee4baa81</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">United States Government Printing Office style manualApril 11, 2022...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 11, 2022</span></span></span>

The pattern continues into the Eisenhower administration. Officially published State Department records from November 1956 include Top Secret memoranda and White House conference records dated **“Washington, November 20, 1956”** and **“Washington, November 21, 1956”**. One White House record was drafted by Colonel Andrew Goodpaster, Eisenhower's staff secretary. These examples are useful because they show that high classification and proximity to the President did not by themselves impose the GPO military day-month-year convention on every document.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1955-57v16/ch3subch1?start=61" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: history.state.gov">[Office of the Historian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">history.state.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Office of the Historian Historical Documents</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Office of the Historian Historical Documents</span></span></span>

CIA records reinforce the need to distinguish guidance from practice. A later CIA handbook for historical writing expressly called day-month-year — giving **“23 June 1953”** as its example — the **preferred** usage and referred writers to the GPO Style Manual except where otherwise specified. That wording is revealing: an agency could adopt a preferred convention while still needing its own instructions about how and where the convention applied.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cia.gov/readingroom/document/cia-rdp81s00991r000200060007-9" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cia.gov">[CIA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cia.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cia.gov.</span></span></span>

## The MJ-12 format remains unusual

Recognising variation does not make every date construction equally ordinary. This is where the strongest form of the sceptical argument survives.

Philip J. Klass focused not merely on the existence of day-month-year dates, which are unquestionably authentic period usage, but on the **hybrid** “18 November, 1952” construction and the [leading zeros]({{ 'leading-zeros/' | relative_url }}) in dates such as “01 August, 1950”. He later reported examining roughly two dozen letters written by Roscoe [Hillenkoetter]({{ 'hillenkoetter/' | relative_url }}) while Hillenkoetter was Director of Central Intelligence and said he found none using the briefing document's hybrid format.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://centerforinquiry.s3.amazonaws.com/wp-content/uploads/sites/29/2022/09/26195532/SUN-72-Summer-2002.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: centerforinquiry.s3.amazonaws.com">[Center for Inquiry]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">centerforinquiry.s3.amazonaws.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Center for Inquiry</span></span></span>

Barry Greenwood subsequently made a related archival argument. In his account of the controversy, he reported that a survey of hundreds of pages of late-1940s and early-1950s government material found the comma after the month in day-month-year dates only rarely, with the few examples concentrated in material associated with one [Air Force]({{ 'air-force/' | relative_url }}) intelligence source. Greenwood therefore treated the MJ-12 construction as an abnormality rather than claiming simply that federal documents never varied.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.theufochronicles.com/2014/06/appendix-the-myth-of-mj-12-annotated.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theufochronicles.com">[The UFO Chronicles]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theufochronicles.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on theufochronicles.com.</span></span></span>

That is a more defensible evidential formulation. A rare deviation can matter, particularly when it recurs throughout a disputed document and when the expected convention is independently documented. But rarity is a probabilistic observation, not a prohibition.

There is also direct evidence that non-standard punctuation could enter real intelligence paperwork. A CIA archival PDF associated with a November 1952 dispatch is indexed with a date rendered as **“18 November:1952”**, apparently reflecting unconventional punctuation in the underlying record or its reproduction. The precise mark is less important than the broader lesson: surviving office records are not typographically pristine implementations of a central stylebook.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cia.gov/readingroom/docs/HOETTL%2C%20WILHELM%20%20%20VOL.%205_0133.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cia.gov">[CIA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cia.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">HOETTL, WILHELM VOL.</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">SPECIFY AIR OR SEA POUCH) DISPATCH NO.October 21, 2024...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 21, 2024</span></span></span>

That does not provide a match for the MJ-12 format, and it should not be presented as one. It simply demonstrates why document examination must distinguish **prescribed form**, **common practice**, **rare practice** and **impossible practice**. Those are four different claims.

## What noncompliance means for the MJ-12 objection

The GPO evidence supports a meaningful but limited inference. If an alleged 1952 military-style briefing repeatedly uses “18 November, 1952”, an examiner is entitled to ask why it does not use the documented military construction “18 November 1952”. The unusual comma, and especially its repetition, can properly contribute to a cumulative authenticity assessment.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/GOVPUB-GP-26106c7aa879602e0a802c3c3f4466cc/pdf/GOVPUB-GP-26106c7aa879602e0a802c3c3f4466cc.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: govinfo.gov">[GovInfo]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">govinfo.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">GOVPUB GP 26106c7aa879602e0a802c3c3f4466cc</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">United States Government Printing Office style manualApril 11, 2022...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 11, 2022</span></span></span>

What the evidence does **not** support is the stronger syllogism that the GPO prescribed one form, therefore every authentic federal document had to use it, therefore any departure proves fabrication. The GPO's statutory responsibility centred on government printing; its production rules recognised special treatment of supplied copy; its own institutional description characterises the manual as a standardisation and printing guide rather than an inflexible grammar imposed identically on every federal writer.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/GPO-STYLEMANUAL-2008/html/GPO-STYLEMANUAL-2008-1.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: govinfo.gov">[GovInfo]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">govinfo.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">GPO STYLEMANUAL</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">U.S. Government Printing Office Style Manual - Title Page, Style Board, Extract from Title 44, U.S.C., About This Manual, GPO&#x27;s On...</span></span></span>

Period records make the same point empirically. On 18 November 1952 alone, surviving high-level government material can be found using both **18 November 1952** and **November 18, 1952**. Later Eisenhower-era White House and State Department records continue to show month-day-year dating even in Top Secret material.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://history.state.gov//historicaldocuments/frus1952-54v15p1/d330" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: history.state.gov">[Office of the Historian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">history.state.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Office of the Historian Historical Documents</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Office of the Historian Historical Documents</span></span></span>

The correct evidential hierarchy is therefore straightforward. **Conformity** with the GPO military rule would make the MJ-12 dating less conspicuous, but would not prove authenticity. **Departure** from it makes the document more conspicuous, but does not by itself prove forgery. A stronger case requires something beyond the stylebook: evidence that the particular originating office, document class, author or production channel consistently followed a specific rule and that the disputed paper violates that demonstrable practice.

For [the alleged MJ-12 documents]({{ 'the-alleged-mj-12-documents/' | relative_url }}), that narrower comparison is more valuable than treating the federal government as a single typist. Klass's Hillenkoetter correspondence research is relevant precisely because it moves in that direction: it compares the disputed paper with documents associated with the individual supposedly responsible for it. Greenwood's frequency argument likewise asks how often the disputed construction actually occurs in comparable archival material. Those approaches can assign evidential weight to an anomaly without converting a printing convention into an absolute bureaucratic law.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://centerforinquiry.s3.amazonaws.com/wp-content/uploads/sites/29/2022/09/26195532/SUN-72-Summer-2002.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: centerforinquiry.s3.amazonaws.com">[Center for Inquiry]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">centerforinquiry.s3.amazonaws.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Center for Inquiry</span></span></span>

## The useful conclusion


<div class="content-callout content-callout--key content-callout--takeaway" markdown="1">

The date objection survives, but in a more qualified form than the simplest version suggests. Federal style guidance clearly supports **18 November 1952**, without a comma, as the expected military-style construction. The MJ-12 briefing's **18 November, 1952** is therefore genuinely non-standard, and the additional use of leading zeros gives document examiners further reason to compare it closely with authenticated contemporary material.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/GOVPUB-GP-26106c7aa879602e0a802c3c3f4466cc/pdf/GOVPUB-GP-26106c7aa879602e0a802c3c3f4466cc.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: govinfo.gov">[GovInfo]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">govinfo.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">GOVPUB GP 26106c7aa879602e0a802c3c3f4466cc</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">United States Government Printing Office style manualApril 11, 2022...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 11, 2022</span></span></span>

</div>

At the same time, the historical record does not justify turning the GPO Style Manual into an inflexible rule for every piece of internal government paperwork. GPO authority was rooted in the production of government printing, real federal records display competing date conventions, and official production procedures themselves allowed different treatment according to the copy and instructions supplied.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/GPO-STYLEMANUAL-2008/html/GPO-STYLEMANUAL-2008-1.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: govinfo.gov">[GovInfo]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">govinfo.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">GPO STYLEMANUAL</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">U.S. Government Printing Office Style Manual - Title Page, Style Board, Extract from Title 44, U.S.C., About This Manual, GPO&#x27;s On...</span></span></span>

For the MJ-12 dispute, the distinction changes the burden of argument. The significant question is not **“Did this document violate a government style rule?”** The answer is broadly yes: its hybrid construction departs from the documented military convention. The better question is **“How improbable would that departure have been in the specific office, document type and production circumstances claimed for this paper?”** On the available evidence, the date style is a legitimate warning sign and potentially useful cumulative evidence, but the GPO rule alone cannot bear the weight of a conclusive [forgery test]({{ 'forgery-test/' | relative_url }}).
