## Introduction

The central credibility problem with the Timothy Cooper collection is not whether some sheets look old, use period typewriters or contain historically plausible details. It is that the collection lacks a continuous, independently documented chain of [custody]({{ 'custody/' | relative_url }}) from a government office or archive to the material now attributed to Cooper. The Majestic Documents researchers who promoted many of the papers themselves call provenance their “weakest link” and acknowledge that they cannot prove Cooper received the documents in his mailbox as described.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://majesticdocuments.com/documents/document-authentication/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: majesticdocuments.com">[Majestic Documents]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">majesticdocuments.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Majestic Documents Document Authentication &#124; Majestic Documents</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Majestic DocumentsDocument Authentication &#124; Majestic DocumentsOctober 29, 2018...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 29, 2018</span></span></span>



<figure class="article-media-figure article-media-figure-illustration"><img src="{{ "/assets/images/alleged_mj12_documen_5d9d42_timothy_cooper_docum_076727_cooper_chain_of_cust_f44dbb-Illustration-1-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" alt="Chain of Custody illustration 1" data-theme-src-dark="{{ "/assets/images/alleged_mj12_documen_5d9d42_timothy_cooper_docum_076727_cooper_chain_of_cust_f44dbb-Illustration-1-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" data-theme-src-light="{{ "/assets/images/alleged_mj12_documen_5d9d42_timothy_cooper_docum_076727_cooper_chain_of_cust_f44dbb-Illustration-1-light.svg" | relative_url }}" width="1920" height="1080" loading="lazy" decoding="async" fetchpriority="low"><figcaption class="article-media-caption"><span class="article-media-caption-label">Explanatory illustration 1</span></figcaption></figure>

That weakness is magnified because “the Cooper collection” did not come from one traceable release. Its catalogue assigns material to a Legionnaire, a supposed former CIA archivist, “Thomas Cryll ‘Cy’ [Cantwheel]({{ 'cantwheel/' | relative_url }})”, a woman presented as Cantwheel’s daughter, and anonymous sources S-1, S-2 and S-3.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://majesticdocuments.com/documents/document-sources/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: majesticdocuments.com">[Majestic Documents]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">majesticdocuments.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Majestic Documents Document Sources &#124; Majestic Documents</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Majestic DocumentsDocument Sources &#124; Majestic DocumentsOctober 29, 2018...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 29, 2018</span></span></span> The result is not one provenance chain but several incomplete ones. Even where envelopes, postmarks, paper or ink can be examined, those features can establish only limited facts about an object’s physical history. Without government-held counterparts or records documenting removal and transfer, they cannot establish that the extraordinary text on the page originated inside the government.

## What a reliable chain of custody would require

For an alleged classified memorandum, strong provenance would ideally begin before the document reached a private collector. Researchers would want an identifiable originating office; an archival file, register or other record showing where the document belonged; a traceable sequence of custodians; and evidence connecting the surviving physical object to that official history. A government archive can sometimes supply precisely this sort of context: record group, series, box, folder, neighbouring records, accession history and administrative documentation.

The distinction between **custody** and **authentication** is important. The US National Archives explicitly says that when it certifies a reproduction, it is attesting that the reproduction is a true copy of a document in its custody; it does not thereby authenticate every statement contained in that document.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/research/military/air-force/ufos" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives Project BLUE BOOK</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesProject BLUE BOOK - Unidentified Flying Objects &#124; National ArchivesJune 25, 2024...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 25, 2024</span></span></span> That is nevertheless much stronger provenance than an anonymous delivery, because researchers can establish where the archival object was found and how it entered government custody.

The [Cooper papers]({{ 'cooper-papers/' | relative_url }}) generally begin much later in the evidential sequence. According to the Majestic Documents account, Cooper started receiving apparently classified papers in his post-office box in 1992 from someone using the name Cantwheel. Robert Wood and later Ryan Wood obtained material from Cooper for analysis. Cooper also protected the identities of people said to have supplied him with documents.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://majesticdocuments.com/documents/document-sources/timothy-s-cooper/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: majesticdocuments.com">[Majestic Documents]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">majesticdocuments.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Majestic Documents Timothy S. Cooper &#124; Majestic Documents</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Majestic DocumentsTimothy S. Cooper &#124; Majestic DocumentsNovember 14, 2018...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 14, 2018</span></span></span> In chain-of-custody terms, therefore, the independently observable history often starts with **Cooper possessing a document**, not with the purported government author creating and filing it.

That leaves several different propositions which should not be collapsed into one:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--step-flow" markdown="1">

1. a particular piece of paper existed when researchers examined it;
2. Cooper possessed or received it at some particular time;
3. somebody sent or handed it to him;
4. that person was the intelligence or military insider claimed;
5. the document previously belonged to the government organisation indicated by its text or markings;
6. the text itself was created by the named official on the stated historical date.

</div>

Evidence establishing one step does not automatically establish the next. That separation is the key to understanding the Cooper provenance problem.

## The collection has multiple broken chains, not one chain

The Majestic Documents catalogue is unusually useful on this point because it openly separates the Cooper-associated material by alleged source. It lists an Air Accident Report under a “Legionnaire”; a Marilyn Monroe document under a “Former CIA Archivist”; a substantial series under Cantwheel; another group under “Salina — daughter of Cantwheel”; and later S-1, S-2 and S-3 streams.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://majesticdocuments.com/documents/document-sources/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: majesticdocuments.com">[Majestic Documents]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">majesticdocuments.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Majestic Documents Document Sources &#124; Majestic Documents</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Majestic DocumentsDocument Sources &#124; Majestic DocumentsOctober 29, 2018...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 29, 2018</span></span></span>

That structure prevents a simple argument that either validates or invalidates everything at once. It also creates a demanding provenance problem: each source stream requires its own verification.

**Cantwheel is an attribution rather than an archival trail.** The Majestic Documents site describes Thomas Cryll “Cy” Cantwheel as a cover name and attributes extensive intelligence credentials to him, including service with the Counter Intelligence Corps and the alleged Interplanetary Phenomenon Unit. But its account of those credentials ultimately rests substantially on Cantwheel's claims and Cooper's investigation of them. The site says his whereabouts were unknown when Cooper prepared his 1996 report.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://majesticdocuments.com/documents/document-sources/thomas-cryll-cy-cantwheel/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: majesticdocuments.com">[Majestic Documents]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">majesticdocuments.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Majestic Documents Thomas Cryll “Cy” Cantwheel &#124; Majestic Documents</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Majestic DocumentsThomas Cryll “Cy” Cantwheel &#124; Majestic DocumentsFebruary 26, 1996...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: February 26, 1996</span></span></span> That is very different from locating personnel records that identify the source, establishing his authorised access to each document and tracing those papers from specified government files into his possession.

**The Salina stream adds another unverified link.** A handwritten note dated 5 June 1996 was presented as coming from Cantwheel's daughter and saying that her father wanted Cooper to have the accompanying material. The Majestic Documents site itself says it seriously doubts that either “Cantwheel” or “Salina” was a real name.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://majesticdocuments.com/documents/majestic-documents/documents-dated-1970-present/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: majesticdocuments.com">[Majestic Documents]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">majesticdocuments.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">documents dated 1970 present</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Majestic DocumentsDocuments Dated 1970-Present &#124; Majestic DocumentsOctober 31, 2018...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 31, 2018</span></span></span> Whatever the note may show about the story supplied to Cooper, an unidentified writer saying that another pseudonymous source wanted documents transferred does not create an independently auditable government chain of custody.

**S-3 illustrates how little a postmark can prove.** The project's account says Cooper received a letter postmarked Sacramento, using a Police Officers Association return address, containing a one-page memorandum purportedly authorising creation of the Interplanetary Phenomenon Unit.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://majesticdocuments.com/documents/document-sources/source-s3/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: majesticdocuments.com">[Majestic Documents]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">majesticdocuments.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Majestic Documents Source S3 &#124; Majestic Documents</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Majestic Documents Source S3 &#124; Majestic Documents</span></span></span> A postal mark can be useful evidence that an envelope passed through the postal system at a certain place and time. It does not establish who originally created the enclosed memorandum decades earlier, where it was kept, or whether the enclosure had any official relationship to the return address.

The same distinction applies to the stronger postal evidence claimed for other Cooper material. Ryan Wood's defence of Cooper points to envelopes, postage meters and postmarks, including material said to have been postmarked at Langley, Virginia, and Fort Meade.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://majesticdocuments.com/pdf/10reasons.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: majesticdocuments.com">[Majestic Documents]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">majesticdocuments.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Majestic Documents Ten Reasons Cooper Provenance.PD F</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Majestic DocumentsTenReasonsCooperProvenance.PDFJune 14, 2007...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 14, 2007</span></span></span> Taken at face value, such evidence can strengthen a claim about **mailing history**. It still does not, without additional evidence, establish the institutional origin of the enclosed historical documents. A letter mailed near or even through a government facility is not automatically a document created, maintained or authorised by that agency.

## Cooper's own account cannot substitute for external provenance

The provenance case has sometimes been framed around Cooper's credibility as an individual. The Majestic Documents site argues that he was basically honest, had little financial incentive to fabricate a huge collection and was willing to sign notarised affidavits.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://majesticdocuments.com/documents/document-sources/timothy-s-cooper/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: majesticdocuments.com">[Majestic Documents]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">majesticdocuments.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Majestic Documents Timothy S. Cooper &#124; Majestic Documents</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Majestic DocumentsTimothy S. Cooper &#124; Majestic DocumentsNovember 14, 2018...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 14, 2018</span></span></span> Those considerations may be relevant when assessing whether Cooper knowingly forged documents. They do not solve the narrower chain-of-custody question.

A collector can accurately describe receiving an object without knowing its true origin. An anonymous source can provide genuine historical records, fabricated records, altered copies, or mixtures of all three. Consequently, even complete confidence in Cooper's honesty would establish at most that he truthfully reported what happened at his end of a transfer. It would not identify who created the documents or establish where they had been during the preceding decades.

The attempt to use a polygraph to clarify Cooper's account actually demonstrates why provenance needs documentary rather than personal verification. A contemporary defence published by Ryan Wood reported that Cooper showed deception when repeatedly answering a question about whether he had lied in saying that he received the Majestic documents in his mailbox. Wood interpreted this not as proof of forgery but as a consequence of Cooper protecting additional sources, and said the episode prompted a more detailed breakdown of which material allegedly came from whom.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://rense.com/politics4/lie.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: rense.com">[Rense]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">rense.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on rense.com.</span></span></span>

That interpretation may or may not be correct; polygraphs are not a substitute for archival evidence in either direction. The important point for provenance is simpler: the history of acquisition required reconstruction from Cooper's account after questions arose about it. That is a weaker evidential structure than contemporaneous government accession, transfer and file records.

## The altered-document problem shows why provenance matters

The practical danger of a broken chain is not merely theoretical. A document can contain authentic period language, names, bureaucratic conventions and historical details because a fabricator has worked from a genuine historical source.

A particularly important Cooper-linked example is a purported 1948 memorandum attributed to CIA director Roscoe [Hillenkoetter]({{ 'hillenkoetter/' | relative_url }}). A contemporary critical review recorded [Stanton Friedman]({{ 'friedman-s-case/' | relative_url }})'s conclusion that it was a doctored version of a memorandum originally associated with President Roosevelt.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://noufors.com/Documents/Books%2C%20Manuals%20and%20Published%20Papers/Specialty%20UFO%20Publications/UFO%20Historical%20Review/January%201999.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: noufors.com">[Noufors]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">noufors.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Barry Greenwood UHR Box 176 Stoneham,January 4, 2014...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 4, 2014</span></span></span> A later account of the controversy likewise reports that Friedman concluded the March 1948 Hillenkoetter memorandum — which he said was the first document Cooper had sent him — was a doctored version of an earlier genuine memorandum.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.scribd.com/document/798430882/Kevin-D-Randle-Case-MJ-12-the-True-Story-Behind-the-Governments-s-UFO-Conspiracies-Speaking-Volumes-LLC-2018" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: scribd.com">[Scribd]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">scribd.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Kevin D. Randle</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Kevin D. Randle</span></span></span>

That episode demonstrates a central forensic trap. Historical accuracy in names, offices, phrasing or format does not necessarily indicate that a disputed document came from the office it purports to represent. Those features can be inherited from authentic source material.

It also shows why one demonstrably altered item does **not**, by itself, prove that Cooper fabricated the whole collection. Because the collection contains several alleged [source streams]({{ 'source-streams/' | relative_url }}), an altered document could theoretically have been introduced by one supplier while other items had different origins. But the same fact cuts against blanket authentication: once a collection with weak provenance is known to contain material produced by modifying genuine historical records, apparent period consistency becomes less probative. Each document needs independent support outside the collection.

## Why forensic consistency cannot repair the missing history

Supporters of the Cooper material have placed considerable weight on physical and typographic testing. The Majestic Documents authentication programme describes examining typography, type style, paper, insignia, handwriting, signatures, chronology and consistency with other classified documents. It also reports work involving ink, paper, watermarks and typewriters, including original-paper material that its researchers considered difficult to explain as modern computer-generated forgery.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://majesticdocuments.com/documents/document-authentication/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: majesticdocuments.com">[Majestic Documents]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">majesticdocuments.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Majestic Documents Document Authentication &#124; Majestic Documents</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Majestic DocumentsDocument Authentication &#124; Majestic DocumentsOctober 29, 2018...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 29, 2018</span></span></span>

Those tests can answer worthwhile questions. A [watermark]({{ 'watermark/' | relative_url }}) introduced after a document's purported date could be powerful evidence against authenticity. Ink containing a chemical formulation unavailable at the claimed date could likewise expose a later production. Conversely, paper and ink compatible with the alleged period can remove certain objections.

But compatibility is not provenance. Old paper can survive unused. Genuine historical documents can be altered. A period typewriter can be used later. Authentic government material can provide models for classification markings and formatting. A photocopy may preserve the visual characteristics of an earlier source without revealing when the disputed wording entered the reproduction chain.

Some Cooper material makes this limitation especially obvious. The Majestic Documents catalogue itself labels several Cantwheel items as “retyped” copies. For a purported 1942 Roosevelt memorandum associated with another source stream, the site acknowledges that authenticating a retyped memorandum is “nearly hopeless” and says period format can provide only some assistance.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://majesticdocuments.com/documents/majestic-documents/documents-dated-prior-to-1948/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: majesticdocuments.com">[Majestic Documents]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">majesticdocuments.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">documents dated prior to 1948</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">documents dated prior to 1948</span></span></span> Once the supposed original government object has disappeared from the evidential chain, analysis of the later retyping cannot reconstruct who authored the original text.

The S-1 “[Burned Memo]({{ 'burned-memo/' | relative_url }})” presents a related problem. Its accompanying letter, dated 23 June 1999, identifies the writer only as a retired CIA counter-intelligence officer and claims that the document was saved from destruction. It further asserts that the paper trail had otherwise been purged.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://majesticdocuments.com/pdf/burnedmemocoverletter.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: majesticdocuments.com">[Majestic Documents]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">majesticdocuments.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on majesticdocuments.com.</span></span></span> That story supplies an explanation for why corroborating records would be absent, but it cannot itself serve as independent evidence that the purge occurred. In provenance analysis, a source's explanation for missing provenance is still part of the claim requiring verification.

## Government archives provide no matching Cooper chain

The broader archival record makes the missing Cooper provenance more consequential. The US National Archives reports extensive searches of National Security Council records and related holdings for “MJ-12”, “Majestic”, UFO terminology and related expressions. Apart from the controversial Cutler-Twining memorandum, those searches did not produce the documentary trail that would be expected to independently anchor the alleged programme. The Archives also records unsuccessful inquiries to the Air Force, Joint Chiefs of Staff and National Security Council for further information.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/research/military/air-force/ufos" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives Project BLUE BOOK</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesProject BLUE BOOK - Unidentified Flying Objects &#124; National ArchivesJune 25, 2024...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 25, 2024</span></span></span>

The US Government Accountability Office reached a similarly important conclusion in 1995 when examining MJ-12 material. It reported that the Information Security Oversight Office, Air Force and National Archives knew the Majestic-12 material through submissions from non-governmental persons, and that there was no evidence the material constituted actual executive-branch documents. Federal archivists had found no records fitting the alleged programme in their searches.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gao.gov/products/154832" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gao.gov">[Government Accountability Office]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gao.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on gao.gov.</span></span></span>

The FBI's treatment of the earlier MJ-12 material also illustrates the difference between possessing a copy and establishing government provenance. Its public files state that FBI offices received copies of the alleged Eisenhower briefing material in 1988, but an Air Force investigation concluded that the document was fake; the FBI now describes that MJ-12 document as bogus.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://vault.fbi.gov/unexplained-phenomenon/search" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: vault.fbi.gov">[FBI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">vault.fbi.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">— Federal Bureau of Investigation</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">— Federal Bureau of Investigation</span></span></span> That finding concerns the earlier MJ-12 document rather than proving anything automatically about every Cooper paper. Its relevance here is methodological: the mere appearance of an alleged classified document within government correspondence or files can record that officials **received and investigated a claim**, not that the government originally created the disputed document.

For the Cooper collection, therefore, a decisive discovery would be qualitatively different from another matching phrase, typeface or piece of old paper. It would be an independently located government copy, file register, routing record, accession history or other archival evidence linking a particular Cooper document to the office and period from which it claims to originate.

## Why provenance remains the collection's core problem

The Cooper collection sits in an awkward evidential position. There is enough information about its arrival to reconstruct portions of its **private** history: Cooper possessed documents; researchers obtained material from him; some pieces reportedly arrived through the post; source labels and approximate receipt periods were recorded; and some physical originals were available for examination. The Majestic Documents catalogue itself preserves much of that acquisition history.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://majesticdocuments.com/documents/document-sources/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: majesticdocuments.com">[Majestic Documents]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">majesticdocuments.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Majestic Documents Document Sources &#124; Majestic Documents</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Majestic DocumentsDocument Sources &#124; Majestic DocumentsOctober 29, 2018...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 29, 2018</span></span></span>

What is missing is the more important earlier half of the chain. For the extraordinary documents, there is generally no independently verified sequence running from the purported government author, through the relevant office and records system, through identifiable custodians, and finally to Cooper. Instead, that space is occupied by pseudonyms, anonymous correspondents, private testimony, protected identities and accounts of clandestine removal.

That does not logically prove that every Cooper document is false. It sets a stricter limit on what the surviving evidence can establish. A document whose paper, ink or typography is compatible with the 1940s may be **physically consistent** with that period. A postmarked envelope may establish that something was mailed from a particular place at a particular time. Historically accurate bureaucratic details may show that the author possessed good source material. None of those findings, separately or together, necessarily demonstrates official authorship.

The strongest way to assess the Cooper collection is therefore document by document and source stream by source stream. Evidence generated within the collection should not be allowed to authenticate other members of the same unverified chain merely because their stories agree. Independent archival corroboration deserves substantially greater weight. Until such corroboration connects particular papers to government custody before their private appearance, the Cooper collection can support arguments about physical consistency and acquisition history, but it cannot supply the missing official provenance on its own.
