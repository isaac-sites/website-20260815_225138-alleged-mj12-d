## Introduction

The alleged 1952 MJ-12 briefing does not describe Roswell as a secret that generated only one or two memoranda. It implies a continuing research-and-intelligence programme with repeated analytical reports, status reports, contingency planning, photographs and maps. Its own attachment list names seven such supporting products in addition to the supposed presidential order establishing Majestic-12. Yet searches of US government archives have not produced that documentary series, or an independently authenticated institutional trail showing that it ever existed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/MJ-12_Document" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: en.wikisource.org">[Wikisource]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">en.wikisource.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">MJ-12 Document</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">MJ-12 Document - Wikisource, the free online library...</span></span></span>

That absence is more significant than simply failing to find one famous MJ-12 document. A programme involving recovered wreckage, biological remains, laboratory analysis, security operations and presidential oversight would normally touch numerous offices and generate records at several levels. The 1994–95 [Air Force]({{ 'air-force/' | relative_url }}) and General Accounting Office (GAO) searches did find genuine Roswell-era material and extensive records from other classified programmes. What they did not find was the web of Roswell follow-up documentation implied by the MJ-12 narrative.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://irp.fas.org/gao/nsi95187.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: irp.fas.org">[Intelligence Resource Program]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">irp.fas.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Intelligence Resource Program GAO</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Intelligence Resource ProgramGAO - Government Records: Results of a Search for Records Concerning the 1947 Crash Near Roswell, New Mexico...</span></span></span>

## The briefing describes a documentary series, not a single secret memo

The alleged Eisenhower briefing presents itself as only an introduction to a fuller operational briefing. More importantly for the paper-trail question, its final page enumerates an unusually concrete set of supporting documents. These are not vague references to unspecified secret files. They have titles, report numbers or dates, suggesting an organised records system stretching from September 1947 to June 1952.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/MJ-12_Document" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: en.wikisource.org">[Wikisource]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">en.wikisource.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">MJ-12 Document</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">MJ-12 Document - Wikisource, the free online library...</span></span></span>

The numbering makes the implied archive larger still. A document called “Blue Team Report #5” naturally suggests at least four earlier numbered reports, while “Status Report #2” presupposes the first status report already listed. The briefing also says that its contingency plan should remain ready in case a public announcement became necessary. On the face of the document, therefore, MJ-12 was supposed to be a continuing bureaucracy rather than an improvised response to the July 1947 recovery.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/MJ-12_Document" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: en.wikisource.org">[Wikisource]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">en.wikisource.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">MJ-12 Document</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">MJ-12 Document - Wikisource, the free online library...</span></span></span>

That matters because the Roswell claims themselves imply further documentation beyond the eight attachments. The briefing describes recovered wreckage being moved for technical study and biological remains being examined by a scientific team. It later refers to material being transported to an Atomic Energy Commission facility at Sandia for study. Even under stringent compartmentalisation, activities of that kind would potentially create transport, [custody]({{ 'custody/' | relative_url }}), laboratory, personnel, security and administrative records outside the briefing package itself.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/MJ-12_Document" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: en.wikisource.org">[Wikisource]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">en.wikisource.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">MJ-12 Document</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">MJ-12 Document - Wikisource, the free online library...</span></span></span>

The archival question is consequently broader than “Where is Attachment D?” It is where the surrounding institutional footprint went.

## Official searches found genuine Roswell records, but not the MJ-12 trail

The strongest evidence concerning this problem comes from the GAO investigation requested by Representative Steven Schiff in the 1990s. GAO examined classified and unclassified records extending from July 1947 through the 1950s, drawing on the Department of Defense, FBI, CIA, National Security Council and numerous military repositories. Its physical searches included Army intelligence correspondence, headquarters message traffic, Air Materiel Command research-and-development files, Eighth Air Force records, Roswell unit histories, Project Sign material and other potentially relevant collections.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://irp.fas.org/gao/nsi95187.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: irp.fas.org">[Intelligence Resource Program]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">irp.fas.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Intelligence Resource Program GAO</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Intelligence Resource ProgramGAO - Government Records: Results of a Search for Records Concerning the 1947 Crash Near Roswell, New Mexico...</span></span></span>

For Roswell itself, GAO found only **two government records originating in 1947** that directly mentioned the incident. One was the July history of the 509th Bomb Group and Roswell Army Air Field, which recorded that the public-information office had been busy answering enquiries about the reported “flying disc” and stated that the object proved to be a radar-tracking balloon. The other was the FBI's 8 July teletype recording information from Eighth Air Force headquarters about an object resembling a high-altitude weather balloon with a radar reflector.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://irp.fas.org/gao/nsi95187.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: irp.fas.org">[Intelligence Resource Program]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">irp.fas.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Intelligence Resource Program GAO</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Intelligence Resource ProgramGAO - Government Records: Results of a Search for Records Concerning the 1947 Crash Near Roswell, New Mexico...</span></span></span>

The surviving monthly history is particularly useful because it demonstrates that mundane administrative documentation from Roswell was being produced. Its discussion ranges across briefings, public-information work, personnel changes and even the workload of the base photographic laboratory. In the middle of those routine entries sits the short “flying disc” passage. Nothing in that surviving history records the extraordinary second layer of operations described by the MJ-12 briefing.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ufotransparency.com/files/decade-1990s-roswell-report-fact-vs-fiction-1994-dtic-ada326148" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ufotransparency.com">[UFO Transparency]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ufotransparency.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">decade 1990s roswell report fact vs fiction 1994 dtic ada326148</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UFO TransparencyRoswell Report: Fact vs Fiction, 1994, U.S. Air Force Headquarters (Office of Air Force History) · 1994 · UFO Transparency...</span></span></span>

GAO also followed the debris trail suggested by the authentic FBI message. Because that teletype said the recovered material was being sent to Wright Field for examination, investigators reviewed Air Materiel Command records from 1947 to 1950 for evidence of involvement. They found no records mentioning the Roswell crash or an Air Materiel Command examination of recovered Roswell debris.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://irp.fas.org/gao/nsi95187.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: irp.fas.org">[Intelligence Resource Program]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">irp.fas.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Intelligence Resource Program GAO</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Intelligence Resource ProgramGAO - Government Records: Results of a Search for Records Concerning the 1947 Crash Near Roswell, New Mexico...</span></span></span>

The search extended well beyond Wright Field. GAO reported that other material it examined — including previously classified records and Project Blue Book Special Report No. 14 — did not mention the July 1947 crash or recovery. The National Security Council, [White House]({{ 'white-house/' | relative_url }}) Office of Science and Technology Policy and Department of Energy reported no Roswell records. After ordering a comprehensive database search specifically for “Roswell, New Mexico”, the CIA likewise reported finding no documents concerning the crash.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://irp.fas.org/gao/nsi95187.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: irp.fas.org">[Intelligence Resource Program]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">irp.fas.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Intelligence Resource Program GAO</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Intelligence Resource ProgramGAO - Government Records: Results of a Search for Records Concerning the 1947 Crash Near Roswell, New Mexico...</span></span></span>

This does not prove that every government document concerning Roswell survived. It does establish something narrower and important: searches across multiple repositories failed to recover the continuing technical and administrative record that the MJ-12 briefing's own narrative would lead a reader to expect.

## Some Roswell files really were destroyed

The missing-record problem has a genuine complication. GAO discovered that portions of the Roswell Army Air Field archive had been destroyed. General administrative records covering March 1945 through December 1949 and outgoing messages covering October 1946 through December 1949 were no longer available. The disposition paperwork did not identify who destroyed them, when they were destroyed or the authority under which disposal occurred.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://irp.fas.org/gao/nsi95187.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: irp.fas.org">[Intelligence Resource Program]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">irp.fas.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Intelligence Resource Program GAO</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Intelligence Resource ProgramGAO - Government Records: Results of a Search for Records Concerning the 1947 Crash Near Roswell, New Mexico...</span></span></span>

That finding is sometimes treated as evidence that incriminating Roswell records could deliberately have been removed. The GAO report itself did not reach that conclusion. The National Personnel Records Center's chief archivist told investigators that many Air Force organisational records from the period had been destroyed without the relevant disposal authority being entered on the forms, and GAO found other records-control forms consistent with that broader records-management problem. The missing Roswell files are therefore a real archival gap, but the available evidence does not establish a targeted MJ-12 purge.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://irp.fas.org/gao/nsi95187.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: irp.fas.org">[Intelligence Resource Program]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">irp.fas.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Intelligence Resource Program GAO</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Intelligence Resource ProgramGAO - Government Records: Results of a Search for Records Concerning the 1947 Crash Near Roswell, New Mexico...</span></span></span>

Nor were all of Roswell's records lost. Unit histories survived, including the combined 509th Bomb Group and base history covering the crucial period. Morning reports, Eighth Air Force records and numerous records from higher commands and other agencies were among the materials searched. That distinction is essential: the archival record is incomplete, but it is not a blank space into which any missing document can automatically be placed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://irp.fas.org/gao/nsi95187.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: irp.fas.org">[Intelligence Resource Program]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">irp.fas.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Intelligence Resource Program GAO</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Intelligence Resource ProgramGAO - Government Records: Results of a Search for Records Concerning the 1947 Crash Near Roswell, New Mexico...</span></span></span>

There is also an important records-management distinction concerning accident reports. Army regulations required reports of aircraft accidents to be retained permanently, but Air Force officials told GAO that a weather-balloon crash did not require such an accident report. GAO consequently could not treat the absence of an accident report as evidence that a required Roswell file had vanished. It found four documented military-aircraft accidents in New Mexico during July 1947, all occurring after 8 July, but no corresponding Roswell aircraft-accident report.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://irp.fas.org/gao/nsi95187.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: irp.fas.org">[Intelligence Resource Program]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">irp.fas.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Intelligence Resource Program GAO</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Intelligence Resource ProgramGAO - Government Records: Results of a Search for Records Concerning the 1947 Crash Near Roswell, New Mexico...</span></span></span>

## The MJ-12 searches created a second, independent paper-trail problem

Roswell was not the only subject searched. The National Archives separately investigated the alleged MJ-12 organisation and the “Briefing Document: Operation Majestic 12”. Its staff searched Air Force and Joint Chiefs of Staff holdings; the Truman and Eisenhower presidential libraries searched their collections; and National Security Council indexes were checked under terms including “MJ-12”, “majestic”, “UFO”, “flying saucers” and “extraterrestrial biological entities”. Those searches did not uncover the briefing or its supposed supporting archive.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/research/military/air-force/ufos" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives Project BLUE BOOK</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesProject BLUE BOOK - Unidentified Flying Objects &#124; National Archives...</span></span></span>

The conspicuous exception was the so-called **Cutler–Twining memorandum**, dated 14 July 1954, referring to an “NSC/MJ-12 Special Studies Project”. Rather than supplying the [missing chain]({{ 'missing-chain/' | relative_url }}), however, the National Archives records a series of anomalies surrounding it. The memorandum lacks the [Top Secret register]({{ 'register-numbers/' | relative_url }}) number expected in the series where it was found; its folder contains no other NSC/MJ-12 documents; searches of related Secretary of Defense, Joint Chiefs and Air Force files produced nothing further; and enquiries to the Air Force, Joint Chiefs and NSC likewise failed to develop a supporting record.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/research/military/air-force/ufos" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives Project BLUE BOOK</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesProject BLUE BOOK - Unidentified Flying Objects &#124; National Archives...</span></span></span>

There are further provenance problems. The National Archives reports that the security marking on the memorandum was not used by the NSC during the Eisenhower administration, and that its paper differed from the representative Cutler documents examined by the Eisenhower Library. The official NSC meeting minutes contain no MJ-12 meeting on the date specified, while Eisenhower's appointment books contain no corresponding special meeting. Robert Cutler himself was travelling overseas when he supposedly issued the memorandum.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/research/military/air-force/ufos" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives Project BLUE BOOK</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesProject BLUE BOOK - Unidentified Flying Objects &#124; National Archives...</span></span></span>

The FBI reached an equally unfavourable conclusion about the MJ-12 material it received in 1988. Its current Vault description says that two FBI offices received copies of an “Operation Majestic-12” memorandum purporting to brief Eisenhower on a secret committee exploiting an extraterrestrial recovery, and that an Air Force investigation determined the document was fake.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://vault.fbi.gov/unexplained-phenomenon/search" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: vault.fbi.gov">[FBI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">vault.fbi.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">— Federal Bureau of Investigation</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">FBI — Federal Bureau of Investigation...</span></span></span>

The significance for Roswell is not merely that individual MJ-12 documents have been disputed. The institutional linkage is missing. The alleged 1952 briefing describes a presidentially controlled programme that had already generated years of formal reports. National Archives searches have not produced the corresponding organisational records that would connect that programme to the ordinary archival structures of the Truman and Eisenhower administrations.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/research/military/air-force/ufos" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives Project BLUE BOOK</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesProject BLUE BOOK - Unidentified Flying Objects &#124; National Archives...</span></span></span>

## Real classified programmes left much richer records

The contrast with Project MOGUL sharpens the issue. MOGUL was itself highly classified: a balloon-borne programme intended to help detect Soviet nuclear tests. Yet decades later researchers were able to reconstruct it from surviving technical and progress reports, organisational records, correspondence, balloon-flight information and testimony from surviving participants. The Air Force's Roswell investigation explicitly reports finding unclassified technical and progress reports whose context revealed the classified MOGUL programme.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.dafhistory.af.mil/The-DAF-Legacy-Vault/DAF-History-Publications/igphoto/2003789023/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: dafhistory.af.mil">[DAF History]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">dafhistory.af.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-title">DAF History The Roswell Report: Fact versus Fiction in the New Mexico Desert</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">DAF History The Roswell Report: Fact versus Fiction in the New Mexico Desert</span></span></span>

The Air Force search was not confined to a few obvious Roswell files. Its records inventory shows searches of personal papers associated with senior officers including Curtis LeMay, Carl Spaatz, Nathan [Twining]({{ 'twining/' | relative_url }}) and Hoyt Vandenberg, as well as Army staff message-centre and Air Adjutant General holdings. Investigators also examined Roswell organisational histories page by page and searched finding aids under terms such as Roswell, unidentified flying objects and weather balloons.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ufotransparency.com/files/decade-1990s-roswell-report-fact-vs-fiction-1994-dtic-ada326148" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ufotransparency.com">[UFO Transparency]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ufotransparency.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">decade 1990s roswell report fact vs fiction 1994 dtic ada326148</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UFO TransparencyRoswell Report: Fact vs Fiction, 1994, U.S. Air Force Headquarters (Office of Air Force History) · 1994 · UFO Transparency...</span></span></span>

This comparison does not create a rule that every secret programme must leave an intact archive. Records can be destroyed, misfiled, remain classified or survive in repositories that researchers have not identified. Compartmentalisation can also keep individual offices from seeing the complete picture.

But secrecy and documentary invisibility are different propositions. MOGUL shows that an important compartmented programme can leave dispersed traces in contracts, technical work, personnel recollections, flight records and administrative files even when its true purpose was concealed. The alleged MJ-12 Roswell programme was supposedly more extraordinary, more organisationally complex and longer-lived. Its briefing names the very reports that should anchor that history, yet those reports lack authenticated archival provenance.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.dafhistory.af.mil/The-DAF-Legacy-Vault/DAF-History-Publications/igphoto/2003789023/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: dafhistory.af.mil">[DAF History]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">dafhistory.af.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-title">DAF History The Roswell Report: Fact versus Fiction in the New Mexico Desert</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">DAF History The Roswell Report: Fact versus Fiction in the New Mexico Desert</span></span></span>

## Why the missing paper trail carries so much weight

No single absent document disproves a secret operation. The stronger argument is cumulative. If the alleged briefing merely claimed that a handful of officers secretly examined debris for several days, archival silence would have relatively limited evidential value. Instead, it describes an operation extending from a recovery in 1947 through continuing scientific study, security measures and contingency planning into the Eisenhower transition. Its attachment list alone spans more than four years.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/MJ-12_Document" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: en.wikisource.org">[Wikisource]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">en.wikisource.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">MJ-12 Document</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">MJ-12 Document - Wikisource, the free online library...</span></span></span>

Such a programme would have several opportunities to leave traces: presidential and National Security Council records; Air Force headquarters correspondence; Air Materiel Command files; intelligence reports; scientific and technical documentation; status reports; security paperwork; transportation and custody records; photographs; maps; and records belonging to the named senior officials. Official searches touched many of precisely these record families without finding the network described by the briefing.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://irp.fas.org/gao/nsi95187.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: irp.fas.org">[Intelligence Resource Program]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">irp.fas.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Intelligence Resource Program GAO</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Intelligence Resource ProgramGAO - Government Records: Results of a Search for Records Concerning the 1947 Crash Near Roswell, New Mexico...</span></span></span>

The surviving evidence is therefore asymmetrical. There **is** a paper trail showing that Roswell generated publicity in July 1947, that military personnel recovered material, that the FBI was told of balloon-like debris, and that the event appeared in the Roswell base history. There is also a substantial documentary trail for the classified balloon programme that the Air Force later identified as the likely source of the debris.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://irp.fas.org/gao/nsi95187.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: irp.fas.org">[Intelligence Resource Program]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">irp.fas.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Intelligence Resource Program GAO</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Intelligence Resource ProgramGAO - Government Records: Results of a Search for Records Concerning the 1947 Crash Near Roswell, New Mexico...</span></span></span>

What has not emerged from comparable archival work is the much larger second record system demanded by the MJ-12 account: the September 1947 analytical report, November status reports, subsequent status report, Blue Team series, contingency plan, photographic folio and the surrounding administrative records of a presidentially controlled Roswell exploitation programme. The destruction of some Roswell files prevents an argument from absolute archival completeness, but it does not supply that missing evidence.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/MJ-12_Document" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: en.wikisource.org">[Wikisource]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">en.wikisource.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">MJ-12 Document</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">MJ-12 Document - Wikisource, the free online library...</span></span></span>

For assessing [the alleged MJ-12 documents]({{ 'the-alleged-mj-12-documents/' | relative_url }}), that is the central paper-trail problem. The briefing does not ask the reader to believe in a secret so perfectly contained that it produced virtually no paperwork. It explicitly tells the reader that substantial paperwork existed — and even provides its titles and dates. The enduring evidential difficulty is that the documentary system described inside the briefing has not been independently recovered outside it.
