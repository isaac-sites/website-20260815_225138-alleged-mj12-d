## Introduction

A typeface can help identify the *kind* of typewriting on a document, but it normally cannot identify the exact physical typewriter that produced it. That distinction is crucial in arguments about [the alleged MJ-12 documents]({{ 'the-alleged-mj-12-documents/' | relative_url }}). A match in letter shapes, pitch or general typestyle may show that a document is compatible with a family of machines, and a securely dated type design can sometimes expose an anachronism. It does not follow that one particular Underwood, Smith-Corona or other machine has been identified.

Forensic document examination treats these as different questions. Type design and spacing are principally **class characteristics**: features shared by a group of machines. Identifying an individual machine requires sufficiently distinctive characteristics such as recurring damaged characters, abnormal alignment or mechanical irregularities, ideally demonstrated by comparison with known typing from the suspected machine. Current FBI procedures likewise distinguish comparison of class characteristics from the later evaluation of distinguishing characteristics needed for source identification.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://fbilabqsd.fbi.gov/file-repository/questioned-documents/doc-223-07-typewriting-examinations.pdf/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: fbilabqsd.fbi.gov">[FBI Lab Vault]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">fbilabqsd.fbi.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Lab Vault Typewriting Examinations</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">FBI Lab VaultTypewriting ExaminationsMay 20, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 20, 2026</span></span></span>

That makes the famous MJ-12 dispute over whether the 1947 Truman-[Forrestal]({{ 'forrestal/' | relative_url }}) memorandum resembles a later Smith-Corona or an earlier Underwood more complicated than the shorthand versions suggest. The dispute concerns classification and dating before it concerns identification of an individual typewriter.

## Typeface Design Is Not Machine Identity

A typewriter leaves two broad kinds of information on paper. The first comes from its design: character shapes, character size, horizontal pitch, line spacing and related properties. These can narrow the possible source to a class of machines. The second comes from the history and condition of an individual machine: a chipped letter, a character that consistently strikes high, a bent typebar, uneven pressure or another repeatable peculiarity.

The distinction is explicit in forensic practice. New York State's forensic standardisation guidance defines a typewritten **class-characteristic** conclusion as one in which documents could have been produced by the same *class* of machine or by other machines exhibiting the same class features. A conclusion about **individual characteristics**, by contrast, requires enough distinctive features to determine that documents were produced by the same machine or machine system. Limited text, poor-quality samples or mixtures of similarities and differences can leave the result inconclusive.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.criminaljustice.ny.gov/forensic/standardization/questioned-documents.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: criminaljustice.ny.gov">[NYS Criminal Justice Services]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">criminaljustice.ny.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">S Criminal Justice Services Questioned Documents</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">S Criminal Justice ServicesQuestioned Documents...</span></span></span>

This is more than terminology. Suppose a disputed page contains ten-character-per-inch typing and letter designs matching a known Underwood specimen. That finding could establish that an Underwood equipped with that design was a plausible source. It would not establish that the page came from a particular Underwood sitting in a particular office.

Forensic literature has long drawn essentially this boundary. An Office of Justice Programs summary on identification of typewritten documents notes that character design, horizontal and vertical spacing and letter height can reveal possible alteration or misdating, whereas individual-machine attribution depends upon such characteristics as typeface defects, platen defects and lateral or vertical misalignment.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ojp.gov/ncjrs/virtual-library/abstracts/identification-typewritten-documents" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ojp.gov">[Office of Justice Programs]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ojp.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on ojp.gov.</span></span></span>

The difference can be expressed simply:

**Typeface resemblance:** *What machines could have produced typing like this?*

**Machine identification:** *Did this particular machine produce this typing?*

In the MJ-12 debate, conclusions answering the first question have sometimes been reported as though they answered the second.

## Why the Same Design Can Appear on Many Machines

Typewriters were manufactured products, not collections of unique alphabets created separately for every machine. Machines of the same model could naturally share type designs, while similar designs could also appear across different machines and manufacturers. A forensic case study published in the *Journal of the American Society of Questioned Document Examiners* specifically notes that manufacturers could obtain type fonts from the same suppliers, leaving different brands equipped with similar or even identical styles. It consequently distinguishes typewriter **classification** from typewriter **identification**.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://account.journal.asqde.org/index.php/asqde-j-jasqde/article/download/19/19" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: account.journal.asqde.org">[JASQDE]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">account.journal.asqde.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">JASQDECause of Typewriter Printwheel</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">March 4, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 4, 2026</span></span></span>

That is why recognising a typestyle is potentially valuable without being individualising. Size, spacing and character design can reduce the field. If reliable historical reference specimens show that a distinctive design did not exist until after a disputed document's stated date, classification alone could become powerful evidence of anachronism. But when several period machines could produce substantially similar characters, resemblance does not tell investigators which one was used.

The problem becomes still more complicated with machines using interchangeable typing elements. Research on print-wheel typewriters, for example, found that their high-quality output could possess limited individuality, changing the identification problem compared with traditional typebar machines.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/0379073886901787" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ScienceDirect Problems in identifying work from print wheel typewriters</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Problems in identifying work from print wheel typewriters - ScienceDirectJanuary 1, 1986...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 1, 1986</span></span></span> Although that technology postdates the principal early MJ-12 papers, it illustrates the general forensic principle: the appearance of an alphabet and the identity of the machine carrying it are not necessarily the same thing.

Even ordinary typebar machines require caution. A questioned-document laboratory's description of conventional examination explains that investigators may sometimes determine a possible make or model by classifying the typing and searching reference collections. Linking the document to a *specific* machine is a separate microscopic and visual comparison involving features such as alignment, spacing, damaged type, ink density and striking pressure.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.questioneddocuments.com/questioned-document-overviews/typewriter-and-typewriting-examinations/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: questioneddocuments.com">[Norwitch Lab]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">questioneddocuments.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Norwitch Lab Typewriter and Typewriting Examinations</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Norwitch LabTypewriter and Typewriting Examinations - Questioned Documents - Norwitch Document Laboratory...</span></span></span>

This also explains why a specimen catalogue can establish compatibility more readily than provenance. Finding an authentic 1940 specimen whose alphabet looks like disputed 1947 typing can rebut the assertion that the design necessarily originated in the 1960s. It cannot, by resemblance alone, establish which machine produced the disputed page in 1947.

## The Truman Memo Shows the Classification Problem

The alleged Truman-to-Forrestal memorandum dated 24 September 1947 provides a particularly clear MJ-12 example. Questioned-document examiner Peter Tytell was cited by sceptic Philip J. Klass as concluding that its typeface indicated a Smith-Corona machine that first appeared in 1963. Klass published the allegation as evidence that the memorandum could not genuinely date from 1947.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://skepticalinquirer.org/wp-content/uploads/sites/29/2019/03/Issue-02-12.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: skepticalinquirer.org">[Skeptical Inquirer]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">skepticalinquirer.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Skeptical Inquirer SKEPTICAL</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">INQUIRER Vol. 14 No. 2/Winter 1990 $6June 27, 2006...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 27, 2006</span></span></span>

If that classification were securely established, the logic would indeed be strong. A document cannot have been typed in 1947 using a type design or machine configuration first introduced sixteen years later. Notice, however, what would make the argument decisive: **the demonstrated date of the relevant class characteristic**, not proof that investigators had located the individual typewriter.

MJ-12 advocate [Stanton Friedman]({{ 'friedman-s-case/' | relative_url }}) disputed the Smith-Corona conclusion. In his account, Tytell did not prepare a formal report, while document examiner James Black was subsequently commissioned to examine the material and concluded that the typing was consistent with an Underwood Standard specimen dating from May 1940. Friedman's published defence therefore treated the later examination as defeating the alleged 1960s anachronism.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.theblackvault.com/casefiles/update-on-operation-majestic-12-documents/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theblackvault.com">[The Black Vault]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theblackvault.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Black Vault Update on Operation Majestic 12 Documents</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Black VaultUpdate on Operation Majestic 12 Documents - The Black Vault Case FilesJanuary 17, 2024...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 17, 2024</span></span></span> Claims circulated by the pro-MJ-12 investigators went further, saying Black had “identified” an Underwood Standard as the typewriter involved.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.oocities.org/Area51/Shadowlands/6583/maji021.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: oocities.org">[GeoCities]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">oocities.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Geo Cities</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GeoCitiesOctober 13, 1999...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 13, 1999</span></span></span>

The wording needs care. Matching a questioned document to a **1940 Underwood specimen or model typestyle** would establish something quite different from linking it to **one individual Underwood Standard machine**. The former would make a 1947 production date typographically possible. The latter would require individualising evidence from the physical machine or suitable known typing attributable to it.

This is precisely where the forensic distinction prevents overstatement in either direction. Tytell's reported Smith-Corona conclusion, if correct and chronologically exclusive, would provide an anachronism argument. Black's reported comparison with a 1940 Underwood specimen, if correct, would answer that particular anachronism argument by demonstrating an earlier compatible source. But an earlier compatible typestyle does not authenticate the memorandum. It merely removes one proposed reason why it could not have been typed in 1947.

Nor does the typography settle the wider authenticity question. The US National Archives records several independent problems with the MJ-12 material, including the unusual filing circumstances of the Cutler-[Twining]({{ 'twining/' | relative_url }}) memorandum and the failure of searches across relevant government records to locate corroborating documentation. Those issues are separate from whether a particular typestyle existed at the relevant date.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/research/military/air-force/ufos" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives Project BLUE BOOK</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesProject BLUE BOOK - Unidentified Flying Objects &#124; National ArchivesJune 25, 2024</span></span></span>

## What Would Identify One Physical Typewriter?

To move from “this resembles an Underwood” to “this was produced by *this* Underwood”, an examiner looks for a repeatable constellation of individual characteristics. These arise because manufacture, use, wear, damage and adjustment gradually give machines their own mechanical peculiarities.

Traditional individualising features include:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **Damaged typefaces.** A chip, dent, broken serif, irregular edge or other defect in a type slug can reproduce the same abnormality whenever that character is struck.
* **Vertical misalignment.** One character may consistently sit slightly above or below the normal baseline.
* **Horizontal misalignment.** A character may repeatedly strike too far left or right relative to its expected position.
* **Tilt or rotation.** A typebar or slug can strike at a slight angle rather than squarely.
* **Uneven impressions.** Mechanical alignment can cause one part of a character to strike more heavily than another.
* **Spacing abnormalities.** Wear or mechanical faults can produce repeatable irregularities in character or line spacing.
* **Platen and ribbon effects.** Defects or characteristic behaviour elsewhere in the typing system can contribute additional comparative information.

</div>

These are not hypothetical forensic criteria. The Office of Justice Programs lists typeface and platen defects, alignment, ribbon characteristics and key pressure among potentially individual characteristics.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ojp.gov/ncjrs/virtual-library/abstracts/identification-typewritten-documents" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ojp.gov">[Office of Justice Programs]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ojp.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on ojp.gov.</span></span></span> Laboratory procedures similarly instruct examiners to consider typestyle and spacing alongside typeface defects and alignment defects, and then to compare questioned and known typing side by side.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://forensic-resources-offload-media.s3.amazonaws.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/09/03111806/IA2-Typewriters-and-Other-Machine-Impressions-6-11.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: forensic-resources-offload-media.s3.amazonaws.com">[Forensic Resources Offload Media]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">forensic-resources-offload-media.s3.amazonaws.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on amazonaws.com.</span></span></span>

A published questioned-document case study describes defects as breaks or irregular impressions caused by such features as chips, bumps, dents and irregular outlines. Wear and damage make those abnormalities valuable because the same physical defect can recur across multiple impressions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://account.journal.asqde.org/index.php/asqde-j-jasqde/article/download/19/19" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: account.journal.asqde.org">[JASQDE]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">account.journal.asqde.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">JASQDECause of Typewriter Printwheel</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">March 4, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 4, 2026</span></span></span>

The important word is **recurring**. One oddly printed “e” proves little if the anomaly could result from ribbon condition, dirt, paper surface or a single imperfect strike. If the same “e” repeatedly contains the same nick, the “t” repeatedly sits high and another character consistently leans in the same direction — and the same combination occurs in known exemplars from a suspect machine — the cumulative pattern becomes much more discriminating.

## A Proper Comparison Needs Known Material

Individual identification is therefore fundamentally comparative. Finding an unusual feature in an MJ-12 photocopy does not by itself identify its source. Investigators need either the suspected machine or reliable known typing from it.

The FBI's current procedure illustrates the sequence. Examiners first compare class characteristics. If the known machine is inconsistent at that level, it can be excluded. If the class characteristics agree, the examiner assesses the machine's condition — including whether it is dirty, worn or damaged — obtains exemplars where appropriate, and conducts side-by-side comparison of the questioned and known impressions before evaluating distinguishing characteristics.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://fbilabqsd.fbi.gov/file-repository/questioned-documents/doc-223-07-typewriting-examinations.pdf/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: fbilabqsd.fbi.gov">[FBI Lab Vault]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">fbilabqsd.fbi.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Lab Vault Typewriting Examinations</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">FBI Lab VaultTypewriting ExaminationsMay 20, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 20, 2026</span></span></span> ASTM's standard guide likewise covers comparisons between questioned and known typewriting, assessment of whether sufficient material exists and procedures for taking exemplars from typewriters.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://store.astm.org/e2494-07.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: store.astm.org">[ASTM Store]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">store.astm.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on astm.org.</span></span></span>

This creates an obvious evidential difficulty for historical controversies. If the exact office typewriter supposedly used in 1947 is unavailable, an examiner cannot simply type an exemplar on it today. Period documents reliably known to have come from that particular machine could potentially preserve its characteristics, but only if their provenance is secure and their reproduction quality retains the tiny defects needed for comparison.

Photocopies and later-generation reproductions can make the problem worse. Fine edges, weak impressions and small defects may be degraded or obscured. Forensic guidance consequently recognises poor image quality and insufficient text as reasons an individual-source determination may remain inconclusive.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.criminaljustice.ny.gov/forensic/standardization/questioned-documents.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: criminaljustice.ny.gov">[NYS Criminal Justice Services]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">criminaljustice.ny.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">S Criminal Justice Services Questioned Documents</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">S Criminal Justice ServicesQuestioned Documents...</span></span></span>

That limitation matters especially for MJ-12 because some of the most disputed material became available to researchers as photographic reproductions rather than as an unquestionably original sheet paired with its alleged typewriter. A striking resemblance visible in reproduction can therefore support classification more readily than the much stronger proposition of individual-machine identification.

## Repeated Defects Can Also Reveal Mixed Typing

Individual characteristics have another useful role: determining whether different passages on a page appear to come from different machines or typing operations. This is more probative than merely noticing that one section “looks different”.

A forensic review indexed by the US Office of Justice Programs states that typewriting examination can help determine whether an entire document was typed on one machine or whether portions came from another. Differences in character design, spacing, alignment, defects, ribbon behaviour and pressure can contribute to such an assessment.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ojp.gov/ncjrs/virtual-library/abstracts/identification-typewritten-documents" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ojp.gov">[Office of Justice Programs]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ojp.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on ojp.gov.</span></span></span>

That distinction bears directly on another disputed feature of the Truman-Forrestal memorandum: claims that part of its date was typed separately. Pro-MJ-12 accounts of James Black's examination say that the numerals in the date sit vertically out of alignment and that the paper could have been removed and reinserted before that portion was typed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.oocities.org/Area51/Shadowlands/6583/maji021.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: oocities.org">[GeoCities]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">oocities.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Geo Cities</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GeoCitiesOctober 13, 1999...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 13, 1999</span></span></span>

Vertical displacement can certainly be relevant to reconstruction of how a page was produced. But it does not, on its own, answer *why* the page was reinserted or why another machine might have been used. Legitimate office preparation, later correction and fraudulent construction are all mechanisms capable of producing mixed or displaced typing. The evidential question is narrower: can the physical characteristics demonstrate a change of source or typing operation? Interpretation of what that change means requires additional evidence.

## What Typography Can and Cannot Establish

The most useful way to read typewriter arguments about MJ-12 is therefore as a ladder of increasingly demanding claims.

At the lower end, an examiner can recognise broad characteristics such as pitch and typestyle. Better reference specimens may narrow the possible manufacturers, models or periods. If every credible source for a distinctive design postdates the document, the result may expose an anachronism without identifying any individual machine.

Higher up the ladder, however, “this looks like an Underwood Standard” remains a class-level conclusion. The existence of a compatible 1940 Underwood specimen would demonstrate that such typing was technically possible before 1947; it would not demonstrate that an Underwood in Truman's office produced the memorandum.

At the strongest level, source identification requires agreement in distinguishing individual characteristics and the absence of differences that would preclude the proposed source. The FBI's present reporting framework expressly reserves “source identification” for a determination that questioned typing was prepared by the known typewriter or typing element because distinguishing characteristics agree; where agreement is limited or other limitations remain, weaker or inconclusive conclusions are appropriate.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://fbilabqsd.fbi.gov/file-repository/questioned-documents/doc-223-07-typewriting-examinations.pdf/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: fbilabqsd.fbi.gov">[FBI Lab Vault]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">fbilabqsd.fbi.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Lab Vault Typewriting Examinations</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">FBI Lab VaultTypewriting ExaminationsMay 20, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 20, 2026</span></span></span>

For the alleged MJ-12 documents, that hierarchy prevents two opposite mistakes. A critic should not treat superficial resemblance to a later typewriter as proof of a post-dated forgery unless the allegedly anachronistic characteristics have actually been shown to be chronologically exclusive. An advocate should not treat resemblance to a period type specimen as proof that the document came from an authentic government machine.

The typography can sometimes answer **could this style have existed then?** Individual-machine evidence addresses the much harder question **did this particular machine make this page?** In the MJ-12 controversy, keeping those questions separate turns an apparently simple argument about “the typewriter” into the more precise forensic issue it actually is.
