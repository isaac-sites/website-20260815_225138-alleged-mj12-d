## Introduction

Later federal archive searches did not merely repeat the FBI’s late-1980s judgement that the publicly circulated Operation Majestic-12 material was bogus. They tested a broader question: **if the MJ-12 papers were authentic products of a highly classified Truman- or Eisenhower-era programme, did the surrounding government record contain the traces that such a programme and its documents should have left?**



<figure class="article-media-figure article-media-figure-illustration"><img src="{{ "/assets/images/alleged_mj12_documen_5d9d42_fbi_mj12_assessment_7b27bd_federal_mj12_archive_eb888c-Illustration-1-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" alt="Archive Cross Check illustration 1" data-theme-src-dark="{{ "/assets/images/alleged_mj12_documen_5d9d42_fbi_mj12_assessment_7b27bd_federal_mj12_archive_eb888c-Illustration-1-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" data-theme-src-light="{{ "/assets/images/alleged_mj12_documen_5d9d42_fbi_mj12_assessment_7b27bd_federal_mj12_archive_eb888c-Illustration-1-light.svg" | relative_url }}" width="1920" height="1080" loading="lazy" decoding="async" fetchpriority="low"><figcaption class="article-media-caption"><span class="article-media-caption-label">Explanatory illustration 1</span></figcaption></figure>

The answer from the National Archives, the Truman and Eisenhower presidential libraries, the [Air Force]({{ 'air-force/' | relative_url }}) and, ultimately, the US General Accounting Office (GAO, now the Government Accountability Office) was consistently negative. Searches covered military records, National Security Council files, classified intelligence holdings and presidential records. The principal anomaly was the so-called Cutler-[Twining]({{ 'twining/' | relative_url }}) memorandum already held by the National Archives, but archivists identified several problems with that document and found no supporting MJ-12 record trail around it. In July 1995, the GAO concluded that there was no evidence that the circulated MJ-12 material consisted of genuine documents created by the executive branch.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gao.gov/products/154832" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gao.gov">[Government Accountability Office]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gao.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Government Accountability Office[Comments on Majestic 12 Material] &#124; U.S. GAOJuly 28, 1995...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 28, 1995</span></span></span>

## What GAO reported about the MJ-12 material

The GAO encountered MJ-12 while conducting a congressionally requested investigation into government records concerning the 1947 Roswell incident. Representative Steven Schiff of New Mexico had asked the agency to investigate what records existed and whether records relating to the incident had been handled properly. The resulting main report, *Government Records: Results of a Search for Records Concerning the 1947 Crash Near Roswell, New Mexico*, was issued on 28 July 1995.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gao.gov/products/nsiad" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gao.gov">[Government Accountability Office]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gao.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">nsiad</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Government Accountability OfficeGovernment Records: Results of a Search for Records Concerning the 1947 Crash Near Roswell, New Mexico &#124;...</span></span></span>

MJ-12 received a separate two-page GAO response on the same date. This is especially useful because it addressed the authenticity question directly rather than attempting to infer authenticity merely from the absence of Roswell records. GAO sought the views of the Information Security Oversight Office, the Office of the Secretary of the Air Force and the National Archives. According to GAO, those bodies said that their knowledge of Majestic 12 came from material supplied by people outside government and that their own files contained no records relating to MJ-12.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gao.gov/products/154832" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gao.gov">[Government Accountability Office]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gao.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Government Accountability Office[Comments on Majestic 12 Material] &#124; U.S. GAOJuly 28, 1995...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 28, 1995</span></span></span>

The resulting judgement was broader than the FBI's earlier treatment of the document it had received. GAO reported that there was **no evidence that the publicly circulated Majestic-12 material represented genuine documents originally produced within the executive branch**. It also reported that the Information Security Oversight Office and Air Force did not consider the material something that should be treated as having genuinely been classified by an executive-branch agency or official. GAO said its own work had uncovered nothing contradicting those conclusions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gao.gov/products/154832" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gao.gov">[Government Accountability Office]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gao.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Government Accountability Office[Comments on Majestic 12 Material] &#124; U.S. GAOJuly 28, 1995...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 28, 1995</span></span></span>

That distinction is important. GAO was not claiming that an archive search could logically prove that no clandestine activity bearing some resemblance to MJ-12 had ever existed. Its finding concerned the **documents and the expected government record behind them**. The searches failed to establish the provenance that the circulated papers implicitly claimed for themselves.

GAO also consulted archivists at the Harry S. Truman and Dwight D. Eisenhower presidential libraries. They reported that repeated searches prompted by public enquiries — including searches of classified intelligence and National Security Council material — had produced neither documents fitting the description of the MJ-12 papers nor references to the supposed designation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gao.gov/products/154832" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gao.gov">[Government Accountability Office]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gao.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Government Accountability Office[Comments on Majestic 12 Material] &#124; U.S. GAOJuly 28, 1995...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 28, 1995</span></span></span>

## What federal archivists actually searched for

The National Archives' MJ-12 reference report provides about why the negative result carries more weight than a simple keyword search. Archivists searched holdings from institutions that should plausibly have intersected with a genuine presidentially connected, military and national-security programme: records of the Secretary of Defense, Joint Chiefs of Staff, Headquarters US Air Force and related files. Enquiries to the Air Force, Joint Chiefs and National Security Council also failed to produce supporting information.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/research/military/air-force/ufos" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives Project BLUE BOOK</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesProject BLUE BOOK - Unidentified Flying Objects &#124; National Archives...</span></span></span>

The National Archives additionally held National Security Council records from the Truman and Eisenhower administrations. Its searches of indexes to NSC policy papers and meeting minutes used not only the obvious terms “MJ-12” and “Majestic”, but related terms including “unidentified flying objects”, “UFO”, “flying saucers”, “extraterrestrial biological entities” and “Aquarius”. Those searches were negative apart from one already controversial item: the purported July 1954 Cutler-Twining memorandum.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/research/military/air-force/ufos" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives Project BLUE BOOK</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesProject BLUE BOOK - Unidentified Flying Objects &#124; National Archives...</span></span></span>

That surviving memo is therefore significant precisely because it did **not** sit within a larger body of corroborating records. Purportedly sent by presidential special assistant Robert Cutler to General Nathan Twining, it referred to an “NSC/MJ-12 Special Studies Project” briefing scheduled for 16 July 1954. Yet the National Archives found no other MJ-12 documents in its folder and no further information about the subject in related departmental records.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/research/military/air-force/ufos" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives Project BLUE BOOK</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesProject BLUE BOOK - Unidentified Flying Objects &#124; National Archives...</span></span></span>

The surrounding record created additional problems:

* The archival series containing the [Cutler-Twining memo]({{ 'cutler-memo/' | relative_url }}) was filed according to [Top Secret register]({{ 'register-numbers/' | relative_url }}) numbers, yet the memo had no such number.
* The National Security Council's Freedom of Information office advised that one security marking appearing on the document was not used by the NSC until the Nixon administration, while the Eisenhower Library likewise said it was not an Eisenhower-era marking.
* The National Archives found no NSC meeting on 16 July 1954 and no mention of either “MJ-12” or “Majestic” in the NSC meeting minutes for that month.
* Eisenhower's appointment books contained no entry for the supposed 16 July briefing, although the presidential library noted that even off-the-record meetings normally generated appointment-book entries.
* Most strikingly, archival records showed that Cutler was visiting military installations in Europe and North Africa from 3 to 15 July 1954. He was therefore overseas on 14 July, the date on which the disputed memorandum purports to have been issued under his name.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/research/military/air-force/ufos" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives Project BLUE BOOK</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesProject BLUE BOOK - Unidentified Flying Objects &#124; National Archives...</span></span></span>

These details transformed the archive search from a simple “nothing found” result into a cross-check against the document's own claims. A purported memorandum can resemble an official document in isolation; once placed alongside registers, meeting records, appointment books, contemporary security practices, the author's travel schedule and neighbouring files, inconsistencies become much more consequential.

The National Archives also makes an important qualification about the [Cutler-Twining paper]({{ 'paper-test/' | relative_url }})'s presence in its collection. Archival [custody]({{ 'custody/' | relative_url }}) is not authentication. When the Archives certifies a reproduction, it certifies that the reproduction faithfully represents a document in its custody; it does **not** certify that the document itself, or the information written on it, is genuine.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/research/military/air-force/ufos" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives Project BLUE BOOK</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesProject BLUE BOOK - Unidentified Flying Objects &#124; National Archives...</span></span></span>

## A second apparent MJ-12 record failed the same test

The GAO review produced another useful cross-check because investigators encountered a separate document apparently bearing an MJ-12 reference. Among material previously supplied to the Information Security Oversight Office was a message dated 17 November 1980 that appeared to originate with the Operations Division of the Air Force Office of Special Investigations (AFOSI) and contained the words “[MJ Twelve]({{ 'mj-twelve/' | relative_url }})”.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gao.gov/products/154832" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gao.gov">[Government Accountability Office]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gao.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Government Accountability Office[Comments on Majestic 12 Material] &#124; U.S. GAOJuly 28, 1995...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 28, 1995</span></span></span>

Rather than treating the apparent official origin as evidence of authenticity, GAO asked AFOSI to verify it against its own records. In February 1995, the commander of AFOSI's Investigative Operations Center reported that a search had found no official file copy of the message. AFOSI had also examined the message during an earlier Freedom of Information Act enquiry and had concluded that it was a forgery.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gao.gov/products/154832" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gao.gov">[Government Accountability Office]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gao.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Government Accountability Office[Comments on Majestic 12 Material] &#124; U.S. GAOJuly 28, 1995...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 28, 1995</span></span></span>

This episode closely parallels the logic behind the FBI's earlier MJ-12 inquiry. An apparently classified or official-looking document was not accepted at face value; investigators checked it with the agency from which it supposedly originated. In both instances, that provenance check failed. The 1980 message therefore did not provide the independent government corroboration that might have weakened the FBI's assessment.

## Why the missing record trail matters


<div class="content-callout content-callout--key content-callout--takeaway" markdown="1">

The archival case against the circulated MJ-12 material is cumulative rather than dependent upon one missing file. A genuinely presidentially authorised operation involving senior military and national-security officials could potentially leave records in numerous places even if its central operational files remained classified or had later been destroyed: meeting calendars, correspondence registers, National Security Council indexes, administrative memoranda, classification records, related military files or references in presidential holdings.

</div>

Federal archivists looked across several of those record systems and found no such supporting network. The National Archives says it made extensive searches of Air Force and Joint Chiefs records; the presidential libraries searched their holdings; National Security Council indexes and meeting minutes were checked under multiple relevant terms; and GAO subsequently canvassed the agencies responsible for archives, security oversight and Air Force records.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/research/military/air-force/ufos" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives Project BLUE BOOK</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesProject BLUE BOOK - Unidentified Flying Objects &#124; National Archives...</span></span></span>

This does not turn archival silence into absolute proof of non-existence. Secret programmes can generate incomplete records, material can remain classified, and government files can be lost or destroyed. The 1995 GAO Roswell investigation itself found a genuine records-management anomaly: some Roswell Army Air Field administrative records from the relevant period had been destroyed without documentation explaining the authority for their disposal. That illustrates why an absence of records has to be interpreted cautiously rather than treated automatically as proof.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gao.gov/products/nsiad" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gao.gov">[Government Accountability Office]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gao.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">nsiad</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Government Accountability OfficeGovernment Records: Results of a Search for Records Concerning the 1947 Crash Near Roswell, New Mexico &#124;...</span></span></span>

MJ-12 presents a stronger problem for authenticity, however, because the issue is not merely that investigators failed to locate one expected master file. The publicly circulated documents claim an executive-branch and national-security provenance, yet searches across the institutional environments associated with that claimed provenance failed to produce corroborating records. Meanwhile, the unusual Cutler-Twining memo conflicted with several features of the contemporary record, and another supposed official “MJ Twelve” communication was separately judged a forgery by the agency it purported to come from.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/research/military/air-force/ufos" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives Project BLUE BOOK</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesProject BLUE BOOK - Unidentified Flying Objects &#124; National Archives...</span></span></span>

## How this reinforced the FBI's finding

The later archive work strengthens the FBI assessment mainly through **independent convergence**. The FBI's 1988 inquiry was comparatively narrow: faced with apparently classified MJ-12 material, it consulted the Air Force Office of Special Investigations and accepted the Air Force determination that the document was fabricated. The later federal work approached the problem from a different direction — looking for the documentary ecosystem that genuine executive-branch MJ-12 records ought to inhabit.

It did not find one. By 1995, GAO could report that the National Archives, Air Force and Information Security Oversight Office had found no records supporting the circulated material, that the Truman and Eisenhower libraries had found no corroboration in presidential, intelligence or NSC holdings, and that GAO itself had uncovered nothing contradicting the agencies' conclusion that the papers were not genuine executive-branch documents.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gao.gov/products/154832" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gao.gov">[Government Accountability Office]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gao.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Government Accountability Office[Comments on Majestic 12 Material] &#124; U.S. GAOJuly 28, 1995...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 28, 1995</span></span></span>

The National Archives' more granular findings make that conclusion particularly difficult to dismiss as a single agency simply repeating another's opinion. The purported Cutler memorandum lacked the expected surrounding MJ-12 records; searches found no corresponding NSC meeting; Eisenhower's calendar supplied no matching presidential engagement; contemporary files placed Cutler overseas when the memo was supposedly issued; and aspects of its markings and physical format did not fit the documentary practices archivists found in comparable records.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/research/military/air-force/ufos" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives Project BLUE BOOK</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesProject BLUE BOOK - Unidentified Flying Objects &#124; National Archives...</span></span></span>

That is the most precise way to understand the later federal evidence. It did not establish the sweeping proposition that every conceivable story about a secret UFO programme had been disproved. It did something narrower and more directly relevant to the FBI file: **repeated searches of the government record failed to validate the claimed official provenance of the publicly circulated MJ-12 documents and instead uncovered additional reasons to doubt it**. In that sense, the National Archives and GAO supplied the broader archival cross-check that the FBI's original, limited investigation had not itself undertaken.
