## Introduction

The Cutler–Twining memorandum, dated 14 July 1954 and referring to an “NSC/MJ-12 Special Studies Project”, has an unusually concrete authenticity problem: its security marking appears not to fit the National Security Council (NSC) practice of the period. The National Archives records the disputed wording as “Top Secret [Restricted]({{ 'restricted/' | relative_url }}) Information” and says the NSC’s Freedom of Information Office reported that this marking did not enter NSC use until the Nixon administration. The Eisenhower [Presidential Library]({{ 'library-searches/' | relative_url }}) separately confirmed that the particular marking was not used during Eisenhower’s presidency.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/research/military/air-force/ufos" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives Project BLUE BOOK</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesProject BLUE BOOK - Unidentified Flying Objects &#124; National ArchivesJune 25, 2024...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 25, 2024</span></span></span>

There is, however, a genuine wrinkle. Later General Accounting Office research reportedly encountered period documents described as “Top Secret Restricted”, giving MJ-12 defender [Stanton Friedman]({{ 'friedman-s-case/' | relative_url }}) grounds to challenge overly broad claims that such words never appeared together anywhere in government. That does not resolve the narrower and more important question: whether the full marking on the Cutler–[Twining]({{ 'twining/' | relative_url }}) memorandum was appropriate for an NSC document supposedly created in July 1954.

## What marking appears on the memorandum

Published reproductions of the Cutler–Twining memorandum show its heading as separate lines containing “TOP SECRET”, “RESTRICTED” and “SECURITY INFORMATION”. The National Archives’ own MJ-12 reference report compresses the relevant wording into “Top Secret Restricted Information”. The distinction is worth preserving because arguments about the document sometimes turn on whether “Restricted” is being treated as a second classification, a handling restriction or merely part of an old-style stamp.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/research/military/air-force/ufos" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives Project BLUE BOOK</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesProject BLUE BOOK - Unidentified Flying Objects &#124; National ArchivesJune 25, 2024...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 25, 2024</span></span></span>

The date is crucial. Executive Order 10501 was issued by President Dwight Eisenhower on 5 November 1953 and took effect on 15 December. Section 1 restricted ordinary national-defence classification to three categories: **Top Secret, Secret and Confidential**. It further provided that no other designation was to be used to classify defence information, except where expressly authorised by statute.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.presidency.ucsb.edu/documents/executive-order-10501-safeguarding-official-information-the-interests-the-defense-the" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: presidency.ucsb.edu">[The American Presidency Project]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">presidency.ucsb.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The American Presidency ProjectExecutive Order 10501—Safeguarding Official Information in the Interests of the Defense of the United Stat...</span></span></span>

That change displaced an earlier system in which “Restricted” had been a legitimate classification. Contemporary government records show the transition happening explicitly. A December 1953 Army document stated that Executive Order 10501 eliminated “RESTRICTED Security Information” from 15 December 1953 and provided procedures for reviewing older material still bearing the superseded marking.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nsa.gov/Portals/70/documents/news-features/declassified-documents/friedman-documents/panel-committee-board/FOLDER_380/41764399080352.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nsa.gov">[nsa.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nsa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">REF ID:A58004April 13, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 13, 2026</span></span></span> A later Department of Defense security review likewise described “Restricted-Security Information” as a category used before Executive Order 10501 and eliminated by that order.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nsa.gov/Portals/75/documents/news-features/declassified-documents/friedman-documents/reports-research/FOLDER_051/41708959074831.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nsa.gov">[nsa.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nsa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">REPORT OF REVIEW OF NATIONAL SECURITY AGENCY SECURITY PROCEDURES AND STATUSMarch 26, 2018...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 26, 2018</span></span></span>

The disappearance of “Security Information” is equally important. A CIA notice implementing the new order told employees that those words, which had previously followed classifications, would no longer be used. It went so far as to direct offices to remove “Security Information” from existing rubber stamps and obliterate the words on pre-printed forms as supplies were used up.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cia.gov/readingroom/document/cia-rdp78-04718a000800290035-6" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cia.gov">[CIA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cia.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">SAFEGUARDING OFFICIAL INFORMATION IN THE INTERESTS OF THE DEFENSE OF THE UNITED STATES &#124; CIA FOIA (foia.cia.gov)...</span></span></span> Another contemporary intelligence record summarised the new position succinctly: there would be no further use of either “Restricted” or “Security Information”, and classified information would instead fall into the three categories Top Secret, Secret and Confidential.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cia.gov/readingroom/document/cia-rdp78-05597a000400030043-3" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cia.gov">[CIA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cia.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">NIS COMMITTEE MEETING NO. 158 &#124; CIA FOIA (foia.cia.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NIS COMMITTEE MEETING NO. 158 &#124; CIA FOIA (foia.cia.gov</span></span></span>

Those instructions make timing the central issue. Genuine documents from **before** the December 1953 change can readily display combinations such as “Top Secret” and “Security Information”. For example, an official State Department historical edition identifies a June 1953 intelligence document as “Top Secret. Security Information”.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1950-55Intel/ch1?start=121" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: history.state.gov">[Office of the Historian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">history.state.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Office of the Historian Historical Documents</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Office of the Historian Historical Documents</span></span></span> An NSC memorandum of 25 September 1953 likewise visibly carries “TOP SECRET” and “SECURITY INFORMATION”.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/4/41/Report_to_the_National_Security_Council_by_the_Continental_Defense_Committee_on_Continental_Defense%2C_Part_5_of_7_-_DPLA_-_68f81c3b20144966beb27f4fdf98665a.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: upload.wikimedia.org">[Wikimedia Commons]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">upload.wikimedia.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wikimedia.org.</span></span></span> Such examples demonstrate that the terminology itself is historically real; they do not explain its appearance on a document supposedly created seven months after the terminology was discontinued.

## What the archivists reported

The strongest criticism comes from the institution holding the document rather than from later UFO writers. The National Archives’ MJ-12 reference report says its staff asked the National Security Council about the disputed marking. According to the Archives, the NSC Freedom of Information Office advised that “Top Secret Restricted Information” did not come into use at the NSC until the Nixon administration. The Eisenhower Presidential Library also confirmed that the particular marking was not used during the Eisenhower administration.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/research/military/air-force/ufos" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives Project BLUE BOOK</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesProject BLUE BOOK - Unidentified Flying Objects &#124; National ArchivesJune 25, 2024...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 25, 2024</span></span></span>

This is a narrower and more useful finding than the sweeping proposition that no US government office ever put “Top Secret” beside some form of “Restricted” during the 1950s. The archival claim concerns **NSC practice and this particular marking**. That matters because the memorandum presents itself as presidential national-security correspondence: it is ostensibly from Robert Cutler, Eisenhower’s Special Assistant to the President, concerns an “NSC/MJ-12” project and purports to alter arrangements for a [White House]({{ 'white-house/' | relative_url }}) briefing.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/research/military/air-force/ufos" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives Project BLUE BOOK</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesProject BLUE BOOK - Unidentified Flying Objects &#124; National ArchivesJune 25, 2024...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 25, 2024</span></span></span>

The classification anomaly also does not stand alone in the Archives’ examination of the document. The Cutler–Twining sheet was found in Record Group 341, entry 267, a series filed by Top Secret register number, yet the memorandum itself bears no such number. No other “NSC/MJ-12” material was found in its folder. Searches of related records at the Department of Defense, Joint Chiefs of Staff, Air Force and NSC produced no corroborating documentation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/research/military/air-force/ufos" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives Project BLUE BOOK</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesProject BLUE BOOK - Unidentified Flying Objects &#124; National ArchivesJune 25, 2024...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 25, 2024</span></span></span>

That filing anomaly is especially relevant to the marking question. A security heading is not simply decorative typography: classified paperwork normally existed within systems of registration, accountability, distribution and later declassification. Here, the wording at the top of the page and the absence of the [register number]({{ 'register-number/' | relative_url }}) expected in the surrounding record series both create friction with the administrative environment in which the memorandum supposedly operated.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/research/military/air-force/ufos" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives Project BLUE BOOK</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesProject BLUE BOOK - Unidentified Flying Objects &#124; National ArchivesJune 25, 2024...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 25, 2024</span></span></span>

The National Archives nevertheless makes an important distinction about what its custody proves. It states that certification under the Archives' seal means a reproduction is a true copy of a document in its custody; it **does not authenticate the document or the information it contains**. Finding the Cutler–Twining sheet in the National Archives therefore establishes provenance to an archival box, not that Robert Cutler or the NSC genuinely created it in 1954.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/research/military/air-force/ufos" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives Project BLUE BOOK</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesProject BLUE BOOK - Unidentified Flying Objects &#124; National ArchivesJune 25, 2024...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 25, 2024</span></span></span>

## The “Top Secret Restricted” counterargument

The security-marking objection is not entirely uncontested. Stanton Friedman, one of the principal defenders of the early MJ-12 documents, argued that government records existed with “Top Secret Restricted” markings from the relevant period. He cited notes produced during the General Accounting Office’s 1990s Roswell investigation. A surviving reproduction of those notes says that, during a 7 December 1994 examination of Air Force atomic-energy and weapons-requirements records covering 1948–56, GAO investigators noticed “Top Secret Restricted” on several documents and specifically remarked that they had previously been told such a classification was not used during that period.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gbppr.net/ufoupdates/pdf/1999-12.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gbppr.net">[GBPPR]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gbppr.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on gbppr.net.</span></span></span>

Friedman consequently argued that the National Archives objection had been disproved. He maintained that the GAO personnel had seen the period examples themselves and that the underlying documents remained classified, preventing him from supplying copies.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gbppr.net/ufoupdates/pdf/2002-12.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gbppr.net">[GBPPR]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gbppr.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on gbppr.net.</span></span></span> This is a relevant counterargument because it cautions against treating bureaucratic marking systems as perfectly uniform or claiming that the words “Top Secret Restricted” could never occur together in a 1950s federal record.

But it does not neatly authenticate the Cutler–Twining marking. The GAO records in question concerned **Air Force atomic-energy and weapons requirements**, not demonstrated NSC memoranda. That distinction matters because nuclear information had its own statutory terminology. “[Restricted Data]({{ 'restricted-data/' | relative_url }})” was a specialised category governing atomic information and could legitimately be combined with a normal classification — official historical records, for example, describe documents as “Top Secret, Restricted Data”.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://history.state.gov//historicaldocuments/frus1955-57v19/d87" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: history.state.gov">[Office of the Historian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">history.state.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Office of the Historian Historical Documents</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Office of the Historian Historical Documents</span></span></span> The available GAO note says “Top Secret Restricted”, but without the underlying classified documents it is difficult to establish exactly what markings those records bore or what “Restricted” meant in each case.

More importantly, even proof of genuine 1954 Air Force documents bearing “Top Secret Restricted” would answer only the broadest objection. It would not by itself overturn the National Archives’ specific report that the **NSC did not use the Cutler–Twining marking during the Eisenhower administration**. Nor would it explain the residual “Security Information” wording, which contemporary implementation documents say was discontinued when Executive Order 10501 took effect.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/research/military/air-force/ufos" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives Project BLUE BOOK</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesProject BLUE BOOK - Unidentified Flying Objects &#124; National ArchivesJune 25, 2024...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 25, 2024</span></span></span>

The counterargument therefore narrows the conclusion rather than eliminating the anomaly. It is unsafe to reason, “the word Restricted appears, therefore the memorandum must be fake”. The better test is institutional and chronological: **does this exact combination, on this kind of NSC-originated memorandum, fit documented NSC practice in July 1954?** On the presently available evidence, the archival answer remains unfavourable.

## Why the mismatch matters for authenticity


<div class="content-callout content-callout--key content-callout--takeaway" markdown="1">

Security markings are useful authentication evidence because they are mundane. A fabricated secret document can imitate names, dates and dramatic language from public history, but administrative conventions change over time and differ among agencies. A convincing period document must reproduce not only what officials might have said but also how their office classified, registered and handled what they wrote.

</div>

The Cutler–Twining memorandum falls awkwardly across a well-documented transition. “Restricted” and “Security Information” are not invented phrases; both belonged to earlier US security practice. The problem is that Executive Order 10501 had already replaced that system before the memorandum’s supposed creation. Contemporary implementation records show agencies actively removing the obsolete wording from stamps and forms.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/files/declassification/pidb/recommendations/transforming-classification.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[archives.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives TRANSFORMING</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Archives TRANSFORMING</span></span></span> That makes an old-style-looking marking in July 1954 more significant than a mere variation in punctuation or formatting.

At the same time, the marking should not be treated as a mathematical proof of forgery. Old forms and stamps could survive administrative changes; federal security practice was not necessarily uniform; specialised statutory markings such as Restricted Data existed outside the ordinary three-level scheme; and the later GAO observation shows that “Top Secret Restricted” appeared somewhere in period Air Force holdings. Those qualifications are sufficient to reject the simplistic claim that the individual words were impossible in 1954.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gbppr.net/ufoupdates/pdf/1999-12.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gbppr.net">[GBPPR]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gbppr.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on gbppr.net.</span></span></span>

They do not, however, supply the evidence needed to make the Cutler–Twining usage normal. The strongest authentication evidence would be a securely proven **Eisenhower-era NSC document**, created after 15 December 1953, carrying the same “Top Secret Restricted Information” or equivalent marking under comparable circumstances. The National Archives reports the opposite institutional finding: the NSC told its archivists that this marking belonged to a later period, while the Eisenhower Library said it was not used during Eisenhower’s administration.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/research/military/air-force/ufos" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives Project BLUE BOOK</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesProject BLUE BOOK - Unidentified Flying Objects &#124; National ArchivesJune 25, 2024...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 25, 2024</span></span></span>

That leaves the disputed marking as a substantial authenticity warning rather than a stand-alone verdict. Its importance comes from the convergence of three specific points: the formal classification rules had changed before July 1954; contemporary agencies were explicitly retiring “Restricted” and “Security Information”; and archivists checking the very institution from which the memorandum purportedly came reported that the marking did not match Eisenhower-era NSC practice.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.presidency.ucsb.edu/documents/executive-order-10501-safeguarding-official-information-the-interests-the-defense-the" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: presidency.ucsb.edu">[ucsb.edu]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">presidency.ucsb.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The American Presidency ProjectExecutive Order 10501—Safeguarding Official Information in the Interests of the Defense of the United Stat...</span></span></span>

For the Cutler–Twining memorandum, that is the key evidential distinction. The question is not whether authentic US documents can be found somewhere with superficially similar words. They can. The question is whether the complete security treatment makes sense **on this document, from this office, on 14 July 1954**. The available official evidence gives good reason to doubt that it does.
