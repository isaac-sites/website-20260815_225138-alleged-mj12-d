## Introduction

The odd date style in the alleged MJ-12 Eisenhower Briefing Document is a legitimate reason to ask questions, but it is not, by itself, a reliable [forgery test]({{ 'forgery-test/' | relative_url }}). The document repeatedly uses forms such as “18 November, 1952” and dates with leading zeroes such as “07 July, 1947”. Critics, notably Philip J. Klass, argued that these hybrid forms departed from the normal American military convention and therefore counted against authenticity.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://skepticalinquirer.org/wp-content/uploads/sites/29/2019/03/Issue-02-10.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: skepticalinquirer.org">[Skeptical Inquirer]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">skepticalinquirer.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Issue</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Skeptical InquirerTHE BRAIN &amp; CONSCIOUSNESSJune 23, 2006 — &quot; Yet the Hillenkoetter document con- tains the following: &quot;01 August, 1950&quot; a...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 23, 2006</span></span></span>



<figure class="article-media-figure article-media-figure-illustration"><img src="{{ "/assets/images/alleged_mj12_documen_5d9d42_anomaly_vs_falsifica_7cdc03_mj12_date_format_obj_4125aa-Illustration-1-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" alt="Date Formats illustration 1" data-theme-src-dark="{{ "/assets/images/alleged_mj12_documen_5d9d42_anomaly_vs_falsifica_7cdc03_mj12_date_format_obj_4125aa-Illustration-1-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" data-theme-src-light="{{ "/assets/images/alleged_mj12_documen_5d9d42_anomaly_vs_falsifica_7cdc03_mj12_date_format_obj_4125aa-Illustration-1-light.svg" | relative_url }}" width="1920" height="1080" loading="lazy" decoding="async" fetchpriority="low"><figcaption class="article-media-caption"><span class="article-media-caption-label">Explanatory illustration 1</span></figcaption></figure>

The difficulty is the jump from **atypical** to **impossible**. Genuine government records from the period show that dates were not rendered with perfect uniformity across agencies, offices and document types. MJ-12 defender [Stanton Friedman]({{ 'friedman-s-case/' | relative_url }}) consequently produced archival counterexamples and argued that senior officials' paperwork could exhibit several formats.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.theblackvault.com/casefiles/update-on-operation-majestic-12-documents/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theblackvault.com">[The Black Vault]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theblackvault.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Black Vault Update on Operation Majestic 12 Documents</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Black VaultUpdate on Operation Majestic 12 Documents - The Black Vault Case FilesJanuary 17, 2024...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 17, 2024</span></span></span> That does not make the MJ-12 briefing genuine. It means only that punctuation and date styling are strongest as comparative clues, not stand-alone chronological falsifiers.

## The date-format objection

The disputed briefing document purports to have been prepared for President-elect Dwight D. Eisenhower on 18 November 1952, with former CIA director Rear Admiral Roscoe H. [Hillenkoetter]({{ 'hillenkoetter/' | relative_url }}) identified as the briefing officer. Transcriptions reproduce the heading date as “18 NOVEMBER, 1952” and the body contains similarly constructed dates.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/MJ-12_Document" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: en.wikisource.org">[Wikisource]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">en.wikisource.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">MJ-12 Document</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">MJ-12 Document - Wikisource, the free online libraryNovember 18, 1952...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 18, 1952</span></span></span>

Klass's objection was more specific than simply saying that the typography looked strange. American civilian correspondence commonly put the month first — “November 18, 1952” — while military-style dating put the day first and omitted the comma — “18 November 1952”. The MJ-12 construction “18 November, 1952” combines the day-month-year ordering of the latter with the comma associated with the former. Klass therefore described it as an unusual hybrid.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://inquiry201.rssing.com/chan-16157765/all_p1.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: inquiry201.rssing.com">[Skeptical Inquirer]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">inquiry201.rssing.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Skeptical InquirerSkeptical Inquirer - Committee for Skeptical Inquiry...</span></span></span>

He also objected to **leading zeroes** on one-digit days. The briefing includes forms such as “07 July, 1947”, “01 August, 1950” and “06 December, 1950”. Klass reported that his examination of military and CIA material from the 1950s through the 1970s showed the conventional documentary form without that zero, such as “1 August 1950”. He further argued that the MJ-12 style resembled a date convention used in correspondence by William Moore, one of the researchers associated with bringing the documents to public attention.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://skepticalinquirer.org/wp-content/uploads/sites/29/2019/03/Issue-02-10.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: skepticalinquirer.org">[Skeptical Inquirer]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">skepticalinquirer.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Issue</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Skeptical InquirerTHE BRAIN &amp; CONSCIOUSNESSJune 23, 2006 — &quot; Yet the Hillenkoetter document con- tains the following: &quot;01 August, 1950&quot; a...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 23, 2006</span></span></span>

That combination gives the objection genuine evidential weight. If a questioned document consistently differs from comparable authentic papers while resembling the habits of somebody connected with its modern provenance, an examiner should investigate the resemblance. But the strength of that inference depends on establishing how rigid the supposed historical convention really was.

## Genuine papers were not perfectly uniform

This is where the simple date-format argument encounters trouble. Genuine government paperwork does demonstrate considerable variation, sometimes even among documents produced close together in time.

The CIA's own declassified holdings contain several documents dated **18 November 1952**, the very day claimed for the MJ-12 briefing. Examples include a memorandum to the Chief of Plans and Programs Staff, another memorandum to the Director of Central Intelligence and an Operational Security Course memorandum. The searchable records render their dates as “18 November 1952”, supporting Klass on what the normal CIA form looked like.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cia.gov/readingroom/docs/CIA-RDP55-00037A000100030129-2.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cia.gov">[CIA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cia.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">18 NOVEMBER 195218 November 1952. MEMORANDUM FOR: Chief, Plans and Programs Staff. THRU. SUBJECT: Deputy for Covert Training. Weekly A...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 1952</span></span></span> An authentic 1947 delegation of authority signed by Hillenkoetter likewise provides a useful comparator: the CIA record identifies the document as dated “24 July 1947” and shows his official signature block as “R. H. HILLENKOETTER, Rear Admiral, USN, Director of Central Intelligence.”<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cia.gov/readingroom/docs/CIA-RDP54-00177A000200050136-7.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cia.gov">[CIA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cia.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cia.gov.</span></span></span>

So the criticism cannot simply be waved away. There really is a recognisable period pattern against which the MJ-12 form looks peculiar.

But “recognisable pattern” is not the same thing as “exceptionless rule”. Friedman argued that archival research at the Truman and Eisenhower presidential libraries turned up numerous date forms in classified and [restricted]({{ 'restricted/' | relative_url }})-distribution records. His particularly useful example concerned three short memoranda from CIA Director Allen Dulles to [White House]({{ 'white-house/' | relative_url }}) staff secretary Andrew Goodpaster within roughly ten days in November 1956. Friedman reported the dates appearing respectively as “12 November 1956”, “November 20, 1956”, and a rubber-stamped “NOV 22 1956”. More importantly for the narrow MJ-12 dispute, Friedman claimed to have located examples involving both Hillenkoetter and another former Director of Central Intelligence, Walter Bedell Smith, using the disputed day-month-comma-year construction.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://medium.com/stanton-friedmans-articles/update-on-operation-majestic-12-documents-ed2df58fd8a1" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: medium.com">[Medium]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">medium.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UPDATE ON OPERATION MAJESTIC 12 DOCUMENTS &#124; by Richard Geldreich, Jr. &#124; Physicist Stanton Friedman’s Articles &#124; Medium...</span></span></span> That is a direct challenge to the strongest version of the date argument. If authentic material associated with the relevant officials can contain the supposedly forbidden punctuation, then the proposition “no genuine paper could look like this” fails even if “this was not the normal format” remains true.

There is an important limitation. Friedman's evidence is an advocate's rebuttal rather than an official authentication of the MJ-12 papers, and critics have disputed whether some of his comparators are sufficiently similar in document type and institutional setting. ## A date format has to be compared like with like

That distinction is crucial in historical document examination. A date appearing in a formal memorandum, a rubber date stamp, a telegram, a filing annotation and a secretary's typed covering note need not obey the same convention. Showing that an unusual form existed somewhere in government therefore does not automatically explain its appearance in a particular questioned document.

Conversely, demonstrating that most surviving documents of the relevant class follow one convention does not prove that every genuine document had to follow it. The CIA's declassified records around November 1952 illustrate the normality of “18 November 1952”, but normality establishes a baseline, not an absolute prohibition.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cia.gov/readingroom/docs/CIA-RDP55-00037A000100030129-2.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cia.gov">[CIA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cia.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">18 NOVEMBER 195218 November 1952. The leading-zero issue deserves the same treatment. “07 July, 1947” is more conspicuous than a misplaced comma because it combines two deviations: a two-digit day and the comma. Klass treated that combination as historically suspicious.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://skepticalinquirer.org/wp-content/uploads/sites/29/2019/03/Issue-02-10.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: skepticalinquirer.org">[Skeptical Inquirer]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">skepticalinquirer.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Issue</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Skeptical InquirerTHE BRAIN &amp; CONSCIOUSNESSJune 23, 2006 — &quot; Yet the Hillenkoetter document con- tains the following: &quot;01 August, 1950&quot; a...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 23, 2006</span></span></span> Yet demonstrating that leading zeroes occurred in military communications or some administrative contexts would establish only that the notation existed, not that it belonged naturally in a presidential briefing document. Critics of Friedman's defence have made exactly this point, arguing that message traffic and ordinary correspondence are not necessarily equivalent document classes.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cufos.org/PDFs/books/The_Roswell_ReportR2.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cufos.org">[Center for UFO Studies]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cufos.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Center for UFO Studies&lt;</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Greorge M. Eberhart Editor CENTER FOR UFO STUDIOctober 31, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 31, 2025</span></span></span>

The useful question is therefore not “Can I find this date style anywhere?” but:

**How frequently does this style occur in authentic documents produced by the same institution, at roughly the same time, for the same administrative purpose, and preferably by the same office or official?**

That formulation turns a superficial anomaly into a historical-comparative test.

## Atypical is not the same as impossible

For an anomaly to become a strong falsification test, investigators need more than evidence that clerks *usually* did something differently. They need a chronological or institutional constraint strong enough to exclude the questioned feature.

Date punctuation rarely reaches that threshold because it is produced by people, typewriters, office habits and sometimes later stamps or annotations. Even within the federal government, surviving records exhibit different conventions. The State Department's published historical record, for example, renders a genuine 1952 memorandum as “Washington, November 26, 1952”, while contemporary CIA memoranda commonly use day-month-year ordering such as “18 November 1952”.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1951-54IranEd2/d148" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: history.state.gov">[Office of the Historian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">history.state.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Office of the Historian Historical Documents</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Office of the Historian Historical Documents</span></span></span> The contrast is a reminder that “government style” was not one universal typographical system.

This leads to three different conclusions that should not be confused.

**First, the MJ-12 dating is unusual.** The comma in “18 November, 1952” does not match the conventional military/CIA examples readily visible from the period, and the leading zeroes make the pattern more distinctive. Klass was justified in treating it as something requiring explanation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://skepticalinquirer.org/wp-content/uploads/sites/29/2019/03/Issue-02-10.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: skepticalinquirer.org">[Skeptical Inquirer]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">skepticalinquirer.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Issue</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Skeptical InquirerTHE BRAIN &amp; CONSCIOUSNESSJune 23, 2006 — &quot; Yet the Hillenkoetter document con- tains the following: &quot;01 August, 1950&quot; a...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 23, 2006</span></span></span>

**Second, unusual formatting can contribute to a cumulative forgery case.** If a questioned document contains numerous departures from the practices of its claimed author and office, and those departures align with practices associated with a possible modern source, their combined evidential value can exceed that of any single anomaly. Klass's reported comparison with Moore's correspondence therefore cannot be dismissed merely because the punctuation alone is non-conclusive.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.debunker.com/historical/KlassMJ12.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: debunker.com">[Debunker]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">debunker.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Klass MJ12</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Debunker&lt;__:&#x27;.(_29 Aug 1985 — on Lhe &quot;MJ-12 Hillenkoetter papels&quot; released by william L. **Third, the date format alone does not establish fabrication.** Friedman's archival counterexamples attack the categorical proposition that authentic high-level government papers were invariably formatted one way. His evidence does not prove that Hillenkoetter prepared the MJ-12 briefing; it narrows what can legitimately be inferred from the date.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.theblackvault.com/casefiles/update-on-operation-majestic-12-documents/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theblackvault.com">[The Black Vault]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theblackvault.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Black Vault Update on Operation Majestic 12 Documents</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Black VaultUpdate on Operation Majestic 12 Documents - The Black Vault Case FilesJanuary 17, 2024...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 17, 2024</span></span></span>

That last distinction is particularly important because the wider MJ-12 authenticity question does not depend on winning or losing this one argument. Official investigations and archival assessments have raised other, independently serious objections to alleged MJ-12 material. Weakening the date-format objection therefore does not reverse those broader findings. It simply prevents one comparatively soft anomaly from being promoted into a conclusive test that it cannot support.

## What the odd date can actually tell us

The safest assessment is that “18 November, 1952” belongs in the category of **probabilistic evidence**. It asks whether the questioned briefing looks statistically and administratively like documents from its claimed environment. The more closely matched genuine comparators an investigator collects, the more informative the discrepancy becomes.

But a conclusive falsifier requires something different: a feature that could not have existed when the document supposedly originated, or could not have arisen through a plausible contemporary process. The date-format dispute has not established such a barrier. Authentic records confirm that “18 November 1952” was a normal CIA form in 1952, while the archival variation cited by Friedman makes it unsafe to elevate punctuation conventions into immutable rules.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cia.gov/readingroom/docs/CIA-RDP55-00037A000100030129-2.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cia.gov">[CIA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cia.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">18 NOVEMBER 195218 November 1952. MEMORANDUM FOR: Chief, Plans and Programs Staff. THRU. SUBJECT: Deputy for Covert Training. Weekly A...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 1952</span></span></span>

The result is deliberately narrower than either side of the MJ-12 controversy might prefer. The briefing document's dates **are anomalous enough to investigate and compare**, particularly the repeated comma and leading zeroes. They are not, on the evidence available, an independent demonstration that the document must have been produced after 1952. In historical authentication, that is the essential dividing line: a convention tells investigators what they should expect; a genuine chronological impossibility tells them what could not have happened.
