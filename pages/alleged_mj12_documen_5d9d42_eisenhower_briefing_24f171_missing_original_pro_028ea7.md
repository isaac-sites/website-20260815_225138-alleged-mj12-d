## Introduction

The most basic problem with the alleged 1952 Eisenhower Briefing Document is not hidden in its typography or its extraordinary claims. It is that nobody has produced the government paper that was supposedly created in 1952. What entered the public record was an anonymous roll of undeveloped 35 mm film mailed to television producer and UFO researcher Jaime Shandera in December 1984. The sender was unidentified, and no documented trail connected the film to the [White House]({{ 'white-house/' | relative_url }}), intelligence agencies, the military or any government archive. Contemporary reporting described the source simply as anonymous.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.washingtonpost.com/archive/lifestyle/1987/06/29/secrets-in-the-saucers/9fdff3b3-462f-4c33-b79f-a29deea66dec/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: washingtonpost.com">[The Washington Post]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">washingtonpost.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Washington Post SECRETS IN THE SAUCERS</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Washington PostSECRETS IN THE SAUCERS - The Washington PostJune 29, 1987...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 29, 1987</span></span></span>

That distinction matters. A photograph can preserve the visible appearance of a document, and copies can still be analysed for wording, layout and some reproduction characteristics. But photographs cannot substitute for the alleged original when investigators want to examine its paper, ink, impressions, alterations and other physical properties. Modern forensic-document guidance explicitly prefers originals for precisely these reasons.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://nij.ojp.gov/topics/articles/overview-questioned-documents" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nij.ojp.gov">[ojp.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nij.ojp.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Institute of JusticeArchived &#124; Overview of Questioned Documents &#124; National Institute of JusticeOctober 1, 2012...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 1, 2012</span></span></span>

The missing original therefore creates two separate weaknesses in the MJ-12 authenticity claim: important physical tests cannot be performed, and the document has no independently verifiable chain of [custody]({{ 'custody/' | relative_url }}) leading back to the government institution said to have created it.

## How the 35 mm film reached Jaime Shandera

The provenance that can actually be documented begins in California in December 1984, not in Washington in November 1952. Shandera was working with William L. Moore, co-author of *The Roswell Incident*, when an anonymous package arrived containing undeveloped film. Accounts consistently identify an Albuquerque, New Mexico postmark and the absence of a return address. After development, the film showed the papers subsequently presented as [the Eisenhower Briefing Document]({{ 'the-eisenhower-briefing-document/' | relative_url }}) and the accompanying Truman-[Forrestal]({{ 'forrestal/' | relative_url }}) memorandum.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.washingtonpost.com/archive/lifestyle/1987/06/29/secrets-in-the-saucers/9fdff3b3-462f-4c33-b79f-a29deea66dec/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: washingtonpost.com">[The Washington Post]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">washingtonpost.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Washington Post SECRETS IN THE SAUCERS</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Washington PostSECRETS IN THE SAUCERS - The Washington PostJune 29, 1987...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 29, 1987</span></span></span>

A more detailed account, based on the story given by the recipients, dates Shandera's receipt of the package to 11 December 1984 and describes nested, heavily sealed envelopes containing a canister of Kodak Tri-X black-and-white 35 mm film. It says the document images were repeated on the film and that Moore and Shandera were present when prints were made from the negatives.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.theufochronicles.com/2014/06/the-myth-of-mj-12-appendix-pt-1.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theufochronicles.com">[The UFO Chronicles]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theufochronicles.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">the myth of mj 12 appendix pt 1</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The UFO ChroniclesThe Myth of MJ-12: Appendix A –Pt 1June 29, 2014...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 29, 2014</span></span></span> Those details describe what happened after the material entered Shandera's possession; they do not identify who photographed the papers, where the photography occurred or how the photographer obtained them.

Steps one to three are precisely the steps that would be needed to connect the images to an authentic 1952 document, yet they are also the undocumented portion of the story.

Even the postal evidence has limitations. The reported [Albuquerque postmark]({{ 'postmark-clues/' | relative_url }}) establishes, at most, where the package entered the postal system; it does not identify the sender, photographer or source of the depicted papers. Philip J. Klass, writing critically about the case, also complained that Moore had not supplied him with the requested photocopy of the postmark, illustrating a broader problem: investigators outside the original circle were dependent on evidence and descriptions supplied by the recipients themselves.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://skepticalinquirer.org/wp-content/uploads/sites/29/2019/03/Issue-02-10.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: skepticalinquirer.org">[Skeptical Inquirer]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">skepticalinquirer.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Skeptical Inquirerthe Skeptical</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Skeptical Inquirerthe Skeptical</span></span></span>

This does not prove that the film was fraudulent. Anonymous sources sometimes provide genuine material. But anonymity removes a potentially powerful line of authentication. There was no source whose access to classified records could be checked, no government office from which the papers were demonstrably removed, no archival box or file number establishing where the alleged originals belonged, and no transfer record running from a government custodian to Shandera.

The contrast with ordinary archival evidence is important. The US National Archives can identify records by record group, series, folder and custody history. In its own MJ-12 work, it searched [Air Force]({{ 'air-force/' | relative_url }}), Joint Chiefs of Staff and National Security Council holdings, while the Truman and Eisenhower presidential libraries searched their collections. Those searches failed to locate the Eisenhower Briefing Document or an institutional record establishing its provenance.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/research/military/air-force/ufos" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives Project BLUE BOOK</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesProject BLUE BOOK - Unidentified Flying Objects &#124; National ArchivesJune 25, 2024...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 25, 2024</span></span></span>

## What forensic tests the missing paper prevents

A photographed document is not forensically worthless, but it contains a narrower class of evidence than the physical object. The distinction is easy to miss because a good photographic reproduction can look convincingly old. Appearance, however, is only one layer of documentary evidence.

The National Institute of Justice describes questioned-document examination as including visual and chemical analysis of inks and paper to investigate authentication and creation. State forensic laboratories likewise examine paper, inks, typewriting, impressions, alterations, printing processes, photocopying and other physical characteristics.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://nij.ojp.gov/topics/articles/overview-questioned-documents" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nij.ojp.gov">[National Institute of Justice]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nij.ojp.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Institute of JusticeArchived &#124; Overview of Questioned Documents &#124; National Institute of JusticeOctober 1, 2012...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 1, 2012</span></span></span> Without the alleged 1952 sheets, many of those examinations cannot be applied directly to the Eisenhower briefing.

**Paper cannot be characterised directly.** An examiner could normally inspect fibres, surface characteristics, watermarks and other properties, and potentially compare the stock with known material. The Georgia Bureau of Investigation notes that paper examinations can sometimes establish a source or whether separate sheets originated from the same source.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://dofs-gbi.georgia.gov/questioned-documents-0" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: dofs-gbi.georgia.gov">[GBI Forensic Sciences]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">dofs-gbi.georgia.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on georgia.gov.</span></span></span> A photographic negative records the image on the paper, not the paper itself.

**Ink and toner cannot be tested as material.** Forensic laboratories can examine inks and distinguish between different writing or printing materials in some circumstances. Such analysis can reveal inconsistencies or establish broad chronological limits, although exact dating is often difficult.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://dofs-gbi.georgia.gov/questioned-documents-0" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: dofs-gbi.georgia.gov">[GBI Forensic Sciences]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">dofs-gbi.georgia.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on georgia.gov.</span></span></span> The film provides no sample of whatever substance produced the characters, signatures or markings on the alleged original.

**Indented impressions are lost.** Typewriters and handwriting can leave pressure impressions in or through sheets of paper. Forensic document examiners specifically examine such indentations because they may reveal writing produced while sheets were stacked together or expose information not visible on the finished page.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.criminaljustice.ny.gov/forensic/standardization/questioned-documents.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: criminaljustice.ny.gov">[NYS Criminal Justice Services]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">criminaljustice.ny.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">S Criminal Justice Services Questioned Documents</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">S Criminal Justice ServicesQuestioned Documents...</span></span></span> A photograph of a document generally does not preserve the three-dimensional evidence required for that examination.

**Physical alterations become harder to reconstruct.** Originals can be examined for erasures, obliterations, overwriting, intersecting lines and other signs that text was changed. The Georgia laboratory notes that examinations can sometimes determine whether an erasure or alteration occurred, while forensic literature treats line intersections and differing inks as established areas of questioned-document analysis.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://dofs-gbi.georgia.gov/questioned-documents-0" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: dofs-gbi.georgia.gov">[GBI Forensic Sciences]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">dofs-gbi.georgia.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on georgia.gov.</span></span></span> Photographic reproduction may conceal precisely the surface differences that make those alterations detectable.

**Typewriting evidence is reduced.** Forensic examination can investigate typewriting and mechanical impressions, potentially comparing questioned material with known machines or processes.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.michigan.gov/msp/divisions/forensic-science/technical-disciplines/questioned-documents" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: michigan.gov">[Michigan]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">michigan.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Questioned Documents</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Questioned Documents...</span></span></span> Researchers can still compare the visible letterforms on the MJ-12 photographs with known typefaces and government documents, but they cannot examine the physical impact of the typing on the alleged original sheets.

These limitations should not be overstated. Copies sometimes remain useful forensic evidence. Virginia's Department of Forensic Science explicitly says that photocopies can be examined when originals have been lost, although it calls originals the best evidence; Indiana State Police similarly say that the best available copy can be examined when an original cannot be obtained.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://dfs.virginia.gov/section/questioned-documents/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: dfs.virginia.gov">[Virginia Department of Forensic Science]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">dfs.virginia.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Department of Forensic Science Questioned Documents Archives</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Virginia Department of Forensic ScienceQuestioned Documents Archives - Virginia Department of Forensic Science...</span></span></span>

That qualification is important to the MJ-12 debate. The absence of the paper does not logically make authentication impossible. Investigators can still test whether the document's language, dates, classification markings, type style, signatures and administrative conventions agree with independently authenticated records. What disappears is the ability to combine those contextual comparisons with direct physical examination of the alleged 1952 artefact.

## Why chain of custody matters to the authenticity claim

Chain of custody is more than a courtroom technicality. In forensic practice it records possession and handling so that investigators can establish that the item being examined is the same evidence that came from the claimed source. FBI forensic-document guidelines require the chain of custody of submitted items to be accurately maintained and the items to be identifiable later.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archives.fbi.gov/archives/about-us/lab/forensic-science-communications/fsc/april2000/swgdoc7.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.fbi.gov">[FBI Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.fbi.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on fbi.gov.</span></span></span>

For the Eisenhower briefing, there are really two possible chains. The first concerns the film after Shandera received it. That history can potentially be reconstructed through the people who handled the negatives, produced prints and studied the material. The second, much more important chain would run backwards from the photographed sheets to the supposed 1952 government briefing.

That second chain is missing.

There is no identified 1952 custodian, no declassification or release record, no documented removal from a government file, no named intermediary and no established connection between the anonymous sender and an archive containing the alleged original. The National Archives says that extensive searches of relevant Air Force, Joint Chiefs and [presidential-library]({{ 'library-searches/' | relative_url }}) holdings failed to identify the briefing documents. Its searches of National Security Council indexes under terms including “MJ-12”, “Majestic”, “UFO” and related expressions were likewise negative apart from the separately disputed Cutler-Twining memorandum.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/research/military/air-force/ufos" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives Project BLUE BOOK</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesProject BLUE BOOK - Unidentified Flying Objects &#124; National ArchivesJune 25, 2024...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 25, 2024</span></span></span>

The US Government Accountability Office reached a similarly relevant conclusion in 1995. After examining agency views of MJ-12 material, it reported that the Information Security Oversight Office, Air Force and National Archives knew the material from submissions by non-governmental people, and that there was no evidence those materials constituted actual documents originating in the executive branch. Archivists had also failed to find records matching Majestic 12 in federal-library holdings.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gao.gov/products/154832" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gao.gov">[Government Accountability Office]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gao.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on gao.gov.</span></span></span>

That finding goes directly to provenance. A document can contain historically accurate names, dates and bureaucratic vocabulary without demonstrating that it came from the institution whose stationery and security markings it imitates. Provenance asks a different question: *Where was this particular document before researchers obtained it, and can that history be independently demonstrated?* For the Eisenhower briefing, the traceable history starts roughly 32 years after its claimed creation.

## Can a document be authenticated without its original?

Yes, sometimes — but the conclusion normally has to reflect the limitations of the surviving evidence. Copies can be compared with authenticated records; signatures, wording, formatting, type styles and reproduction artefacts may expose a forgery or support consistency with a claimed period. Forensic laboratories therefore do not automatically refuse copies when originals are unavailable.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://dfs.virginia.gov/section/questioned-documents/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: dfs.virginia.gov">[Virginia Department of Forensic Science]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">dfs.virginia.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Department of Forensic Science Questioned Documents Archives</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Virginia Department of Forensic ScienceQuestioned Documents Archives - Virginia Department of Forensic Science...</span></span></span>

What cannot reasonably happen is for the absence of contradictory physical evidence to become positive evidence of authenticity. In the MJ-12 case, nobody can say that the paper passed a 1952-era paper examination, that the ink proved compatible with the claimed date, or that original typewriter impressions matched a government machine, because the alleged original has never been available for those tests.

Archival corroboration could have partly compensated for that loss. If an authenticated government file contained a carbon copy, routing slip, registry entry, distribution record or independently preserved reference to the briefing, the anonymous film would become less important: another evidential route would connect the text to government activity. Instead, official searches have failed to locate the briefing or records establishing MJ-12. The National Archives reports negative searches across multiple relevant collections, while GAO found no evidence that the submitted MJ-12 material actually originated in the executive branch.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/research/military/air-force/ufos" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives Project BLUE BOOK</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesProject BLUE BOOK - Unidentified Flying Objects &#124; National ArchivesJune 25, 2024...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 25, 2024</span></span></span>

The FBI's encounter with MJ-12 material reinforces rather than repairs this provenance problem. Its investigation began after copies of the supposed classified material circulated publicly; the Bureau was not examining a document recovered from its original government file. After consultation with Air Force investigators, the FBI treated the material as bogus.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Majestic_12" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Majestic 12</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Majestic 12</span></span></span> Whatever weight one gives that judgement, it does not supply the missing original or a government custody trail.

The fairest assessment is therefore narrower than either “the missing original proves a hoax” or “the photographs are sufficient proof”. The anonymous film preserves something that can be studied: images of purported government documents. What it does not preserve is the strongest evidence for where those papers came from. Until a physical original, an independently archived counterpart or a demonstrable government chain of custody emerges, the Eisenhower Briefing Document cannot be tied securely to the government provenance it claims for itself.
