## Introduction

Missing [Top Secret control]({{ 'control-sheets/' | relative_url }}) numbers are a genuine problem for the alleged MJ-12 papers, but they do not provide a simple rule that proves forgery by themselves. The strongest version of the objection applies to the 1954 Cutler–Twining memorandum because the National Archives says the record series in which it was found was specifically filed by [Top Secret register]({{ 'register-numbers/' | relative_url }}) number, yet the MJ-12 memo bears none. The broader claim that every authentic Top Secret document of the period had to display such a number is harder to sustain. Contemporary security rules required agencies to control and register Top Secret material, but implementation varied, and authentic presidential-level records survive without an obvious Top Secret control number on the document itself.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.trumanlibrary.gov/library/executive-orders/10290/executive-order-10290" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: trumanlibrary.gov">[Harry S. Truman Presidential Library]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">trumanlibrary.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">executive order 10290</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Harry S. Truman Presidential LibraryEXECUTIVE ORDER 10290 &#124; Harry S. TrumanSeptember 24, 1951...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: September 24, 1951</span></span></span> The sensible conclusion is therefore narrower: a missing number can be suspicious when the document's particular office, series and handling procedure called for one, but absence alone is not a universal authentication test.

## Why control numbers were expected

A control number was part of the machinery used to account for highly classified documents. It could help an organisation trace a document through registries, distinguish copies and establish who had responsibility for material whose circulation was tightly restricted. Genuine 1950s records provide abundant examples. A declassified 1955 National Security Agency conference report, for instance, carries a visible Top Secret control number, copy information and page count. Similar NSA material from 1954 shows the same sort of controlled-copy notation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nsa.gov/portals/75/documents/news-features/declassified-documents/friedman-documents/patent-equipment/FOLDER_143/41726519076582.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nsa.gov">[NSA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nsa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">OFFICIAL REPORT</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">OFFICIAL REPORT - NATIONAL SECURITY AGENCY FIELD COMMANDERS CONFERENCE - 1955March 27, 2018...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 27, 2018</span></span></span>

There was also a firm policy basis for controlling Top Secret information. President Truman's Executive Order 10290, issued in 1951, required agency heads to designate Top Secret Control Officers to receive, maintain registers of and dispatch Top Secret information. The order therefore supports the critics' basic premise that Top Secret material was supposed to be subject to unusually close accountability.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.trumanlibrary.gov/library/executive-orders/10290/executive-order-10290" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: trumanlibrary.gov">[Harry S. Truman Presidential Library]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">trumanlibrary.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">executive order 10290</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Harry S. Truman Presidential LibraryEXECUTIVE ORDER 10290 &#124; Harry S. TrumanSeptember 24, 1951...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: September 24, 1951</span></span></span>

But the executive order does **not** say that every sheet of every Top Secret document throughout the executive branch must display a standardised control number in a particular location. It established minimum government-wide safeguards while allowing agencies to issue additional instructions suited to their own operations. That distinction becomes important because some agencies did adopt more prescriptive marking rules.

CIA correspondence guidance, for example, instructed that each copy of every page of Top Secret correspondence should carry a CIA Top Secret control number obtained from a control officer, together with preparation and copy information.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cia.gov/readingroom/document/cia-rdp74-00005r000200060045-8" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cia.gov">[CIA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cia.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">CORRESPONDENCE STYLE &amp; PROCEDURES &#124; CIA FOIA (foia.cia.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">CORRESPONDENCE STYLE &amp; PROCEDURES &#124; CIA FOIA (foia.cia.gov)December 22, 2016...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 22, 2016</span></span></span> An NSA document can therefore be judged against NSA practice and a CIA document against [CIA rules]({{ 'cia-rules/' | relative_url }}), but it is risky to turn one agency's detailed procedure into an automatic test for every [White House]({{ 'white-house/' | relative_url }}), National Security Council, military and presidential paper.

This is the central weakness in the most sweeping control-number argument. The historical proposition that Top Secret information was carefully controlled is well supported. The stronger proposition that *no authentic Top Secret document could lack a visible control number* is not.

## Why the Cutler–Twining memo is different

For the Cutler–Twining memorandum, however, the objection is unusually concrete. The document was discovered in Record Group 341 at the National Archives, among [Air Force]({{ 'air-force/' | relative_url }}) headquarters records. According to the Archives' own MJ-12 reference report, the relevant series was “filed by a Top Secret register number”, but the purported Cutler memo did not bear such a number.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/research/military/air-force/ufos" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives Project BLUE BOOK</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesProject BLUE BOOK - Unidentified Flying Objects &#124; National ArchivesJune 25, 2024...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 25, 2024</span></span></span>

That fact carries more weight than saying merely, “Top Secret papers usually had numbers”. The archival filing system itself creates an expectation. If neighbouring material entered the series through a Top Secret register and was organised by its register number, an unnumbered sheet requires an explanation for how it entered that system.

The Archives identified several related irregularities that sharpen the point. The memo was sitting in folder T4-1846, but no other material in the folder referred to the supposed “NSC/MJ-12” project. Searches of records belonging to the Secretary of Defense, Joint Chiefs of Staff, Air Force and associated offices failed to produce corroborating material, while enquiries to the National Security Council and other agencies likewise yielded nothing.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/research/military/air-force/ufos" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives Project BLUE BOOK</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesProject BLUE BOOK - Unidentified Flying Objects &#124; National ArchivesJune 25, 2024...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 25, 2024</span></span></span>

The missing number therefore matters most as part of an **archival-context test**. A document found in a series whose organising principle assumes such numbers is more anomalous than an isolated White House memorandum that never passed through that filing arrangement.

This also explains why the objection should not be transferred mechanically to the other core MJ-12 papers. [The Eisenhower Briefing Document]({{ 'the-eisenhower-briefing-document/' | relative_url }}) and purported Truman–Forrestal memorandum became public through photographs on anonymously mailed film rather than through an intact government file. Their lack of visible control numbers can be noted, but there is no original archival folder whose registration sequence can be inspected in the same way. The provenance problem is greater, while the specific control-number inference is weaker.

## Exceptions cited from presidential archives

MJ-12 defender [Stanton Friedman]({{ 'friedman-s-case/' | relative_url }}) challenged the universal version of the argument by pointing to genuine Top Secret presidential-era documents without visible control numbers. His published account says Eisenhower Library archivist Herbert Pankratz wrote to him in January 2003 that the library held “numerous” Top Secret documents without control numbers. Friedman also said he had located Top Secret memoranda associated with Robert Cutler that lacked such numbers.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.theblackvault.com/casefiles/update-on-operation-majestic-12-documents/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theblackvault.com">[The Black Vault]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theblackvault.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Black Vault Update on Operation Majestic 12 Documents</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Black Vault Update on Operation Majestic 12 Documents</span></span></span>

That correspondence is principally preserved in Friedman's own advocacy rather than in an independent National Archives finding about MJ-12, so it should not be treated as equivalent to the Archives' formal reference report. Nevertheless, the general point is independently consistent with surviving presidential records.

One useful example is a White House memorandum recording a conference with President Eisenhower on 24 November 1954. The National Security Archive identifies the document as Top Secret, and its published transcription shows the memorandum, date, participants and signature of Staff Secretary Andrew Goodpaster without displaying the sort of conventional Top Secret control number found on heavily registered NSA material.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://nsarchive.gwu.edu/media/27520/ocr" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nsarchive.gwu.edu">[National Security Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nsarchive.gwu.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Security Archive OCR of the Document &#124; National Security Archive</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Security Archive OCR of the Document &#124; National Security Archive</span></span></span>

Another authentic 1954 Goodpaster memorandum concerning NATO nuclear policy was distributed as “Copy 1 of 6 copies”. Its surviving transcription illustrates that presidential-level classified paperwork could use copy-accounting information without resembling the highly standardised control blocks visible on some intelligence-agency records.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://nsarchive.gwu.edu/document/20485-national-security-archive-doc-04-colonel-j" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nsarchive.gwu.edu">[National Security Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nsarchive.gwu.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on gwu.edu.</span></span></span>

These exceptions do not prove that the MJ-12 documents are genuine. They establish only that the proposed universal rule — “Top Secret plus no control number equals forgery” — is historically too rigid. Genuine records could be controlled through copy numbers, registers, cover sheets, staff procedures or office-level systems whose markings were not necessarily reproduced identically on every surviving page.

There is a further archival complication. A document viewed decades later may no longer be accompanied by every control sheet, envelope, receipt or routing form that originally travelled with it. A CIA Top Secret control-and-cover sheet preserved in the agency's declassified holdings explicitly describes the cover sheet as something attached to Top Secret material for control purposes.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cia.gov/readingroom/docs/CIA-RDP86B00269R000300070001-0.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cia.gov">[CIA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cia.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">TOP SECRET</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">TOP SECRET</span></span></span> Authentication therefore requires distinguishing between a number that should have appeared on the underlying document itself and accountability information that might originally have existed on associated paperwork.

## Context needed to judge the anomaly

The most useful way to handle the control-number issue is to ask a series of document-specific questions rather than apply a single authenticity rule.

**Which organisation supposedly created the paper?** Requirements differed. CIA correspondence regulations could prescribe page-by-page control markings, while White House records did not necessarily follow exactly the same visible format.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cia.gov/readingroom/document/cia-rdp74-00005r000200060045-8" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cia.gov">[CIA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cia.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">CORRESPONDENCE STYLE &amp; PROCEDURES &#124; CIA FOIA (foia.cia.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">CORRESPONDENCE STYLE &amp; PROCEDURES &#124; CIA FOIA (foia.cia.gov)December 22, 2016...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 22, 2016</span></span></span>

**What kind of document was it?** A multi-copy intelligence report circulating through a registry naturally creates stronger expectations for formal control markings than a one-page memorandum prepared for direct senior-level delivery. Authentic NSA records containing dozens or hundreds of controlled copies demonstrate the more formal end of that spectrum.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nsa.gov/portals/75/documents/news-features/declassified-documents/friedman-documents/patent-equipment/FOLDER_143/41726519076582.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nsa.gov">[NSA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nsa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">OFFICIAL REPORT</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">OFFICIAL REPORT - NATIONAL SECURITY AGENCY FIELD COMMANDERS CONFERENCE - 1955March 27, 2018...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 27, 2018</span></span></span>

**Does the archive itself establish a numbering requirement?** This is where the Cutler–Twining memorandum fares poorly. The National Archives did not merely observe that a control number would have been customary; it stated that the series containing the document was filed according to Top Secret register numbers and that this sheet lacked one.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/research/military/air-force/ufos" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives Project BLUE BOOK</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesProject BLUE BOOK - Unidentified Flying Objects &#124; National ArchivesJune 25, 2024...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 25, 2024</span></span></span>

**Is associated control paperwork missing?** Executive Order 10290 required Top Secret registers and designated control officers, but that institutional requirement need not translate into one universal marking pattern on every surviving page.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.trumanlibrary.gov/library/executive-orders/10290/executive-order-10290" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: trumanlibrary.gov">[Harry S. Truman Presidential Library]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">trumanlibrary.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">executive order 10290</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Harry S. Truman Presidential LibraryEXECUTIVE ORDER 10290 &#124; Harry S. TrumanSeptember 24, 1951...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: September 24, 1951</span></span></span> Cover sheets, receipts and transmission records therefore matter when originals are available.

**Does the anomaly stand alone?** A missing number becomes more persuasive when it coincides with other failures of provenance or archival fit. For the Cutler–Twining memo, the absence of a register number sits alongside the lack of related MJ-12 material in the folder and the failure of extensive archival searches to uncover corroborating records.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/research/military/air-force/ufos" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives Project BLUE BOOK</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesProject BLUE BOOK - Unidentified Flying Objects &#124; National ArchivesJune 25, 2024...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 25, 2024</span></span></span> Conversely, finding a genuine Top Secret memorandum without a control number demonstrates an exception to a rule; it does not establish that another anomalous document belongs in a particular government file.

## What the control-number dispute actually proves

The documented exceptions substantially weaken one familiar anti-MJ-12 argument: it is not historically sound to declare every unnumbered Top Secret paper fraudulent simply because no control number appears on its face. Government-wide rules required accountability, but the surviving documentary record shows that the visible form of that accountability was not completely uniform.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.trumanlibrary.gov/library/executive-orders/10290/executive-order-10290" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: trumanlibrary.gov">[Harry S. Truman Presidential Library]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">trumanlibrary.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">executive order 10290</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Harry S. Truman Presidential LibraryEXECUTIVE ORDER 10290 &#124; Harry S. TrumanSeptember 24, 1951...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: September 24, 1951</span></span></span>

That correction does not rescue the alleged MJ-12 material. In the most important case, the Cutler–Twining memorandum, the objection survives in a stronger and more precise form. The problem is not simply that an authentic Top Secret memo “should look” a certain way. It is that the National Archives says the specific series containing the paper was organised by Top Secret register number and the purported MJ-12 item lacks one.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/research/military/air-force/ufos" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives Project BLUE BOOK</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesProject BLUE BOOK - Unidentified Flying Objects &#124; National ArchivesJune 25, 2024...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 25, 2024</span></span></span>

Control numbers are therefore best treated as **context-sensitive forensic evidence**. Their presence can demonstrate conformity with a known administrative system; their absence can become significant when that system specifically required or relied upon them. But neither presence nor absence should be detached from provenance, office procedure, copy history and archival location. For [the alleged MJ-12 documents]({{ 'the-alleged-mj-12-documents/' | relative_url }}), that narrower approach avoids both extremes: missing numbers do not by themselves prove forgery, yet genuine exceptions elsewhere do not make the Cutler–Twining anomaly disappear.
