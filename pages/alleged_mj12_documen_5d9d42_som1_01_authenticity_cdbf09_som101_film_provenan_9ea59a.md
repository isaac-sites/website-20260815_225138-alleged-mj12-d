## Introduction

SOM1-01’s provenance can be followed backwards only a short distance. In March 1994, aviation writer and UFO researcher Don Berliner received an anonymous package containing exposed, undeveloped 35 mm film. When processed, the film showed pages of a purported 1954 “Majestic-12 Group Special Operations Manual”. Investigators later linked the package’s postage meter to Quillin’s Drugs in La Crosse, Wisconsin. That establishes something useful about the 1994 mailing, but it does not establish where the photographed manual came from, who photographed it, who possessed it beforehand, or whether it ever belonged to a United States government records system.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.specialoperationsmanual.com/2014/12/30/som1-01-overview-authentication/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: specialoperationsmanual.com">[SOM1-01]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">specialoperationsmanual.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">SOM1-01SOM1-01 Overview &amp; Authentication</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">SOM1-01December 30, 2014...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 30, 2014</span></span></span>

That distinction is why the provenance question remains unresolved. A traceable postal origin is not the same as a traceable documentary origin. No paper original has entered an archive or been produced for independent forensic examination, and no identified government custodian, file, accession, records series or authenticated contemporary reference connects SOM1-01 to an official source. Even the document’s leading defenders acknowledge that this missing original prevents the provenance problem from being closed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.specialoperationsmanual.com/faq/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: specialoperationsmanual.com">[SOM1-01]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">specialoperationsmanual.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Authenticity - SOM1-01...</span></span></span>

## What is known about the 1994 mailing

The strongest part of SOM1-01’s history begins on the receiving end. Accounts from both supporters and critics agree that Berliner received the material as anonymous 35 mm film rather than discovering a paper manual in government files. A 1999 joint statement signed by Berliner and four other UFO researchers described the material as having arrived “as a roll of film from an anonymous source” and stressed that the sender could not be identified or evaluated.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://groups.google.com/g/no.prat.ufo/c/eWxV9q5XQgA" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: groups.google.com">[Google Groups]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">groups.google.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Groups Joint Statement</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Google GroupsJoint Statement - MJ-12 SOM-01March 23, 1999...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 23, 1999</span></span></span>

Robert and Ryan Wood, prominent defenders of SOM1-01, subsequently developed the Wisconsin trail further. Their account says that the package bore a 7 March 1994 La Crosse postmark and postage from Postalia meter number 2223773, which they traced to Quillin’s Drugs. The package was hand-addressed to Berliner. Their published reconstruction therefore gives the mailing a considerably more specific geographical origin than “anonymous post from Wisconsin”: it associates the physical dispatch with a particular business and postal meter in La Crosse.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.specialoperationsmanual.com/2014/12/30/som1-01-overview-authentication/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: specialoperationsmanual.com">[SOM1-01]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">specialoperationsmanual.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">SOM1-01SOM1-01 Overview &amp; Authentication</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">SOM1-01December 30, 2014...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 30, 2014</span></span></span>

That is genuine provenance information, but only for the package. It shows that the film apparently passed through a mailing mechanism associated with the pharmacy; it does not show that Quillin’s Drugs created the film, owned the alleged manual or had any government connection. Nor does a postage meter identify the individual who placed the film in the package. Several very different scenarios — an employee mailing it, another person obtaining access to the meter, or someone connected with the premises sending it for another party — remain compatible with the basic postal evidence.

There are also inconsistencies in secondary retellings of exactly when Berliner received the material. The Wood account gives the postage-meter date as 7 March, while contemporary or near-contemporary sceptical accounts describe Berliner as receiving the film on 14 March. These dates need not conflict: one can describe dispatch and the other receipt. More importantly, neither date reaches backwards into the purported manual’s history before 1994.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.specialoperationsmanual.com/2014/12/30/som1-01-overview-authentication/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: specialoperationsmanual.com">[SOM1-01]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">specialoperationsmanual.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">SOM1-01SOM1-01 Overview &amp; Authentication</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">SOM1-01December 30, 2014...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 30, 2014</span></span></span>

The photographic medium creates another tempting but limited clue. Wood has stated that the [Tri-X film]({{ 'tri-x-film/' | relative_url }}) itself was manufactured in 1954 and has argued that this means the photographs *could* have been taken that early. But an old roll of film supplies, at most, an earliest-material clue; its manufacture date does not establish when it was exposed. Film manufactured decades earlier could in principle be used later. Accordingly, the age attributed to the film cannot by itself convert a 1994 anonymous mailing into evidence of a 1954 government document.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.specialoperationsmanual.com/2014/12/30/som1-01-overview-authentication/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: specialoperationsmanual.com">[SOM1-01]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">specialoperationsmanual.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">SOM1-01SOM1-01 Overview &amp; Authentication</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">SOM1-01December 30, 2014...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 30, 2014</span></span></span>

## Where the chain of custody breaks

The decisive gap lies immediately behind the film. There is no publicly demonstrated chain running from Berliner to the photographer, from the photographer to an owner of the paper manual, and from that owner to an identifiable government office that supposedly issued or retained SOM1-01.

This matters because provenance asks a different question from whether a document *looks* appropriate to its claimed period. A convincingly reproduced 1950s typeface, plausible military terminology, a historically real building or period-correct photographic stock may strengthen an argument that an artefact is compatible with the era. None identifies the person who created it or establishes its [custody]({{ 'custody/' | relative_url }}) before discovery.

The distinction is particularly sharp in SOM1-01 because the available object is photographic evidence of another object. The photographs can be inspected for what they depict, but investigators cannot directly test the purported 1954 manual’s paper, ink, printing process, binding, physical alterations or ageing. The defenders’ own FAQ concedes the central point: [original documents]({{ 'original-document/' | relative_url }}) and established provenance have considerable value, but “we do not have that”.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.specialoperationsmanual.com/faq/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: specialoperationsmanual.com">[SOM1-01]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">specialoperationsmanual.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Authenticity - SOM1-01...</span></span></span>

The 1999 joint statement made essentially the same problem its starting point. Its signatories — Jan Aldrich, Don Berliner, Thomas Deuley, Richard Hall and Mark Rodeghier — argued that anonymity meant there was no source whose reliability could be checked and no original document available for forensic examination. Significantly, Berliner was not merely a distant critic: he was the recipient of the film.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://groups.google.com/g/no.prat.ufo/c/eWxV9q5XQgA" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: groups.google.com">[Google Groups]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">groups.google.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Groups Joint Statement</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Google GroupsJoint Statement - MJ-12 SOM-01March 23, 1999...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 23, 1999</span></span></span>

### The claimed Wisconsin extension does not close the gap

The Woods have presented later testimony as taking the trail another step backwards. Their account names researcher James T. Westwood as someone who went to La Crosse and reportedly handled a paper copy of the manual associated with the investigation, describing it as old paper. Wood’s presentation nevertheless acknowledged uncertainty about where the supposed manual was subsequently located and explicitly avoided claiming knowledge of its present whereabouts.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.specialoperationsmanual.com/2014/12/28/why-the-critics-are-wrong-about-som1-01-mufon-journal-jan-2015/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: specialoperationsmanual.com">[SOM1-01]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">specialoperationsmanual.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">SOM1-01Why The Critics Are Wrong About SOM1-01</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">SOM1-01Why The Critics Are Wrong About SOM1-01</span></span></span>

That claim is intriguing precisely because, if independently documented and tied securely to the photographed object, it could have moved the case beyond film alone. As published, however, it remains testimonial rather than a documented chain of custody. The Wood account itself summarises the evidence as an investigator’s word that he handled the object. There is no publicly available custody record showing who supplied that manual, how that person acquired it, whether it was the exact object photographed, or how it travelled from a government repository to Wisconsin.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.specialoperationsmanual.com/2014/12/28/why-the-critics-are-wrong-about-som1-01-mufon-journal-jan-2015/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: specialoperationsmanual.com">[SOM1-01]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">specialoperationsmanual.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">SOM1-01Why The Critics Are Wrong About SOM1-01</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">SOM1-01Why The Critics Are Wrong About SOM1-01</span></span></span>

Nor does “old paper” solve the problem. Even if laboratory testing established that the substrate dated from the early 1950s, that would date a material rather than authenticate the writing printed upon it. Period paper can be used later. A much stronger result would require examination of the complete physical object alongside evidence of its custody and documentary context.

This is why the Wisconsin findings are best described as extending the *mailing trail*, not completing the manual’s provenance. They move the question from “where was the film posted?” towards “who around La Crosse might have had access to it?”, but they have not publicly produced an identified source who can account for the manual’s journey from an issuing government organisation to the camera.

## The missing government trail is the larger problem

For a purportedly classified operational manual, the most persuasive provenance would not begin at a pharmacy in 1994. It would connect SOM1-01 to records generated or retained by the organisation that supposedly produced, distributed or used it.

No such connection has emerged from official archival research. In 1995 the US General Accounting Office, now the Government Accountability Office (GAO), reported on searches concerning the broader Majestic-12 material. The Information Security Oversight Office, [Air Force]({{ 'air-force/' | relative_url }}) and National Archives told GAO that their knowledge of the material was limited to submissions from non-governmental sources; GAO said there was no evidence that the material represented actual executive-branch documents. Federal archivists and the Truman and Eisenhower presidential libraries had found no records corroborating Majestic 12.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gao.gov/products/154832" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gao.gov">[Government Accountability Office]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gao.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Government Accountability Office[Comments on Majestic 12 Material] &#124; U.S. GAO</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Government Accountability Office[Comments on Majestic 12 Material] &#124; U.S. GAO</span></span></span>

The National Archives likewise says it conducted extensive searches among Air Force and Joint Chiefs of Staff records for MJ-12 material, with the Truman and Eisenhower libraries also searching their holdings. This does not prove that every conceivable secret document could never have existed — absence from a search is not logically equivalent to proof of non-existence — but it leaves SOM1-01 without the independent institutional anchor that would transform its provenance.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/research/military/air-force/ufos" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives Project BLUE BOOK</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesProject BLUE BOOK - Unidentified Flying Objects &#124; National ArchivesJune 25, 2024...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 25, 2024</span></span></span>

That point should not be confused with the FBI’s conclusion about an earlier MJ-12 document. The FBI records concern a different purported Majestic-12 briefing that reached the Bureau in 1988; the Air Force investigation characterised that material as fabricated. It would therefore be an overreach to say that the FBI separately authenticated or disproved SOM1-01 itself. What the official record does show is that SOM1-01 cannot simply borrow provenance from the wider MJ-12 corpus: federal searches did not uncover an official records trail that independently establishes that corpus as government-origin material.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://vault.fbi.gov/search?SearchableText=Majestic" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: vault.fbi.gov">[FBI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">vault.fbi.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">— Federal Bureau of Investigation</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">FBI — Federal Bureau of Investigation...</span></span></span>

## Why period-correct details cannot substitute for provenance

Supporters have accumulated several arguments intended to show that SOM1-01 contains details compatible with the 1950s. Among them are claims concerning its Monotype Modern typography, the “N.MEX.” abbreviation, the existence of Building 21 at Kirtland Air Force Base and the age of the film. These questions matter to the broader authenticity debate, but they answer a different evidential question.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.specialoperationsmanual.com/2014/12/28/why-the-critics-are-wrong-about-som1-01-mufon-journal-jan-2015/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: specialoperationsmanual.com">[SOM1-01]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">specialoperationsmanual.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">SOM1-01Why The Critics Are Wrong About SOM1-01</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">SOM1-01Why The Critics Are Wrong About SOM1-01</span></span></span>

A useful way to separate the issues is to ask what each kind of evidence could establish:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **Postal evidence** can help establish where and when the 1994 package entered the mail.
* **Film evidence** might establish characteristics or manufacture of the photographic stock.
* **Typography and formatting** can test whether the photographed pages are compatible with printing practices of the claimed period.
* **Historical cross-checking** can determine whether names, buildings, abbreviations and procedures existed when the document says they did.
* **Provenance evidence**, by contrast, must explain who created or held the document and how it moved through identifiable custody before reaching the recipient.

</div>

A forger with good historical knowledge can reproduce correct details. Conversely, a genuine document can contain errors or unconventional formatting. That is why neither the accumulation of apparently correct details nor the identification of anomalies alone supplies the [missing chain]({{ 'missing-chain/' | relative_url }}) of custody. Provenance is valuable precisely because it provides evidence external to the disputed document.

The Woods themselves illustrated this distinction when discussing another claimed MJ-12-related item, the Vernon Bowen manuscript. Wood called its provenance “excellent” because he said originals had been returned from a Freedom of Information Act office at Fort Meade, their age could be examined and Bowen’s son could be contacted about authorship. Whatever conclusions one draws about that separate material, the comparison reveals what SOM1-01 lacks: an identifiable institutional route, a physical original and witnesses connected to its creation rather than merely its later circulation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://lo2.org/pdf/magazines/crash_retrieval_conference/Crash_Retrieval_Conference_Proceedings_1st_Annual_Nov_2003.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: lo2.org">[Lo2]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">lo2.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">UFO Crash Retrieval Conference Proceedings</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UFO Crash Retrieval Conference Proceedings...</span></span></span>

## What a credible provenance would require

Resolving SOM1-01’s provenance would therefore require evidence that bridges the missing decades rather than further refinement of the already-established 1994 mailing. The most valuable discovery would be the actual paper manual shown in the photographs, accompanied by evidence sufficient to demonstrate that it is the photographed object.

That would permit forensic examination of paper, ink, printing or typesetting characteristics, binding, stamps, wear and other physical features that cannot be tested adequately from photographic reproductions. The SOM1-01 defenders themselves have acknowledged that locating the original would permit testing of its paper and ink.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.specialoperationsmanual.com/faq/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: specialoperationsmanual.com">[SOM1-01]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">specialoperationsmanual.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Authenticity - SOM1-01...</span></span></span>

Physical examination alone would still not be ideal. A strong provenance case would also need independent documentary links: for example, an accession or file reference, distribution register, contemporary correspondence mentioning the manual, a matching copy in a separately preserved government collection, or custody records connecting the artefact to an identifiable office and responsible officials. The crucial feature would be independence from the anonymously supplied SOM1-01 material itself.

An identified photographer or sender could also materially change the assessment, provided that the person supplied a verifiable account rather than another anonymous assertion. Investigators would need to establish how that individual obtained the manual, when and where it was photographed, who possessed it previously and whether those claims could be checked against surviving records or witnesses. A confession by a fabricator, supported by production materials or other corroboration, could of course resolve provenance in the opposite direction.

What would *not* be enough is simply discovering that another historically accurate feature appears in the photographed pages. Such findings may affect judgements about fabrication, but they cannot fill an undocumented custody interval of roughly four decades.

## The narrow conclusion the evidence supports

The [anonymous film]({{ 'anonymous-film/' | relative_url }}) trail proves more than “nothing”, but considerably less than authentication. It provides a reasonably specific entry point into public circulation: exposed 35 mm film reached Don Berliner in March 1994 through a package whose postage has been traced to Quillin’s Drugs in La Crosse. That is the firmest portion of the story, and it is useful because it constrains where investigation of the immediate sender should begin.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.specialoperationsmanual.com/2014/12/30/som1-01-overview-authentication/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: specialoperationsmanual.com">[SOM1-01]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">specialoperationsmanual.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">SOM1-01SOM1-01 Overview &amp; Authentication</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">SOM1-01December 30, 2014...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 30, 2014</span></span></span>

Beyond that point, the evidential chain fragments into testimony, inference and disputed clues. The publicly available record does not identify the photographer, establish the owner of the photographed manual, document its custody before 1994, locate a securely authenticated physical original, or connect SOM1-01 to a government records series. Official searches of the broader MJ-12 material likewise failed to supply the missing institutional foundation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gao.gov/products/154832" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gao.gov">[Government Accountability Office]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gao.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Government Accountability Office[Comments on Majestic 12 Material] &#124; U.S. GAO</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Government Accountability Office[Comments on Majestic 12 Material] &#124; U.S. GAO</span></span></span>

That does not, by provenance alone, demonstrate who made SOM1-01 or exactly when it was made. It means something narrower and more important for assessing the manual: its claimed 1954 government origin has not been established by its transmission history. Until evidence bridges the gap between the anonymous Wisconsin mailing and an identifiable pre-1994 custodian — ideally an independently documented government source — SOM1-01 remains a photographed document of uncertain origin rather than an artefact with a verifiable official chain of custody.
