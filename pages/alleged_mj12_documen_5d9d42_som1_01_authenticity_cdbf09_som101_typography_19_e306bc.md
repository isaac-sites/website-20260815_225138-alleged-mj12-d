## Introduction

SOM1-01’s typography does not provide a simple smoking gun against its claimed April 1954 date. The strongest typographic objection says that some sans-serif headings are Helvetica, a typeface first released as Neue Haas Grotesk in 1957. If that identification were secure, those headings would indeed be anachronistic. But it is not secure: Helvetica belongs to a much older family of grotesque sans-serifs, and period American faces such as Franklin Gothic existed decades before 1954 and can look deceptively similar in photographic reproductions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.myfonts.com/de/pages/fontscom-2011-06-06-helvetica-the-prequel" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: myfonts.com">[myfonts.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">myfonts.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">My Fonts Helvetica: the Prequel « Fonts.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Helvetica: the Prequel « Fonts.comJune 6, 2011...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 6, 2011</span></span></span>

That leaves a narrower conclusion than either side of the MJ-12 dispute sometimes claims. The typography **could plausibly have been produced with type available in the mid-1950s**, so the familiar “Helvetica proves forgery” argument is weaker than it first appears. But visual compatibility with old type is not authentication. Because researchers have photographs rather than the alleged paper original, they cannot establish from typography alone when, where or by whom SOM1-01 was actually composed and printed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.specialoperationsmanual.com/the-manual/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: specialoperationsmanual.com">[Special Operations Manual]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">specialoperationsmanual.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Special Operations Manual The Manual</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Special Operations ManualThe Manual - SOM1-01January 1, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 1, 2026</span></span></span>

## The Helvetica anachronism claim

The objection has an appealingly straightforward logic. Researcher Barry Greenwood and others associated with the critical analysis of SOM1-01 argued that sans-serif subheadings resemble Helvetica. One published version of that critique specifically points to features including the capital **R** and maintains that the lettering is Helvetica rather than the older Akzidenz-Grotesk. Since SOM1-01 carries a 7 April 1954 date, that would put a post-1954 typeface in a supposedly 1954 document.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ufohastings.com/storage/files/aldrich-analysis-som-101-field-manual.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ufohastings.com">[UFO Hastings]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ufohastings.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">UFO Hastings This is an excerpt from my article, “Operation</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UFO HastingsThis is an excerpt from my article, “OperationDecember 15, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 15, 2025</span></span></span>

The chronology of Helvetica itself is not seriously in dispute. Eduard Hoffmann of the Haas Type Foundry commissioned Max Miedinger to develop the design in 1956. It appeared commercially in Switzerland in 1957 as **Neue Haas Grotesk** and was subsequently renamed Helvetica for wider international distribution. Contemporary histories from the type industry therefore place the design after SOM1-01’s stated publication date.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.myfonts.com/de/pages/fontscom-2011-06-06-helvetica-the-prequel" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: myfonts.com">[MyFonts]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">myfonts.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">My Fonts Helvetica: the Prequel « Fonts.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Helvetica: the Prequel « Fonts.comJune 6, 2011...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 6, 2011</span></span></span>

So the important question is not “Did Helvetica exist in April 1954?” It did not. The question is **whether the photographed SOM1-01 lettering can actually be identified as Helvetica to the exclusion of older alternatives**.

That is considerably harder.

Helvetica was not a completely unprecedented visual invention. It belongs to the grotesque or neo-grotesque sans-serif tradition and was developed against established faces such as Akzidenz-Grotesk. The Haas project itself arose because existing grotesques were already popular; historical accounts identify Akzidenz-Grotesk, known as Standard in American and British markets, as one of the important precedents.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://lapolice.ch/stories/footnotes-a-b-article-5/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: lapolice.ch">[lapolice.ch]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">lapolice.ch</span><span class="citation-popover-title">La Police – article 5 (The Haas Typefoundry</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">La Police – article 5 (The Haas Typefoundry</span></span></span>

This matters because the disputed SOM1-01 headings are not pristine pieces of metal type available for microscopic comparison. What surfaced in 1994 was an undeveloped roll of 35 mm film containing photographs of the alleged manual. The SOM1-01 proponents themselves acknowledge that focus varies between frames, with some pages much less distinct than others. Fine details that distinguish closely related grotesques can consequently be degraded by the original printing, photography, film grain, enlargement and later reproduction.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.specialoperationsmanual.com/2014/12/30/som1-01-overview-authentication/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: specialoperationsmanual.com">[Special Operations Manual]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">specialoperationsmanual.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Special Operations Manual SOM1-01 Overview &amp; Authentication</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Special Operations ManualSOM1-01 Overview &amp; Authentication - SOM1-01December 30, 2014...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 30, 2014</span></span></span>

A confident visual resemblance to Helvetica is therefore evidence worth investigating, but it is not automatically a unique typeface identification.

## Franklin Gothic and other period alternatives

The strongest answer to the Helvetica objection is that American printers had sans-serif faces with broadly comparable forms long before 1954. **Franklin Gothic** is the clearest example. American Type Founders credits Morris Fuller Benton with the influential Franklin Gothic family, while historical references date the original face to the opening years of the twentieth century. Franklin Gothic was therefore not merely theoretically available in 1954: it belonged to an established American tradition of commercial and display typography by then.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://atftype.com/history/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: atftype.com">[American Type Founders Collection]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">atftype.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">American Type Founders Collection History &#124; American Type Founders Collection</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">American Type Founders Collection History &#124; American Type Founders Collection</span></span></span>

SOM1-01 proponents subsequently compared the disputed headings with type specimens rather than accepting the Helvetica identification. Robert Wood reports examining the US Government Printing Office's 1959 *Specimens of Type Faces* and finding two candidates for the bold sans-serif headings: **Franklin Gothic** and **Square Gothic**. His assessment was that Franklin Gothic matched closely and Square Gothic appeared closer still in stroke consistency and spacing.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://icypaws.tripod.com/mj3.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: icypaws.tripod.com">[icypaws.tripod.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">icypaws.tripod.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">M J-12 Documents</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">M J-12 Documents</span></span></span>

That does not prove either face was actually used. It does, however, expose the logical weakness in treating “looks like Helvetica” as equivalent to “must have been set in Helvetica”. To make the anachronism decisive, a critic would need diagnostic letterforms that distinguish the photographed heading not only from Akzidenz-Grotesk but from the relevant American grotesques and gothics available to printers before April 1954.

There is another typographic layer to SOM1-01 that is sometimes confused with this argument. The disputed sans-serif concerns chiefly involve headings; the **body text is serifed**. Robert Wood says that Robert C. McArtor of the Government Printing Office examined high-quality photographic copies and identified the lower-case body face as Monotype Modern, particularly from its *f* and *g*. Monotype's own history dates Monotype Modern to 1896, making the family far older than SOM1-01's claimed date.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.specialoperationsmanual.com/2014/12/30/som1-01-overview-authentication/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: specialoperationsmanual.com">[Special Operations Manual]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">specialoperationsmanual.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Special Operations Manual SOM1-01 Overview &amp; Authentication</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Special Operations ManualSOM1-01 Overview &amp; Authentication - SOM1-01December 30, 2014...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 30, 2014</span></span></span>

McArtor was not simply an unidentified expert invoked by proponents. Later Government Printing Office material identifies Robert C. McArtor as a former chairman of the GPO Style Board and adviser to a subsequent Style Board. That independently establishes relevant professional credentials, although the SOM1-01 conclusions attributed to him reach us principally through Wood's account rather than a published forensic report signed by McArtor.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/U.S._Government_Printing_Office_Style_Manual/About_This_Manual" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: en.wikisource.org">[Wikisource]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">en.wikisource.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">U.S. Government Printing Office Style Manual/About This Manual</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">U.S. Government Printing Office Style Manual/About This Manual</span></span></span>

The result is a mixed but useful finding. Nothing about **Monotype Modern body text** requires a post-1954 date, while the sans-serif headings have plausible pre-1954 competitors to Helvetica. The typography is consequently less obviously anachronistic than the strongest version of the forgery argument suggests.

## The printing process complicates the date

Typeface identification is only part of the typographic dispute. SOM1-01 proponents also argue that the appearance of the text preserves signs of **hot-metal composition**, the technology in which type was cast from molten metal rather than generated photographically or digitally.

The most famous example is the “raised z”. Wood says McArtor noticed several instances in which a lower-case *z* sits slightly above the normal type line. According to Wood's account of McArtor's explanation, an infrequently used character could fail to seat correctly in a Monotype system, producing this sort of displacement. Wood and his collaborators regard these anomalies, together with the spacing, as evidence that the source document was genuinely produced by period printing machinery.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.specialoperationsmanual.com/2014/12/30/som1-01-overview-authentication/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: specialoperationsmanual.com">[Special Operations Manual]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">specialoperationsmanual.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Special Operations Manual SOM1-01 Overview &amp; Authentication</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Special Operations ManualSOM1-01 Overview &amp; Authentication - SOM1-01December 30, 2014...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 30, 2014</span></span></span>

That claim has more evidential value than simply saying the page “looks old”, but it needs careful wording. A mechanical-looking defect can support an inference about **how an image was produced** without establishing **when it was produced**. Old printing equipment and old type do not cease to exist when a decade ends. A later creator could in principle use letterpress or Monotype equipment, photograph material made with it, reproduce genuine period printing, or deliberately imitate characteristic irregularities.

There is also an awkward wrinkle in the proponents' own chronology. One defence published on the SOM1-01 website states that the manual was “unquestionably printed in 1957 using a hot lead printing process”, despite the document's April 1954 publication date. The site's broader argument invokes SOM1-01's change-control page, which records revisions extending into 1957, as a possible explanation for later physical production or replacement pages.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.specialoperationsmanual.com/2014/12/28/why-the-critics-are-wrong-about-som1-01-mufon-journal-jan-2015/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: specialoperationsmanual.com">[Special Operations Manual]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">specialoperationsmanual.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Special Operations Manual Why The Critics Are Wrong About SOM1-01</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Special Operations Manual Why The Critics Are Wrong About SOM1-01</span></span></span>

That is not necessarily impossible for a working manual: government publications can be revised, pages replaced and later impressions produced. But it means that “period hot-metal printing” and “printed on 7 April 1954” are different propositions. Typography might place a document somewhere within a technological era without fixing it to the date printed on its title page.

## Why visual type matching cannot authenticate provenance

The central limitation is that SOM1-01 has no publicly available paper original on which normal forensic document examination can be performed. The circulating source derives from photographs on film received anonymously in 1994. Even the proponents' FAQ concedes the basic authentication problem raised by critics: without an original, investigators cannot directly examine such physical characteristics as the paper itself.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.specialoperationsmanual.com/the-manual/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: specialoperationsmanual.com">[Special Operations Manual]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">specialoperationsmanual.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Special Operations Manual The Manual</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Special Operations ManualThe Manual - SOM1-01January 1, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 1, 2026</span></span></span>

Typography can answer useful but narrower questions:

* **Could this typeface have existed in 1954?** Monotype Modern and Franklin Gothic certainly could; Helvetica could not.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.myfonts.com/collections/monotype-modern-font-monotype-imaging/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: myfonts.com">[myfonts.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">myfonts.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">My Fonts Monotype Modern MT Font &#124; Webfont &amp; Desktop &#124; My Fonts</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">My Fonts Monotype Modern MT Font &#124; Webfont &amp; Desktop &#124; My Fonts</span></span></span>
* **Does a heading resemble Helvetica?** Critics say yes, but proponents have identified older candidate faces from period specimens, preventing resemblance alone from establishing an anachronism.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ufohastings.com/storage/files/aldrich-analysis-som-101-field-manual.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ufohastings.com">[UFO Hastings]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ufohastings.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">UFO Hastings This is an excerpt from my article, “Operation</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UFO HastingsThis is an excerpt from my article, “OperationDecember 15, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 15, 2025</span></span></span>
* **Does the printing resemble hot-metal composition?** The reported raised letters and spacing are consistent with that interpretation, according to Wood's account of McArtor's examination.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.specialoperationsmanual.com/2014/12/30/som1-01-overview-authentication/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: specialoperationsmanual.com">[Special Operations Manual]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">specialoperationsmanual.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Special Operations Manual SOM1-01 Overview &amp; Authentication</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Special Operations ManualSOM1-01 Overview &amp; Authentication - SOM1-01December 30, 2014...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 30, 2014</span></span></span>
* **Does that prove an April 1954 government printing?** No. Neither a period-compatible font nor a period-compatible production technique identifies the printer, agency, location or actual date of manufacture.

The distinction is important in alleged MJ-12 documents because **consistency and provenance are different kinds of evidence**. Finding a typeface known to have existed in 1954 removes one possible anachronism. It does not establish that a photographed page was physically present in 1954. Conversely, identifying an unmistakably post-1954 face would be powerful negative evidence because it would set an earliest possible production date. That asymmetry is why the Helvetica dispute matters: a genuine Helvetica identification could damage SOM1-01 much more decisively than a Franklin Gothic identification could authenticate it.

## Does the typography fit 1954?

On the evidence available, **yes, SOM1-01's typography can plausibly be reconciled with mid-1950s printing — but it cannot securely be dated to 1954 from typography alone**.

The original Helvetica accusation contains a sound chronological premise but a contested identification. Helvetica's precursor Neue Haas Grotesk really did arrive only in 1957. Yet grotesque sans-serif typography long predates it, Franklin Gothic was an established American face decades earlier, and SOM1-01 proponents have produced period type-specimen comparisons offering Franklin Gothic and Square Gothic as alternatives for the disputed headings. The serif body text attributed to Monotype Modern presents no comparable chronological problem.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.myfonts.com/de/pages/fontscom-2011-06-06-helvetica-the-prequel" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: myfonts.com">[myfonts.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">myfonts.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">My Fonts Helvetica: the Prequel « Fonts.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Helvetica: the Prequel « Fonts.comJune 6, 2011...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 6, 2011</span></span></span>

That makes typography a weaker case for anachronism than a simple “Helvetica did not exist yet” argument implies. It does **not**, however, turn the same evidence into proof of authenticity. Photographic reproduction [limits]({{ 'limits/' | relative_url }}) fine-grained type identification; period typefaces could be used later; and alleged hot-metal characteristics establish, at most, compatibility with a production process rather than an official chain of [custody]({{ 'custody/' | relative_url }}).

For the SOM1-01 authenticity debate, the most defensible typographic verdict is therefore deliberately modest: **the surviving images do not establish a clear typographic impossibility for 1954, but neither can they demonstrate that the manual was printed then**. Without the physical document, typography can narrow the historical possibilities. It cannot supply the missing provenance.
