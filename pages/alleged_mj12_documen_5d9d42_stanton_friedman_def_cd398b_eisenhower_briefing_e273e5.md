## Introduction

The odd formatting of the alleged 1952 Eisenhower Briefing Document (EBD) is suspicious, but it is not as decisive a proof of forgery as some early MJ-12 criticism suggested. The document uses such features as the hybrid date form “18 November, 1952” and calls Rear Admiral Roscoe [Hillenkoetter]({{ 'hillenkoetter/' | relative_url }}) simply “Adm.” Critics including Philip J. Klass and Kevin Randle argued that these details did not fit the practices expected in a high-level military briefing.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://skepticalinquirer.org/wp-content/uploads/sites/29/2019/03/Issue-02-10.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: skepticalinquirer.org">[Skeptical Inquirer]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">skepticalinquirer.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The odd formatting of the alleged 1952 Eisenhower Briefing Document (EBD) is suspicious, but it is not as decisive a proof of forgery as...</span></span></span>

## The date format looked wrong — but how wrong?

One of the most memorable objections concerns the EBD's dates. Instead of the conventional American civilian form “November 18, 1952” or the familiar military form “18 November 1952”, the briefing uses the hybrid “18 November, 1952”. It also employs leading zeroes on some single-digit dates. Klass argued that this was not Hillenkoetter's practice and noted that genuine memoranda he examined used forms such as “6 Aug. 1948”, “14 June 1949” and “3 March 1950”.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://skepticalinquirer.org/wp-content/uploads/sites/29/2019/03/Issue-02-10.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: skepticalinquirer.org">[Skeptical Inquirer]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">skepticalinquirer.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The odd formatting of the alleged 1952 Eisenhower Briefing Document (EBD) is suspicious, but it is not as decisive a proof of forgery as...</span></span></span>

Randle and Don Schmitt similarly treated the date as evidence against authenticity. Their reasoning was not simply that the comma looked unconventional. Eisenhower had spent decades immersed in military paperwork, while Hillenkoetter was a senior naval officer and former Director of Central Intelligence. A briefing prepared at that level, they argued, should not contain an elementary departure from military dating practice.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cufos.org/PDFs/books/The_Roswell_ReportR2.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cufos.org">[Center for UFO Studies]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cufos.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Randle and Don Schmitt similarly treated the date as evidence against authenticity. Their reasoning was not simply that the comma looked...</span></span></span>

There is a real point here. A genuine CIA memorandum actually dated 18 November 1952, preserved in the CIA's declassified collection, uses “18 November 1952” without the disputed comma. Another CIA memorandum from the same date does likewise. These records show that the conventional day-month-year format certainly was in active official use at precisely the time the EBD claims to have been prepared.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cia.gov/readingroom/docs/CIA-RDP55-00037A000100030129-2.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cia.gov">[CIA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cia.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">There is a real point here. A genuine CIA memorandum actually dated 18 November 1952, preserved in the CIA&#x27;s declassified collection, use...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 1952</span></span></span>

But that establishes a convention, not an absolute rule.

Friedman argued that archival research at the Truman and Eisenhower presidential libraries produced classified or limited-distribution documents with numerous date styles. He cited three memoranda from CIA Director Allen Dulles to White House Staff Secretary Andrew Goodpaster, produced within roughly ten days in November 1956, which he said used “12 November 1956”, “November 20, 1956” and a rubber-stamped “NOV 22 1956”. He also reported finding a folder containing seven date formats and examples associated with Hillenkoetter and fellow former CIA director Walter Bedell Smith using the day-month-comma-year construction at issue in the EBD.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://medium.com/stanton-friedmans-articles/update-on-operation-majestic-12-documents-ed2df58fd8a1" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: medium.com">[Medium]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">medium.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">update on operation majestic 12 documents ed2df58fd8a1</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Friedman argued that archival research at the Truman and Eisenhower presidential libraries produced classified or limited-distribution do...</span></span></span>

The important distinction is between **unusual** and **impossible**. The CIA's surviving records demonstrate that “18 November 1952” was an ordinary form. Friedman's archival examples, if accepted as characterised, show that government offices did not invariably impose one format across every memorandum and administrative document. The disputed comma therefore remains a warning sign, particularly when combined with the EBD's other peculiarities, but it cannot safely bear the whole authentication argument.

## “Admiral” Hillenkoetter was another apparent mistake

The second formatting objection is more subtle because it concerns how ranks were abbreviated. The EBD identifies its supposed briefing officer as “ADM. ROSCOE H. HILLENKOETTER”. Hillenkoetter was a **rear admiral**, not a full admiral. Official historical sources identify him accordingly, and genuine correspondence could give his full rank as Rear Admiral, USN.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1945-50Intel/d293" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: history.state.gov">[state.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">history.state.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The second formatting objection is more subtle because it concerns how ranks were abbreviated. For critics, this seemed difficult to reconcile with a document supposedly prepared inside the military-intelligence establishment. Randle regarded the error as especially striking because Hillenkoetter himself was supposedly responsible for the briefing.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cufos.org/PDFs/books/The_Roswell_ReportR2.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cufos.org">[Center for UFO Studies]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cufos.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Randle and Don Schmitt similarly treated the date as evidence against authenticity. Friedman's response was to examine the EBD's naming convention as a whole rather than treating Hillenkoetter in isolation. The document also calls Nathan [Twining]({{ 'twining/' | relative_url }}), Hoyt Vandenberg and Robert Montague simply “Gen.” Yet when the alleged MJ-12 group was supposedly created in September 1947, those men did not all hold the four-star rank of full general. Twining, for example, was a lieutenant general when he issued his well-known September 1947 memorandum on “flying discs”.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.theblackvault.com/casefiles/update-on-operation-majestic-12-documents/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theblackvault.com">[The Black Vault]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theblackvault.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Stanton Friedman’s answer was essentially an argument from historical variation. ## Period variation weakens an anachronism argument

This is where Friedman's defence of the EBD is strongest. Authentication arguments based on office practice have different evidential strengths. If a disputed feature **could not have existed in 1952** — because the terminology, technology or security system did not yet exist — it would be powerful evidence of fabrication. If the feature was merely unusual, however, the question becomes empirical: did real contemporary offices ever do the same thing?

The surviving record cautions against imagining federal paperwork as completely uniform. Even highly classified government material came from different agencies, secretariats, typists and administrative systems. Contemporary CIA records demonstrate the ordinary military-style date format, while Friedman's reported archival comparisons indicate that White House-era documents could employ alternatives.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cia.gov/readingroom/docs/CIA-RDP55-00037A000100030129-2.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cia.gov">[CIA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cia.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">There is a real point here. The same principle applies to titles. Official historical material naturally identifies Hillenkoetter precisely as a rear admiral, but government prose could also use “Admiral Hillenkoetter” conversationally or generically. A State Department historical account of the early CIA, for example, refers to “Admiral Hillenkoetter” when discussing him as Director of Central Intelligence rather than reproducing his complete naval grade. That does not authenticate the EBD, but it illustrates why the bare word “Admiral” cannot automatically be read as a claim that he held the four-star grade.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://1997-2001.state.gov/about_state/history/intel/intro8.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: 1997-2001.state.gov">[State Department Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">1997-2001.state.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Department Archive</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The same principle applies to titles. Friedman's useful contribution, then, was methodological. A critic cannot establish an anachronism simply by producing examples of the *usual* format. To make the objection decisive, the critic needs good reason to believe the disputed form was excluded from the relevant office, period and type of document. Finding authentic exceptions changes the argument from “this could not have been produced then” to the much weaker “this is not how such documents were normally produced”.

That is a meaningful shift — but only a defensive one.

## Compatibility is not authentication

The danger is to reverse the burden of proof. Showing that a peculiar feature has a possible 1950s analogue does not show that the EBD came from Hillenkoetter, the CIA, the White House or any alleged MJ-12 organisation. A competent fabricator can reproduce genuine period conventions, and an inconsistent fabricator can accidentally reproduce real-world inconsistencies.

This distinction is especially important because the EBD did not emerge from an authenticated government file. The document became public through photographs of anonymously supplied film rather than through a traceable archival chain of [custody]({{ 'custody/' | relative_url }}). Unlike an original paper document, such material does not permit ordinary examination of its paper, ink and other physical characteristics. ## What the formatting dispute actually establishes

The most defensible conclusion sits between the strongest claims made by the two sides. [The Eisenhower Briefing Document]({{ 'the-eisenhower-briefing-document/' | relative_url }}) contains formatting that is unusual enough to deserve scrutiny. Critics were justified in asking why a purported high-level 1952 briefing used a hybrid date style and simplified military titles, particularly when authentic CIA documents from the same day demonstrate the conventional “18 November 1952” form.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cia.gov/readingroom/docs/CIA-RDP55-00037A000100030129-2.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cia.gov">[CIA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cia.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">There is a real point here. Period government paperwork was not perfectly uniform, and generic treatment of military ranks is not inherently anachronistic. The fact that every military member in the EBD's list receives a simplified “Gen.” or “Adm.” title also makes Hillenkoetter's designation less conspicuous than it appears when quoted by itself.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.theblackvault.com/casefiles/update-on-operation-majestic-12-documents/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theblackvault.com">[The Black Vault]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theblackvault.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Stanton Friedman’s answer was essentially an argument from historical variation. So **could the EBD's odd formatting still be compatible with an authentic 1952 document? Yes.** The date comma and generic ranks are not strong enough, by themselves, to demonstrate fabrication.

But “compatible with the period” is a much lower evidential threshold than “authentic”. Friedman's counterexamples principally remove two proposed knockout arguments. They do not establish who typed the EBD, where it was kept, how it left government custody, or why searches of the relevant official collections have failed to recover an independently verifiable copy or supporting MJ-12 record trail.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/research/military/air-force/ufos" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">This distinction is especially important because the EBD did not emerge from an authenticated government file. That distinction is the lasting value of the formatting debate. The peculiarities are better treated as **risk indicators to be weighed with provenance and corroboration**, not as either a self-contained proof of forgery or, once explained away, evidence that the extraordinary story printed on the pages must be true.
